    Refraction and Snell's law

    NCERT Class 10 Science · Light  ·  Class 12 Physics · Ray Optics Goes up to Olympiad practised
    Show depth up to
    Builds on Laws of reflection Speed of light Sine of an angle Vectors Derivatives and maxima
    Try this before reading
    A coin lies at the bottom of a bucket filled with water to a depth of 40 cm. Looking straight down, how deep does the coin seem to be?

    The idea Class 10

    Light travels at about 3 × 108 m/s in vacuum and more slowly inside matter. When a beam crosses into a new material at an angle, one edge of the beam slows down before the other, so the beam swings round. A column of marchers turns the same way when one end steps into mud first.

    The refractive index compares the two speeds: n = c/v. Water has n ≈ 1.33 and ordinary glass about 1.5. A larger n means slower light and a sharper bend towards the normal.

    n1sinθ1=n2sinθ2 Both angles are measured from the normal. The incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal lie in one plane.

    Move the ray yourself Class 10

    Worked example Class 10

    A ray in air strikes a glass slab (n = 1.5) at 60° to the normal. Find the angle of refraction.
    1. Write the law: 1.00 × sin 60° = 1.5 × sin θ2.
    2. sin θ2 = 0.866 / 1.5 = 0.577.
    3. θ2 = sin−1(0.577) ≈ 35.3°. The ray bends towards the normal, as expected for a slower medium.

    Why the law holds Class 12

    Treat light as a wavefront, a line of crests that moves perpendicular to itself. Let the front AB reach the surface at A while its other end B still has a distance BC to go.

    In the time t that B takes to reach C at speed v1, the wavelet from A spreads into medium 2 to a radius AD = v2t. The new wavefront is the line from C that just touches this wavelet. The triangles ABC and ADC share the side AC, so

    sin i = BC/AC = v1t/AC,    sin r = AD/AC = v2t/AC  ⇒  sin i / sin r = v1/v2 = n2/n1

    The law is a statement about speeds and nothing else. The frequency cannot change at the boundary, because crests arrive at the surface at one rate on both sides. So the wavelength shrinks in the slower medium: λ2 = λ1n1/n2.

    Consequences you will use constantly JEE Main

    • Reversibility. A ray sent backwards retraces its path, so the index of 2 relative to 1 is the reciprocal of 1 relative to 2: 1n2 = 1/2n1.
    • Deviation at one surface. The ray turns by δ = i − r.
    • Critical angle. Going from dense to rare, the refracted ray grazes the surface when sin θc = n2/n1. Beyond θc all the light reflects (total internal reflection). The largest turn refraction alone can give is therefore 90° − θc.
    • Apparent depth, near-normal viewing. An object at depth h in medium n, seen from air, appears at h/n. In general, apparent depth = real depth × nobserver/nobject.
    • Parallel slab. The ray leaves parallel to the way it came in, shifted sideways by d = t sin(i − r)/cos r, because the path inside is t/cos r long and makes angle i − r with the original line. Seen face-on, the slab lifts an image by t(1 − 1/n).

    The invariant n sin θ JEE Advanced

    Stack any number of parallel layers. At every boundary the product n sin θ is handed on unchanged, so

    n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2 = … = nk sin θkThe final direction depends only on the first and last media, never on the layers between.

    This turns multilayer problems into one line, and it tells you when total internal reflection happens deep inside a stack: wherever n1 sin θ1 exceeds the local n. In the limit of a continuously varying index it becomes n(y) sin θ(y) = constant, which drives the Olympiad section below.

    Apparent depth at an angle Irodov

    The rule h/n assumes you look almost straight down. Look obliquely and the image moves. Put the object at depth h in water (n) and follow a ray leaving at angle θ1. It meets the surface at x = h tan θ1 and continues in air at θ2, with n sin θ1 = sin θ2.

    Your eye collects a thin bundle of neighbouring rays, and the image is where those rays, traced backwards, cross. A backward-traced ray at depth y sits at x − y tan θ2; neighbouring rays meet where this does not change with θ1:

    dx/dθ1 = y sec²θ2 · dθ2/dθ1,   with dx/dθ1 = h sec²θ1 and n cos θ1 dθ1 = cos θ2 dθ2
    h′ = h cos³θ2 / (n cos³θ1)θ1 in water, θ2 in air. For θ → 0 this returns h/n.

    Since θ2 > θ1, the ratio is below 1/n: the more obliquely you look, the shallower the object seems. That is why the floor of a pool appears to rise towards the far end.

    Snell's law as a vector equation Irodov

    Let k̂1 and k̂2 be unit vectors along the incident and refracted rays, and N̂ the unit normal pointing into medium 2. Snell's law and the rule that both rays share a plane with the normal combine into one statement: the part of nk̂ along the surface is conserved.

    n1(k̂1 × N̂) = n2(k̂2 × N̂)  ⇔  n2k̂2 = n1k̂1 + (n2 cos θ2 − n1 cos θ1)N̂

    This form handles tilted surfaces in three dimensions without drawing anything, which makes it the natural tool for prism and wedge problems in Irodov and the formula ray-tracing software uses.

    Fermat's principle Olympiad

    One rule produces straight-line travel, reflection and refraction together: light follows a path along which the travel time is stationary. Equivalently, the optical path length ∫n ds is stationary.

    Put A at height a above the boundary and B at depth b below it, a horizontal distance d apart. If the ray crosses at x, then

    cT(x) = n1√(a² + x²) + n2√(b² + (d − x)²)

    Setting dT/dx = 0 gives n1x/√(a² + x²) = n2(d − x)/√(b² + (d − x)²), and each fraction is a sine: n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2. "Stationary" matters: for some mirrors the actual path takes the longest time, not the shortest.

    Curved rays in graded media Olympiad

    If n changes smoothly with height, the layer invariant becomes n(y) sin θ(y) = constant, with θ measured from the vertical. The ray bends steadily towards higher n, with curvature set by the part of the index gradient perpendicular to the ray:

    1/R = (1/n) ∂n/∂N   and, in general,   dds(ndrds)=∇n The second form, the ray equation, is Newton's second law for light rays.

    Worked example: a parabolic ray

    A medium has n² = n0²(1 + αy). A ray starts horizontally at y = 0. Find its path.
    1. Horizontal start means sin θ = 1 at n = n0, so n sin θ = n0 everywhere.
    2. The slope is dy/dx = cot θ = √(n² − n0²)/n0 = √(αy).
    3. Separate and integrate: 2√y = √α · x, so y = αx²/4. The ray is a parabola, bending towards larger n.

    A mirage on a hot road is the same physics: air near the surface is hotter, so its index is lower, and rays from the sky curve upwards into your eye from below.

    Two refinements Olympiad

    • Dispersion. n depends on wavelength. For glass in the visible range, Cauchy's formula n(λ) ≈ A + B/λ² works well, so violet bends more than red. This is how a prism splits white light.
    • How much light reflects. Refraction never takes all of the light. At normal incidence the reflected fraction is R = ((n1 − n2)/(n1 + n2))², about 4% at an air–glass surface. The Fresnel equations give the angle dependence.

    Toolkit and traps all levels

    • Angles are measured from the normal. Many problems give the angle with the surface instead.
    • Total internal reflection needs light going from dense to rare. Check the direction before using sin θc = n2/n1.
    • For small angles, sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ (in radians) turns most near-normal problems into simple ratios.
    • In layered problems, use n sin θ = constant and skip the layers in between.
    • h/n is a near-normal result. For oblique viewing, use the cos³ formula.
    • Any "quickest route" puzzle, such as a lifeguard running then swimming, is Snell's law with speeds in place of indices.
