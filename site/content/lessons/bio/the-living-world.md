## What counts as alive? {lvl=1 id=s-alive label="Class 9–10"}

Look around you: a potted plant, a crow on the wire, a rock, a cooking pot, the wind in the neem tree. You sort these in your head in half a second. Living or not? The chapter begins with exactly that wonder, and NCERT says that the question "what is life?" really hides two. One is technical: what makes something **living** rather than non-living? The other is philosophical: what is the purpose of life? As scientists we only tackle the first.

The 2026-27 reprint of the Class 11 chapter poses the question and moves on to diversity. In earlier editions and in most school courses, a living thing is recognised by a cluster of features rather than one: it is made of cells, it grows, it reproduces, it takes in and changes matter and energy (metabolism), and it senses and responds to its surroundings [Unverified: the list is from earlier NCERT editions, not the reprint text]. No single test settles it. A fire grows and uses fuel, and a crystal grows too, yet neither is alive. It is the whole cluster, together with the cell as the basic unit, that marks a living thing.

## How many kinds of life are there? {lvl=1 id=s-diversity label="Class 9–10"}

Walk through a dense forest and you meet far more kinds of living things than in your school garden. The wider you look, the more you find: potted plants, insects, birds and pets, and countless organisms too small to see with the naked eye. Each different kind is called a **species**. The number of species known and described is about **1.7 to 1.8 million**, and this variety is called **biodiversity**: the number and types of organisms present on Earth. New organisms keep being identified, even in old, well-explored areas.

India is a **biodiversity hotspot**: a region with many species found nowhere else and with serious habitat loss. Species restricted to one region of the world are called **endemic**. The Nilgiri tahr, the lion-tailed macaque, the pitcher plant *Nepenthes khasiana* and the Neelakurinji are found only in India. The Western Ghats, Indo-Burma (including North East India), the Himalayas and Sundaland (including the Nicobar Islands) are named as hotspots.

This variety is not a fixed list. NCERT Class 9 explains that small differences among individuals affected their chances of survival, adapted them to changed conditions, and over many generations gave rise to new forms. Today's diversity is the outcome of a vast span of change, and classification gives us a framework to study it.

## Why we need to classify {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Class 9–10"}

Imagine a library where thousands of books lie scattered on the floor and you want *On the Origin of Species*. Without subjects, authors or sections you would not know where to begin. The Earth is that library, with millions of books. So we group organisms by their similarities and differences, and this scientific system of grouping is called **biological classification**.

Which features do we use? Scientists begin with broad, easily visible ones and then look at finer ones: external features (shape, size, body organisation), mode of nutrition (autotroph or heterotroph), internal structure (skeleton, organs, tissues), cell structure (unicellular or multicellular, prokaryote or eukaryote, cell wall present or not), ecological role (producer, consumer, decomposer), reproduction, and genetic similarity from DNA. The same organism can fit into different groups depending on the criterion, which is why the choice of features matters.

Classification helps in six ways:

1. It makes the study of living organisms organised and systematic.
2. It shows similarities and differences among living beings.
3. It shows how organisms are related and how they interact.
4. It helps in identifying and naming newly discovered organisms.
5. It supports conservation by pointing out organisms under threat of extinction.
6. It lets scientists everywhere discuss organisms in one common system.

The Class 9 case study of Pakke Tiger Reserve makes this concrete. Scientists have recorded nearly 300 bird species there, and four different hornbills nest in large, old trees and feed on particular fruits. Telling the four apart, and knowing which tree each needs, lets a scientist ask precise questions and see at once what would be lost if the old trees were cut.

## Groups inside groups {lvl=1 id=s-address label="Class 9–10"}

Classification works in steps. It starts with very broad groups and moves to smaller, more specific ones. At each lower step the members share more features, and every lower group is part of the group above it. Class 9 compares this to a house address: country, state, city, street, house. The order is **kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species**.

A tiger's address is Animalia (kingdom), Chordata (phylum), Mammalia (class), Carnivora (order), Felidae (family), *Panthera* (genus), *P. tigris* (species). A pea plant's is Plantae, Magnoliophyta, Magnoliopsida, Fabales, Fabaceae, *Pisum*, *P. sativum*. The last two words of the address are the scientific name, which the next sections unpack.

## Why names must be standard {lvl=2 id=s-nomenclature label="Class 11–12"}

Millions of plants and animals exist, and we know those in our own area by local names, which vary from place to place, even within one country. A single organism can have a dozen names, and one name can mean different organisms elsewhere. So there is a need to **standardise naming**, so that an organism is known by the same name all over the world. This process is **nomenclature**.

Naming is only possible when the organism has been described correctly and we know exactly which organism the name belongs to. That step is **identification**: you cannot label something you cannot recognise.

To avoid chaos, biologists have agreed on international rules. For plants the principles are in the **International Code for Botanical Nomenclature (ICBN)**, and for animals in the **International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN)**. [Unverified: the plant code has since been renamed the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi and plants.] These codes ensure that each organism has only one name, that anyone describing it in any part of the world arrives at the same name, and that the name has not been used for any other known organism.

## Binomial nomenclature {lvl=2 id=s-rules label="Class 11–12"}

Every scientific name has two components: the **generic name** (the genus) and the **specific epithet**. This two-part system is **binomial nomenclature**, given by **Carolus Linnaeus** and practised by biologists all over the world. A two-word format was found convenient. Take the mango, *Mangifera indica*. *Mangifera* is the genus; *indica* is the species or specific epithet.

The universal rules, in NCERT's order:

1. Biological names are generally in **Latin** and written in italics. They are Latinised or derived from Latin whatever their origin.
2. The first word is the **genus** and the second is the **specific epithet**.
3. Both words, when handwritten, are **separately underlined**; when printed they are in **italics**, which shows their Latin origin.
4. The genus starts with a **capital letter**; the specific epithet starts with a **small letter**.

The **name of the author** appears after the specific epithet, at the end of the biological name, in abbreviated form: *Mangifera indica* Linn. means this species was first described by Linnaeus.

## Taxon and taxonomy {lvl=2 id=s-taxon label="Class 11–12"}

It is nearly impossible to study every living organism one by one, so we **classify**: we group things into convenient categories using easily observable characters. You already do this: "dogs", "cats", "insects", "mammals", "wheat", "rice", "plants", "animals" are all convenient categories. The scientific word for such a category is a **taxon** (plural **taxa**). Taxa can sit at very different levels. A dog is a mammal and mammals are animals, so "dogs", "mammals" and "animals" are taxa at three different levels.

Classifying organisms into taxa is **taxonomy**. The basis of modern taxonomy is external and internal structure, the structure of the cell, the process of development and ecological information. Four jobs are basic to it: **characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature**.

Taxonomy is not new. Early humans needed food, clothing and shelter, so the earliest classifications were based on the uses of organisms. People also wanted to know how organisms are related. The study of this is **systematics**, from the Latin *systema*, a systematic arrangement of organisms (Linnaeus titled his book *Systema Naturae*). Its scope was later widened to include identification, nomenclature and classification, and it takes **evolutionary relationships** between organisms into account.

| Term | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Taxon | A group of organisms at any rank (a unit of classification) |
| Taxonomy | Characterising, identifying, classifying and naming organisms |
| Systematics | Arrangement of organisms plus their evolutionary relationships |
| Nomenclature | Giving each identified organism a standard scientific name |

## Taxonomic categories {lvl=2 id=s-categories label="Class 11–12"}

Classification is not a single step. It is a **hierarchy of steps**, each representing a **rank** or category. Since each category is part of the overall arrangement, it is a **taxonomic category**, and together they form the **taxonomic hierarchy**. Insects, for example, share three pairs of jointed legs, so they are a recognisable group that can be given a rank. NCERT stresses that these groups are distinct biological entities and not merely morphological aggregates.

The common categories are **kingdom, phylum (division, for plants), class, order, family, genus and species**. Species is the lowest category in both plant and animal kingdoms. To place an organism you need to know the characters of the individual or the group, and compare similarities and dissimilarities with others of the same and different kinds. Taxonomists also use sub-categories (such as sub-phylum) for a finer fit.

**Memory aid.** Kingdom-to-species in a sentence: *Kings Play Chess On Fine Grained Sand*. Learn the NCERT order, then use whatever sentence sticks.

### Species and genus

A **species** is a group of individuals with fundamental similarities, distinguishable from closely related species by clear morphological differences. In *Mangifera indica* (mango), *Solanum tuberosum* (potato) and *Panthera leo* (lion), the words *indica*, *tuberosum* and *leo* are specific epithets, and *Mangifera*, *Solanum* and *Panthera* are genera. Humans are the species *sapiens* in the genus *Homo*: *Homo sapiens*.

A **genus** is a group of related species with more characters in common than species of other genera: genera are aggregates of closely related species. Potato and brinjal are two species of *Solanum* (others are *S. nigrum* and *S. melongena*). Lion (*Panthera leo*), leopard (*P. pardus*) and tiger (*P. tigris*) are all species of *Panthera*, which differs from *Felis*, the genus of cats.

### Family and order

A **family** is a group of related genera, with still fewer similarities than a genus. In plants, families are characterised by both vegetative and reproductive features: *Solanum*, *Petunia* and *Datura* are placed in the family **Solanaceae**. In animals, *Panthera* (lion, tiger, leopard) and *Felis* (cats) go together in the family **Felidae**. Cats and dogs share some features but differ in others, so they are in separate families, Felidae and Canidae.

An **order** is an assemblage of families that exhibit a few similar characters. Higher categories are identified from aggregates of characters, and the similarities are fewer than among the genera of one family. Plant families Convolvulaceae and Solanaceae are placed in one order mainly on floral characters; NCERT writes it "Polymoniales" (the standard spelling is Polemoniales [Unverified]). The animal order **Carnivora** includes the families Felidae and Canidae.

### Class, phylum and kingdom

A **class** includes related orders. The order Primata (monkey, gorilla, gibbon) and the order Carnivora (tiger, cat, dog) both belong to class **Mammalia**, which has other orders too.

Classes of fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals make up the next category, the **phylum**, here **Chordata**, based on common features such as a notochord and a dorsal hollow neural system. In plants, classes with a few similar characters are placed in a higher category called a **division**.

All animals, from their various phyla, are placed in the highest category, **Kingdom Animalia**; **Kingdom Plantae** comprises all plants from the various divisions.

## Climbing the ladder {lvl=2 id=s-ladder label="Class 11–12"}

Look at the ladder from species up to kingdom. What changes as you climb? **The number of common characteristics goes down.** The lower the taxon, the more characters its members share. The higher the category, the harder it is to see how it relates to other taxa at the same level, so classification becomes more complex.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Man | *Homo sapiens* | *Homo* | Hominidae | Primata | Mammalia | Chordata |

Read the table down the columns. Man and housefly are both animals but differ already at phylum (Chordata against Arthropoda). Mango and wheat are both flowering plants (same division) but differ already at class: dicot against monocot. The moment two rows differ, you have found the lowest level at which they were still together.

:::worked title="Placing the housefly"
Housefly is *Musca domestica*. Its genus is *Musca*, family Muscidae, order Diptera, class Insecta, phylum Arthropoda, and kingdom Animalia. The fly shares kingdom with man, but not the phylum: man has a notochord (Chordata), the fly has jointed legs (Arthropoda).
:::

## What is a species, really? {lvl=3 id=s-species label="NEET · beyond"}

"Species" looks simple until you ask what makes two populations different species. NCERT's Exercise 8 asks you to collect the accepted meanings of the word, and to compare higher plants and animals with bacteria. The meanings you will meet:

- **Morphological species**: a group of individuals with fundamental similarities, distinguishable by clear structural differences from close relatives. This is the working definition in NCERT's §1.2.1 and the one every field guide uses.
- **Biological species**: a group of individuals that can interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring. NCERT credits Ernst Mayr with the currently accepted definition of a biological species; Class 9 describes a species as a group of similar individuals "capable of interbreeding and producing offsprings".
- **Why bacteria are awkward**: most bacteria reproduce without mating, so "can they interbreed?" does not apply and they are grouped by structure, biochemistry and genetic similarity instead [Unverified detail].

Neither definition is perfect, which is why the question is still discussed among biologists.

## Why the system keeps changing {lvl=3 id=s-changing label="NEET · beyond"}

Classification is never finished. Aristotle (4th century BCE) grouped animals by habitat: land, water and air. In 1758 Linnaeus used two kingdoms, Plantae and Animalia. But where do the amoeba, paramecium and bacteria go? They move like animals yet are single-celled and often heterotrophic. So Protista was added as a third kingdom (Haeckel, 1866). Microscopes then showed that an amoeba has a true, membrane-bound nucleus and bacteria do not, so Monera was split off (Copeland, 1938, four kingdoms). Fungi, which absorb food from dead matter, got their own kingdom in Whittaker's five-kingdom system (1969).

Then DNA came in. Organisms with similar DNA are considered to share ancestry, and in 1977 Carl Woese proposed a **three-domain** system: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya, which showed that microscopic life is far more diverse than believed. That is the answer to NCERT's question "why do classification systems keep changing?": **new tools (microscopes, stains, DNA) give new evidence, and science revises its maps when the evidence changes.** The five kingdoms are the subject of the next lesson.

## Tools of the taxonomist {lvl=3 id=s-aids label="NEET · beyond"}

[Unverified: this section is from earlier NCERT editions and is not in the 2026-27 reprint of Chapter 1.] Taxonomists need places to keep and compare specimens, and written guides for identification:

| Aid | What it holds or does |
|---|---|
| Herbarium | Dried, pressed and labelled plant specimens on sheets, kept for reference |
| Museum | Preserved animal and plant specimens, including insects in boxes and animals in jars |
| Botanical garden | Living plants grown and labelled by scientific name |
| Zoological park | Living wild animals kept in conditions close to their natural habitat |
| Key | A set of paired contrasting statements (couplets) used step by step to identify an organism |

A key works like twenty questions: at each step you choose one of two descriptions, and each choice narrows the possibilities until one species remains.

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. Living things are recognised by a cluster of features, not one; **biodiversity** is the number and types of organisms on Earth, with about 1.7 to 1.8 million species known. {lvl=1}
2. We classify to organise study, show relationships, name new organisms, support conservation and let scientists share a common system. {lvl=1}
3. **Nomenclature** gives each organism one standard name; **identification** must come first. The codes are ICBN (plants) and ICZN (animals). {lvl=2}
4. A scientific name is binomial (genus plus specific epithet), Latin, in italics or underlined; genus capitalised, epithet small, author's name last and abbreviated. {lvl=2}
5. A **taxon** is a group at any rank; **taxonomy** classifies organisms, and **systematics** adds evolutionary relationships. {lvl=2}
6. The ranks are species, genus, family, order, class, phylum (division), kingdom; species is the lowest in both plants and animals. {lvl=2}
7. Going up the hierarchy, members share fewer characters; going down, more. {lvl=2}
8. Species can be defined by structure or by interbreeding, and neither definition suits every organism. {lvl=3}
9. Classification keeps changing because new tools bring new evidence (two kingdoms to five kingdoms to three domains). {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Writing *Mangifera Indica*: only the genus is capitalised. {lvl=1}
- Treating a common name (tiger, mango, neem) as a scientific name: only the two-word Latin name is the standard. {lvl=1}
- Going up the ladder, species are NOT more alike: the number of shared characters goes down. {lvl=2}
- Mixing phylum and division: animals have phyla, plants have divisions. {lvl=2}
- Treating "taxon" as a rank: it is a group at any rank, while "category" refers to the rank. {lvl=2}
- Learning only the spelling in the textbook: NCERT's "Primata" and "Polymoniales" are its own spellings, and standard spellings differ [Unverified]. {lvl=3}
:::
