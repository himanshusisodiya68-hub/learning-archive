## What a proverb is {lvl=1 id=s-what label="Foundation"}

A **proverb** is a short saying, known to many people, that gives advice or states something people have found to be true again and again. *Honesty is the best policy. Practice makes perfect. Time and tide wait for no man.* Three features make it a proverb:

- It is a **complete sentence** with its own verb and meaning.
- Its **wording is fixed**. You cannot say "Honesty is the finest rule".
- Its meaning is usually **figurative**: the words name one thing and the lesson is wider.

Proverbs are easy to confuse with two neighbours, so keep them apart.

| | Proverb | Idiom | Quotation |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | A full sentence of advice or truth | A phrase with a hidden meaning | Words of a known person |
| Example | Where there is a will, there is a way. | to pull someone's leg | "Be the change you wish to see in the world." (often credited to Gandhi, [Unverified]) |
| Fits into a sentence? | Stands on its own | Yes, with tense and person changes | Stands on its own, with a name |
| Can it change form? | No | Yes: *He pulled my leg.* | No |
| Who owns it? | Everyone, no author | Everyone, no author | One named speaker |

:::qc
q: Which of these is a proverb, and not an idiom?
options: ["once in a blue moon", "Where there is smoke, there is fire.", "to bury the hatchet", "to spill the beans"]
answer: 1
right: Right. It is a full sentence giving a general truth, with fixed wording. The other three are phrases that fit inside sentences.
wrong: A proverb is a whole sentence of advice or truth. Three of these are phrases you can put in the past tense or change the person of; only one stands as a sentence by itself.
:::

## Why proverbs stick {lvl=1 id=s-stick label="Foundation"}

Before printing, advice had to be carried in memory. A saying that people could not remember did not survive. So proverbs grew features that make them easy to recall, and you can spot these features in almost every one.

| Device | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Rhyme | Gives a tune your memory holds on to | *Haste makes waste.* (haste / waste) |
| Balance | Two halves of the same length | *Easy come, easy go.* |
| Contrast | Two opposites sharpen each other | *Man proposes, God disposes.* |
| Alliteration | Repeated first sounds | *Look before you leap.* *Slow and steady wins the race.* |
| A picture | A concrete image carries an abstract idea | *Empty vessels make the most noise.* |

This is why you should learn a proverb as a **whole**. Take one word out and the music and the meaning both break.

## Matching a proverb to a situation {lvl=2 id=s-match label="Class 10"}

In an exam, a question often describes a small situation and gives four proverbs. The mistake is to match words: if the story has a bird, the "bird" proverb looks attractive. The method that works is three steps.

1. **Strip the story.** Remove names, objects and places. What happened, in the plainest words?
2. **State the rule.** Turn it into advice: "Do it early", "Don't boast", "Work together."
3. **Choose the proverb that states that rule**, and check it does not state a different one.

:::worked title="Four situations, four rules"
(a) *Seema practised her singing for two hours daily for a year and won the district prize.* Rule: steady practice pays. Proverb: practice makes perfect (or slow and steady wins the race).

(b) *Arjun bragged that he would beat everyone at the quiz, but he forgot to attend the first round.* Rule: boasting ends badly. Proverb: pride goes before a fall.

(c) *The shop did not stock the book, so Neha waited. When it arrived she was the first buyer.* Rule: patience pays. Proverb: all good things come to those who wait.

(d) *A man spent his whole month's pay on a fancy phone and had nothing left for the bus.* Rule: spend within your means. Proverb: cut your coat according to your cloth.
:::

Watch for **near neighbours** that pull in different directions. *Make hay while the sun shines* (use the chance) and *Rome was not built in a day* (be patient) both involve time, but one says hurry and the other says wait.

:::qc
q: "The club had a successful open day, but its president insisted on taking all the credit and forgot to thank the volunteers who set it up." Which rule is inside the story?
options: ["Credit should be shared with those who helped.", "Start early.", "Spend within your means.", "Think before you act."]
answer: 0
right: Right. One person kept credit that belonged to a group, so the rule is about sharing credit. Always name the rule before looking for the proverb.
wrong: Strip the story: one person took the credit that belonged to a group. The rule is about sharing credit, not about time, money or thought.
:::

## Using proverbs in your own writing {lvl=2 id=s-write label="Class 10"}

In the board's writing section (an article, a speech, a letter to the editor) a well-placed proverb gives your writing a polished feel. Overuse spoils it. Follow these points.

| Where | How | Sample |
|---|---|---|
| Opening of an article | Quote the proverb, then say why it matters now | *"A stitch in time saves nine," as the old saying goes. Nowhere is it truer than on our roads.* |
| Opening of a speech | Greet the audience, then use the proverb as a hook | *Respected principal and friends, they say that honesty is the best policy. Today I will ask whether our school lives up to it.* |
| Body paragraph | Use it as a link or an example, not as a decoration | *Our experience supports the saying that many hands make light work.* |
| Closing | Return to the opening proverb to round off | *So let us remember that slow and steady wins the race.* |

Do not use more than one or two in a 120-word answer. Always **explain** the proverb in your own words; the examiner is looking for your thinking, not a list of sayings.

:::worked title="A proverb-led opening (about 70 words)"
Topic: *Why we should save water.*

"Water, water everywhere, nor any drop to drink" (Coleridge) once described a becalmed sailor, but it may soon describe our towns. Groundwater in many Indian cities sinks a little further every year. We turn taps on without thinking, and a single dripping tap can waste many litres a month. If we keep waiting for the problem to become big, we will learn the hard way that a stitch in time saves nine.

Why it works: the opening line hooks the reader, a concrete fact follows, and the closing proverb states the lesson in a single, memorable line.
:::

### Reporting a proverb

A proverb is a statement of a general truth, so when you report it, **the verb does not change**, even after *said*.

- *The teacher said, "Haste makes waste."* becomes *The teacher said that haste makes waste.*
- *Grandfather said to us, "Honesty is the best policy."* becomes *Grandfather told us that honesty is the best policy.*

## Proverbs in reading and vocabulary questions {lvl=2 id=s-read label="Class 10"}

Proverbs appear in the board paper in three indirect ways [Unverified: exact pattern varies from year to year].

1. **Comprehension.** A passage may close with a proverb, and a question asks what message it conveys. Read the last two sentences and ask what rule the writer wants to leave with you.
2. **Vocabulary in context.** A question asks which phrase or saying has a meaning close to a given line. Do the strip-the-story method on the line.
3. **Writing.** A topic is given as a proverb ("Honesty is the best policy", "United we stand, divided we fall") and you must write an article, speech or short paragraph on it.

Common wrong options in multiple-choice questions follow a pattern. The **word trap** repeats a word from the passage but states a different lesson. The **opposite trap** gives a proverb that argues the opposite way (for example, *look before you leap* beside *he who hesitates is lost*). The **too-general trap** gives a proverb that is true but not about the particular rule in the passage.

:::qc
q: A passage ends: "The old farmer had never taken a loan, never bought on credit, and never worried when prices fell." Which proverb sums up his habit?
options: ["Cut your coat according to your cloth.", "Make hay while the sun shines.", "Too many cooks spoil the broth.", "Birds of a feather flock together."]
answer: 0
right: Right. He lived within his means, which is the rule of cutting your coat according to your cloth.
wrong: He never borrowed and so never worried: the rule is living within your means. Choose the proverb that says that.
:::

## Getting the wording exactly right {lvl=2 id=s-exact label="Class 10"}

Because a proverb is a fixed form, changing a word is an error. These are the common slips.

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A rolling stone **gather** no moss. | A rolling stone **gathers** no moss. | The subject is a stone (singular). |
| **Too much** cooks spoil the broth. | **Too many** cooks spoil the broth. | Cooks can be counted. |
| All that **glitter** is not gold. | All that **glitters** is not gold. | The verb agrees with the singular all that. (Shakespeare's line has the older word glisters, [Unverified: exact wording from memory].) |
| Look before you **jump**. | Look before you **leap**. | The word is fixed. |
| Empty vessels make **more** noise. | Empty vessels make **the most** noise (or **much** noise). | Both are used; learn one fixed version. |
| Haste make waste. | Haste **makes** waste. | The subject haste is singular. |
| Birds of feather flock together. | Birds of **a** feather flock together. | The article is part of the fixed form. |
| A friend in need is a friend **in deed**. | A friend in need is a friend **indeed**. | Indeed is one word meaning truly. |

:::note title="Fixed form, flexible use" tone=tip
You cannot change the wording of a proverb, but you may change the sentence around it. You may also use a proverb as a noun phrase: *He is one of those people who believe in "slow and steady wins the race."* Put the proverb inside quotation marks to show it is a quotation.
:::

## When proverbs disagree {lvl=3 id=s-disagree label="Beyond the board"}

Collect proverbs long enough and you find pairs that cannot both be right.

| One says | The other says | How to settle it |
|---|---|---|
| Look before you leap. | He who hesitates is lost. | Use the first for big, irreversible decisions; the second when the chance is about to close. |
| Many hands make light work. | Too many cooks spoil the broth. | Many hands suit tasks that divide into parts (cleaning a field); many cooks fail when one decision is needed (cooking one dish). |
| Out of sight, out of mind. | Absence makes the heart grow fonder. | The first is about forgetting; the second is about missing, usually in close bonds. |
| Better safe than sorry. | Nothing ventured, nothing gained. | The first for risks with heavy costs; the second when the cost of trying is small and the prize is large. |
| Slow and steady wins the race. | Strike while the iron is hot. | Slow when the work is long; quick when the moment is short. |

The lesson for a thinker is that proverbs are **rules of thumb**, not laws of nature. Each is true within a range. Pick by asking: What is at stake? Can this be undone? Is the chance about to close? Can the work be divided?

:::qc
q: Which pair of proverbs most directly disagrees?

## Proverbs across Indian languages {lvl=3 id=s-india label="Beyond the board"}

Proverbs travel. A lesson that one language states with a bird is stated in another with a pot or a field, and it is a pleasure to see the same rule in a different costume. A good translation of a proverb does not translate the words but **finds the equivalent**.

In an exam, a question asking for the "English equivalent" of a Hindi saying wants the English proverb that carries the same lesson, not a word-for-word translation. "Jiski lathi uski bhains" is answered with "Might is right", not "He who has the stick owns the buffalo".

## Where proverbs come from, and when they age {lvl=3 id=s-origins label="Beyond the board"}

Proverbs are shaped by the places where people live. English has many farm and craft sayings (*make hay while the sun shines*, *a stitch in time*, *too many cooks*). Some come from stories (*Slow and steady wins the race* is the lesson of Aesop's fable of the tortoise and the hare, though Aesop's authorship is [Unverified]). Some come from scripture or plays: the Bible gave English *the blind leading the blind*, and a line in Shakespeare's *The Merchant of Venice* lies behind "all that glitters is not gold" [Unverified: exact wording and attribution]. Printed collections, in the 1500s and 1700s, fixed the wording [Unverified: dates]. Hindi sayings come from village life and from poets such as Kabir, Rahim and Vrind, whose couplets (dohas) are quoted as proverbs.

Not every proverb stays wise. Some reflect the prejudices of their age: *Spare the rod and spoil the child* argues for beating children, a view that modern schools and the law reject. *Boys will be boys* is often used to excuse bad behaviour. A thoughtful writer can **quote a proverb, then question it**.

:::worked title="Respectfully questioning a proverb (about 80 words)"
"Spare the rod and spoil the child," the old saying warns. But research on children's learning suggests that fear teaches children to hide mistakes, not to avoid them. Patient guidance, clear rules and the example of adults teach more lastingly. The saying is right that children need limits; it is wrong about how those limits should be taught. Discipline need not mean punishment.

Why it works: it states the proverb, grants what is true in it, and then makes a clear, reasoned objection. This earns credit for analysis in a long writing task.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A proverb is a short, fixed saying, a full sentence, giving advice or a general truth. It differs from an idiom (a phrase with a hidden meaning) and a quotation (one named speaker). {lvl=1}
2. Proverbs stick through rhyme, balance, contrast, alliteration and pictures, so learn each as a whole. {lvl=1}
3. Learn proverbs by theme: effort and time, people and company, caution and character. {lvl=1}
4. To match a proverb to a situation, strip the story, state the rule, then pick the proverb that states that rule. {lvl=2}
5. Do not match on shared words; watch for the word trap, the opposite trap and the too-general trap. {lvl=2}
6. Use one proverb at a strong spot in your writing (opening or closing) and explain it in your own words. {lvl=2}
7. Keep the wording exact: a rolling stone gathers no moss, indeed (one word), birds of a feather. {lvl=2}
8. Reporting a proverb: the tense does not shift because it is a general truth. {lvl=2}
9. Proverbs can disagree (look before you leap vs he who hesitates is lost); they are rules of thumb true in a range of situations. {lvl=3}
10. An "equivalent" in another language carries the same lesson in a different image: jiski lathi uski bhains = might is right. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Matching on a shared word (a bird in the story, a bird in the proverb) instead of on the lesson. {lvl=1}
- Treating a proverb as a phrase that can be bent to fit the sentence: the wording is fixed. {lvl=1}
- Confusing an idiom with a proverb: a proverb is a full sentence of advice or truth. {lvl=1}
- Misquoting: "a rolling stone gather no moss", "all that glitter is not gold", "friend in deed". {lvl=2}
- Piling three or four proverbs into a short answer; one well-explained proverb scores better. {lvl=2}
- Quoting a proverb without explaining it in your own words. {lvl=2}
- Backshifting the tense of a proverb in reported speech: "He said that haste made waste" should be "haste makes waste". {lvl=2}
- Translating a Hindi saying word for word when the question asks for the English equivalent. {lvl=3}
- Treating every proverb as true in all cases; ask what situation it fits, and note when two proverbs disagree. {lvl=3}
- Repeating an out-of-date proverb without question where the lesson is now rejected. {lvl=3}
:::
