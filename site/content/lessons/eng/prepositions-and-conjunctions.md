## Why English needs linking words {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Nouns and verbs carry the meaning of a sentence, but they cannot say how things are **related**. Take three words: *book*, *table*, *Asha*. Nothing tells you who is doing what, where, or when. Add small words and the picture appears:

- Asha put the book **on** the table.
- Asha put the book **under** the table.
- Asha put the book **in** the table drawer **after** school.

Two kinds of small word do this work.

1. **Prepositions** link a *noun or pronoun* to the rest of the sentence and show a relation: place, time, direction, means, cause.
2. **Conjunctions** link *words, phrases or whole clauses* and show how ideas relate: addition, contrast, reason, condition.

A sentence without them breaks into a heap of facts. That is why these words are among the most frequent in English and why a wrong one stands out.

## Prepositions: what they do and what follows them {lvl=1 id=s-prep label="Foundation"}

A preposition is followed by a **noun, a pronoun or a gerund** (a verb in -ing used as a noun). Together they form a *prepositional phrase*.

- noun: *in the garden*
- pronoun: *with her*
- gerund: *by working hard*

Two consequences follow.

**A pronoun after a preposition takes the object form.** Say *with him*, *for us*, *between you and me*, not *with he*, *for we*, *between you and I*. The pronoun is the object of the preposition, so it takes the object case (me, him, her, us, them).

**A verb after a preposition takes -ing.** Say *She is good at singing*, not *good at sing*. The exception is **to** in *to + base verb* (*I want to go*), which is not a preposition there. A test: if you can put a noun in place of the -ing word, it is a preposition (*I look forward to the holidays*, so *I look forward to meeting you*).

:::note title="Position" tone=tip
Many learners are told never to end a sentence with a preposition. In ordinary English it is fine: *Who are you talking to?* is natural. In very formal writing you may prefer *To whom are you talking?*. Board answers accept the natural form.
:::

## Time, place and direction {lvl=1 id=s-tpd label="Foundation"}

### Time: at, on, in

The three words work from small to large.

| Word | Used with | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **at** | a clock time or a point | at 6 o'clock, at noon, at night, at the moment |
| **on** | a day or a date | on Monday, on 5 May, on Diwali, on my birthday |
| **in** | a month, year, season or long period | in June, in 2020, in winter, in the morning |

Notice *in the morning*, *in the evening* but *at night* and *on Monday morning*. These are fixed habits of English and have to be learned.

### Place: at, on, in

Think of **at** as a point, **on** as a surface, **in** as an enclosed space.

- *She is at the bus stop.* (a point)
- *The picture hangs on the wall.* (a surface)
- *The keys are in the drawer.* (inside)

Larger places usually take *in* (*in Chennai, in India*); a place seen as a stop on a journey takes *at* (*The train stops at Nagpur.*).

### Direction and movement

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| to | towards a destination | They walked to the market. |
| into | from outside to inside | The boys ran into the classroom. |
| out of | from inside to outside | She came out of the house. |
| towards | in the direction of, not necessarily arriving | The cow walked towards the gate. |
| through | passing within | The train goes through a tunnel. |
| across | from one side to the other | He swam across the river. |
| along | following the length | We walked along the canal. |

*In* shows where something is; *into* shows movement from outside to inside. *The fish swims in the pond* but *The boy jumped into the pond.*

:::qc
q: Choose the correct word: "The exam begins ___ 10 a.m. ___ Friday."
options: ["at ... on", "on ... at", "in ... on", "at ... in"]
answer: 0
right: Right. At goes with a clock time and on goes with a day.
wrong: Remember the order from small to large: at for a clock time, on for a day, in for a month or year.
:::

## Conjunctions: two jobs {lvl=1 id=s-conj label="Foundation"}

A conjunction joins, and the way it joins depends on whether the two parts are **equal** or **unequal**.

### Co-ordinating conjunctions: joining equals

These join words or clauses of equal rank, each of which could stand alone. A memory aid is **FANBOYS**: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.

| Job | Word | Example |
|---|---|---|
| addition | and | Meera sings and Kiran plays the guitar. |
| contrast | but, yet | He is small, but he is strong. |
| choice | or | Will you have tea or coffee? |
| result | so | It was late, so we went home. |
| reason | for | We hurried, for the gates were about to close. |
| negative addition | nor | He did not call, nor did he write. |

*For* as a conjunction is formal and rare. In speech use *because*.

### Subordinating conjunctions: making one clause depend on another

These join a **main clause** to a **subordinate clause** that cannot stand alone.

| Job | Words | Example |
|---|---|---|
| time | when, while, after, before, until, as soon as | Wait until the bell rings. |
| reason | because, since, as | We stayed in because it was raining. |
| contrast | although, though, whereas | Although he was tired, he kept working. |
| condition | if, unless, provided that | Unless you hurry, you will miss the train. |
| purpose | so that, in order that | She studied hard so that she could pass. |
| result | so ... that, such ... that | It was so cold that the lake froze. |

The subordinate clause can come first (then a comma) or second (usually no comma): *Because it rained, we stayed in.* / *We stayed in because it rained.*

## Appropriate prepositions {lvl=2 id=s-appr label="Class 10"}

Some words need one particular preposition after them. The meaning does not tell you which; you learn them as pairs. These are called **appropriate prepositions** or dependent prepositions, and the board loves them.

| Adjectives | Examples |
|---|---|
| afraid of, fond of, proud of, jealous of, capable of, aware of, ashamed of | She is proud of her team. |
| good at, bad at, quick at | He is good at chess. |
| interested in, weak in (a subject), rich in | Iron is rich in minerals. [Unverified: "weak in" and "weak at" both appear in textbooks; "in" is the common choice for a subject] |
| keen on, dependent on, based on | The film is based on a true story. |
| angry with (a person), angry at or about (a thing) | Papa was angry with me. |
| responsible for, famous for, sorry for | The town is famous for its silk. |
| different from, similar to, superior to, inferior to, senior to, junior to | Her view is different from mine. |
| married to, kind to, polite to | He is married to a teacher. |

| Verbs | Examples |
|---|---|
| look at / look for / look after | Look after the baby. Look for your keys. |
| listen to, wait for, depend on, prevent ... from | The rain prevented us from leaving. |
| congratulate ... on, succeed in, accuse ... of | They congratulated her on her win. |
| prefer ... to (not *than*) | I prefer tea to coffee. |
| divide between (two) / among (many) | She shared the sweets among her friends. |

:::note title="Comparatives that take to" tone=warn
Words that come from Latin comparatives, such as *superior, inferior, senior, junior, prefer*, take **to** and never *than*. *He is senior to me.* is right; *He is senior than me.* is wrong.
:::

:::qc
q: "The teacher congratulated Divya ___ her prize."
options: ["for", "on", "at", "with"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Congratulate someone on an achievement.
wrong: This is a fixed pairing: congratulate on. For is tempting but is not the standard choice.
:::

## Pairs that are easily confused {lvl=2 id=s-conf label="Class 10"}

| Pair | The difference | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **between / among** | between = two; among = three or more taken as a group | The prize was shared between the two girls. The prize was shared among the class. |
| **beside / besides** | beside = next to; besides = in addition to | She sat beside me. Besides Hindi, she speaks Tamil. |
| **in / into** | in = position; into = movement from outside | He lives in the city. He moved into a flat. |
| **on / upon** | upon is a more formal on | The cat sat on the mat. |
| **by / with** | by = the doer (agent); with = the tool (instrument) | The letter was written by Ravi with a green pen. |
| **since / for** | since = point in time; for = length of time | I have waited since noon. I have waited for an hour. |
| **during / for / while** | during + noun; for + length; while + clause | During the summer we stayed home. We stayed for a month. We read while it rained. |
| **till / by** | till = continues up to a time; by = done not later than | I will stay till five. Submit it by Friday. |
| **at / in (a city or place)** | at for a stop or building as a point; in for a city or country | He arrived at the airport. He lives in Kanpur. |

:::worked title="By or with?"
*The window was broken ___ the boys ___ a cricket ball.* Ask: who did it, and what did they use? The boys are the doers, so **by**. The ball is the tool, so **with**. Answer: *by the boys with a cricket ball.*
:::

## Correlative conjunctions and linked clauses {lvl=2 id=s-corr label="Class 10"}

**Correlatives** are conjunctions that come in pairs.

| Pair | Example |
|---|---|
| both ... and | Both Tara and Leela won prizes. |
| either ... or | You can either stay or go. |
| neither ... nor | Neither the manager nor the clerks were present. |
| not only ... but also | She is not only kind but also brave. |
| whether ... or | I do not know whether he will come or stay away. |
| no sooner ... than | No sooner had she left than it began to rain. |
| hardly / scarcely ... when | Hardly had we sat down when the lights went out. |

Three points the board checks:

1. **Parallel structure.** The two halves must be the same kind of word or phrase. *She is not only a good singer but also dances well* is clumsy. *She not only sings well but also dances well* or *She is not only a good singer but also a good dancer* is balanced.
2. **Agreement with the nearer subject.** With *either...or* and *neither...nor* the verb agrees with the subject closer to it: *Neither the teacher nor the students were present.* *Neither the students nor the teacher was present.*
3. **Inversion after no sooner and hardly.** When these open the sentence, the helper comes before the subject, and the first part is in the past perfect: *No sooner had she left* (not *No sooner she left*). The partner word is **than** for *no sooner* and **when** for *hardly* and *scarcely*. Not the other way round.

### Doubling mistakes

English does not allow two conjunctions to do the same job.

- Wrong: *Although he was tired, but he kept working.* Right: *Although he was tired, he kept working.* Or: *He was tired, but he kept working.*
- Wrong: *Because it rained, so we stayed in.* Right: *Because it rained, we stayed in.* Or: *It rained, so we stayed in.*

### Unless and if not

*Unless* already means *if not*, so it is not followed by another negative. *Unless you hurry, you will miss it* (not *unless you do not hurry*).

### Time and condition clauses

After *if, unless, when, before, after, until, as soon as*, the future is expressed by the present simple (see the lesson on tenses): *I will call you when I reach.*

:::qc
q: "No sooner had he stepped out ___ it began to pour."
options: ["when", "then", "than", "that"]
answer: 2
right: Right. No sooner takes than.
wrong: Hardly and scarcely take when. No sooner is paired with than.
:::

## Prepositions and conjunctions in board questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

The paper tests this topic in three ways.

1. **Gap filling with prepositions.** Look at the word just before the gap. If it is an adjective or verb with a fixed pairing, use the pairing. If it is a noun of time or place, apply the at/on/in rules.
2. **Editing.** One word in a line is wrong or extra. Common errors: a wrong appropriate preposition, a doubled conjunction (*although ... but*), a missing preposition (*He is married a doctor*), an extra preposition (*He entered in the room*), or the wrong correlative partner.
3. **Sentence joining (synthesis).** Combine two sentences using the word you are told. Pick the conjunction that matches the relation: contrast (*although*), condition (*unless*), reason (*because*), result (*so ... that*).

A routine that works:

- Ask what **relation** the sentence expresses (time, place, reason, contrast, condition).
- Check for a **fixed pairing** with the word before the gap.
- Read the finished sentence and check you have not used **two** linking words for one job.
- Check the **form** after a preposition: object pronoun or -ing.

:::worked title="Joining with although"
*He was ill. He attended the function.* Relation: contrast. Use **although** with the subordinate clause: *Although he was ill, he attended the function.* Alternative with a preposition: *In spite of being ill, he attended the function.* After *in spite of* comes a noun or gerund, not a full clause.
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "She has been married with a doctor for five years."
options: ["married with should be married to", "has been should be is", "for should be since"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The standard pairing is married to.
wrong: Check the fixed pairing: married to. For five years is correct because it names a length of time.
:::

## Beyond the board: finer points {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### One word, three jobs

Many words can be a preposition, a conjunction or an adverb. The test is what follows.

| Word | Preposition | Conjunction | Adverb |
|---|---|---|---|
| after | after lunch | after we ate | soon after |
| before | before noon | before the rain began | I have seen him before |
| since | since 2015 | since you asked | we have not met since |
| until | until Friday | until it stops | (no adverb use) |

A preposition has a noun or pronoun as its object. A conjunction begins a clause with its own subject and verb. An adverb stands alone.

### Conjunctive adverbs

*However, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, consequently, otherwise* join ideas in meaning but do not join the grammar. Two full sentences linked by one of them need a full stop or a semicolon, not a bare comma.

- Wrong: *He was tired, however he went on.*
- Right: *He was tired; however, he went on.* Or: *He was tired. However, he went on.*

Compare *but*, a true conjunction, which can follow a comma: *He was tired, but he went on.*

### Like and as

*Like* is a preposition and compares nouns: *She sings like a bird.* *As* is a conjunction that begins a clause (*Do as I say*) or means "in the role of" (*He works as a pilot*). *Like* before a full clause (*Do like I do*) is common in speech but is avoided in formal writing.

### Because of and due to

*Because of* and *owing to* answer "why" and go with an action: *The match was cancelled because of the rain.* *Due to* traditionally follows a form of be: *The delay was due to the rain.* [Unverified: some usage guides now accept *due to* in both positions; board answers are safest following the traditional rule.]

### Different from, different than

*Different from* is the standard in school grammar. *Different to* is common in British English and *different than* in American English; do not use them in board answers.

### Lest and in case

*Lest* means "for fear that" and is followed by *should*: *Run, lest you should miss the bus.* It already carries a negative, so do not add *not*. *In case* is more usual in modern English: *Take an umbrella in case it rains.*

### Between three or more

*Among* is the board answer for three or more, but *between* is also correct when the items are seen as distinct and related one to one: *The treaty between the three nations.* Examiners at school level expect between for two and among for more.

### Prepositions and phrasal verbs

A preposition or adverb joined to a verb can change the meaning completely: *look after* (care for), *look up* (search), *look into* (investigate), *give up*, *put off*. These phrasal verbs are learned as units, and they appear often in comprehension passages.

:::qc
q: Which is correct?
options: ["He was tired, however he worked on.", "He was tired; however, he worked on.", "He was tired however, he worked on."]
answer: 1
right: Right. However is a conjunctive adverb, not a true conjunction, so it needs a semicolon or full stop before it.
wrong: A comma alone cannot join two full sentences. However needs a semicolon or full stop before it, and a comma after it.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A preposition links a noun, pronoun or gerund to the rest of the sentence; a conjunction links words, phrases or clauses. {lvl=1}
2. After a preposition use the object form of a pronoun (between you and me) and -ing for a verb (good at singing). {lvl=1}
3. Time: at (clock time), on (day or date), in (month, year, season). Place: at (point), on (surface), in (enclosed space). {lvl=1}
4. Co-ordinating conjunctions (and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor) join equals; subordinating ones (because, although, if, unless, when) make one clause depend on another. {lvl=1}
5. Learn appropriate prepositions as pairs: afraid of, good at, interested in, married to, different from. {lvl=2}
6. Superior, inferior, senior, junior and prefer take to, never than. {lvl=2}
7. Between for two, among for many; beside means next to, besides means in addition to; by for the doer, with for the tool. {lvl=2}
8. Never use two conjunctions for one job: although with but, or because with so. {lvl=2}
9. Neither...nor and either...or: the verb agrees with the nearer subject. No sooner takes than; hardly and scarcely take when. {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: the same word can be preposition, conjunction or adverb; however joins ideas but needs a semicolon or full stop. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "Between you and I": use the object form, "between you and me". {lvl=1}
- "Good at sing" or "interested in to learn": use -ing after a preposition. {lvl=1}
- "In Monday", "on June": days take on, months take in. {lvl=1}
- "Afraid from", "fond for", "good in chess": learn the fixed pairing instead of guessing. {lvl=2}
- "Senior than me", "prefer tea than coffee": use to. {lvl=2}
- "Although he was tired, but he worked on": drop one of the two. {lvl=2}
- "Because it rained, so we stayed in": drop one of the two. {lvl=2}
- "He entered in the room", "She is married with him": an extra preposition, or the wrong one. {lvl=2}
- "Unless you do not hurry": unless already means if not. {lvl=2}
- "No sooner she left than...": invert after no sooner, and use had + V3. {lvl=2}
- "He was tired, however he worked on": a comma cannot join two sentences with however. {lvl=3}
:::
