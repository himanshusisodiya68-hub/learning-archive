## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-story label="Foundation"}

Kisa Gotami's only son dies. She cannot accept it, so she carries the body from neighbour to neighbour and asks for medicine. People think she has lost her senses. At last a man who cannot help her says he knows someone who can: the Buddha.

She goes to him and begs for the medicine that will cure her boy. The Buddha replies that he needs a handful of mustard seed. When she joyfully promises to fetch it, he adds a condition: the seed must come from a house where nobody has lost a child, husband, parent or friend.

She goes from house to house. People are kind and offer seed, but when she asks whether anyone has died in the family, they answer that the living are few and the dead are many, and ask her not to open old wounds. Every home has lost someone. Tired and hopeless, she sits by the road and watches the city lights come on and go out. Slowly she sees that human lives flicker up and die away in the same way, that death is common to all, and that she has been **selfish in her grief**.

The Buddha then speaks. He says that the life of mortals is short and full of pain, that no one born can avoid dying, and that neither fathers nor relatives can save the dying. Weeping and complaining bring no peace and cannot save the dead. Whoever wants peace must pull out the arrow of grief, and only then can he become calm and free of sorrow.

:::qc
q: Why does Kisa Gotami fail to get a single mustard seed?

answer: 1
right: Correct. Villagers are happy to give seed, but the condition (no death in the family) cannot be met by any home, which is what teaches her.

## The Buddha and the setting {lvl=1 id=s-buddha label="Foundation"}

The chapter begins with a short biography. Gautama Buddha was born a prince named Siddhartha Gautama in northern India. According to the introduction he was sent away to study at twelve, came home to marry a princess, had a son and lived like a royal for about ten years. Around the age of twenty-five he went hunting and, for the first time, saw a sick man, an old man, a funeral procession and a monk begging for alms. These sights moved him so deeply that he left home to seek **enlightenment** (a state of high spiritual knowledge).

After wandering for seven years he sat under a peepal tree and vowed to stay until enlightenment came. After seven days he was enlightened, the tree was renamed the Bodhi Tree (Tree of Wisdom), and he became known as the Buddha, "the Awakened". He preached his first sermon at Benares, a holy city on the Ganges, and the chapter says this sermon reflects his wisdom on one **inscrutable** (impossible to understand) kind of suffering.

| Detail | What the chapter says | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Sheltered young life | Siddhartha was shielded from suffering in his palace | His shock at seeing sickness, age and death explains why he later understands Kisa Gotami's grief |
| Four sights | A sick man, an old man, a funeral, a monk | The first three are what every mortal faces; the monk shows one response |
| Benares | Holy city on the Ganges, a "dipping place" (bathing place) | The chapter calls it the place of his first sermon |
| Dates | 563 B.C. to 483 B.C. in the book | Treat exact dates as [Unverified] in an essay; modern scholars differ |

## How the Buddha teaches {lvl=2 id=s-teach label="Class 10 board"}

**The method.** The Buddha could have said at once: "Your son is dead and nothing can bring him back." She would have heard it as cruelty. Instead he does three things in order.

1. He **accepts her request** and gives her hope. She leaves smiling, because for the first time someone has taken her seriously.
2. He sets a **task with a hidden condition**. The mustard seed is ordinary and the families will gladly give it, so she cannot feel sent away.
3. He lets her **find the answer in the world**. Each refusal shows her that death has visited all, and the walk does what a lecture could not.

Only after she has understood does he speak, and his sermon puts her discovery into words.

**First walk and second walk.**

| | First walk | Second walk |
|---|---|---|
| What she asks for | Medicine to cure her dead son | A handful of mustard seed from a house where no one has died |
| Does she get it? | No. People think she has lost her senses and the child is beyond help | No. Seed is offered, but no house is free of death |
| Why not? | Nobody has a cure for death | The condition cannot be met, because death is in every home |
| What changes | She is alone and cannot accept the loss | She sees death is common to all and that she was selfish in her grief |

**Why only the second time?** The first time she refuses to hear anyone: her grief shuts out all argument, so the truth is just words. The second time the Buddha has given her a purpose and a question, and the people she meets tell her their own losses in their own voices. She works it out, so it belongs to her. The Buddha has changed her understanding by changing her **question** from "Where is the cure?" to "Is there any house without death?"

**Selfish in her grief.** In everyday talk, selfish means caring only for one's own gain. Here it has a gentler sense: grief that sees only my loss and forgets that everyone else has the same one. Kisa Gotami does not mean she did something wrong; she means she felt as though her suffering was unique. Whether you agree is a personal view. One possible view: she is not blameworthy, but her grief had narrowed her sight until she could not see others.

**Themes.**
- **Death is common to all.** No house is free of it, and no father or relative can save the dying.
- **Grief that clings does not help.** Weeping does not bring back the dead; it only harms the body and the mind.
- **Compassion and wisdom together.** The Buddha feels her pain, yet he does not lie to comfort her.
- **Learning by experience.** The deepest lessons are the ones we discover.

## The sermon in three steps {lvl=2 id=s-sermon label="Class 10 board"}

The sermon is a short argument. Break it into steps to answer any question on it.

| Step | What the Buddha says (in our words) | Image he uses |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Life is short and ends in death | Everyone born must die, young or old, wise or foolish | Ripe fruit that may fall at any time; earthen pots that are all broken in the end |
| 2. No one can rescue another | A father cannot save his son, nor relatives their kin; they can only watch | An ox led to the slaughter while others look on |
| 3. Grief does not help | Weeping adds to pain and sickens the body, and the dead are not saved by it | An arrow that must be drawn out for the wounded to find peace |

Notice the logic. Steps 1 and 2 are facts about the world; step 3 is advice that follows from them. If death cannot be avoided and cannot be prevented by crying, then clinging to grief gives no benefit, and **peace comes from letting go**. The last sentence makes it a promise: the person who draws out the arrow and grows calm will find peace and be free from sorrow.

right: Correct. The Buddha compares grief to an arrow lodged in the body; only by pulling it out can the person find peace.

## Old-fashioned language {lvl=2 id=s-oldstyle label="Class 10 board"}

**Why the text sounds old.** The incident is more than two thousand years old, and the English retelling imitates an older, formal style. Some words are no longer used in everyday speech; others have simply changed in tone. Rewriting them in current English is a useful skill: you must understand the meaning first, then find the plain form.

| Old-fashioned form | Modern equivalent | Note |
|---|---|---|
| give thee medicine for thy child | give you medicine for your child | *thee* is the old object form of *you*; *thy* is *your* |
| Pray tell me | Please tell me | *Pray* here is a polite request, as in "I pray you" |
| Kisa repaired to the Buddha | Kisa went to the Buddha | *repaired* in this sense means *went*; not the same as mending |
| there was no house but someone had died in it | every house had lost someone (or: there was no house where no one had died) | *but* here means *except that*, a double negative in the old style |
| kinsmen | relatives, family members | *kin* means family; *kinsmen* are people of the same family or tribe |
| Mark! | Look! / Take note! / Listen! | A call for attention before an important point |

**Tip.** When you rephrase, keep the meaning but change only the form. Do not make the sentence longer, and do not lose the politeness: "Pray tell me" is polite, so "Tell me" alone is too blunt.

## Semicolons and dashes {lvl=2 id=s-semi label="Class 10 board"}

**Why they exist.** Usually we join two clauses with a word such as *and*, *or*, *but*, *yet* or *then*. But sometimes no such word fits. The two clauses may be too closely linked to be separate sentences, yet no joining word says exactly how. Then we use a **semicolon** (;) or a **dash** (—).

| Mark | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Semicolon | Joins closely linked clauses of equal weight, often so the second explains or comments on the first | She has no interest in music; I doubt she will become a singer. |
| Dash | Adds a comment, summary or an afterthought; slightly more dramatic than a semicolon | The rain stopped at last — the match could begin. |
| Semicolon in a list | Separates long items that already contain commas | We visited Agra, which has the Taj; Jaipur, the pink city; and Delhi. |

**The text's sentence.** The Buddha says there is no means by which the born can avoid dying; after old age there is death; of such a nature are living beings. Split into three simple sentences it reads:
1. There is not any means by which those who have been born can avoid dying.
2. After reaching old age there is death.
3. Of such a nature are living beings.

**Which is better?** The semicolon version has a steady, accumulating rhythm, like a slow drum: each clause adds weight to the last and the whole feels like one solemn truth. Three short sentences stop and start, and feel more like three separate announcements. For a sermon the single sentence suits the tone; for a quick explanation the short ones are easier to follow. Both are correct; the choice is about effect.

**Common slips.** Do not put a semicolon before a joining word like *and* when it joins two short clauses (use a comma instead). Do not use a semicolon where the second part is not a full clause.

:::qc
q: Which sentence uses a semicolon correctly?
options: ["He was tired; because he had run all day.", "He was tired; he had run all day.", "He was tired; and, he had run all day."]
answer: 1
right: Right. Each side of the semicolon is a full clause, and the second explains the first, so no joining word is needed.
wrong: A semicolon must be followed by a complete clause and not by a joining word such as because. Which option has two complete clauses?
:::

## The craft of the sermon {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**A story that holds a sermon.** The chapter pairs a short narrative (the mustard seed) with a speech (the sermon). The story makes the idea felt; the sermon then states it in general terms. Without the story, the sermon would sound abstract; without the sermon, the story would leave the lesson unspoken. Together they follow the pattern of many religious and moral texts: **experience first, explanation after**.

**Images from ordinary life.** The Buddha does not use technical terms. His images come from things the listeners have seen daily: ripe fruit hanging on a tree, a potter's clay pots, an ox led away. Each image carries a simple idea: *ripe fruit* (death may come at any time, and the riper, the sooner), *earthen vessels* (anything made will one day break), *ox led to the slaughter* (the doomed are carried off while family watch helplessly). Notice that all three images are calm and plain. He does not threaten or shock; he describes.

**Parallelism and repetition.** The sermon uses balanced structures: "both young and adult, both those who are fools and those who are wise". This pairing says that death does not choose, and the repetition of *both* makes the point firm. The same device appears in the semicolon sentence, where three clauses of similar shape follow each other.

**Tone.** The tone is calm, grave and compassionate. Words like *mark!* demand attention, while the statements are plain and unemotional. The calm is part of the lesson: the Buddha models the composure he hopes she will find.

**A note on the opening.** The question "Can *sermon* also be used in a negative way or as a joke?" shows that the word has two lives. In its main sense it is a respected religious address; in casual speech it can mean a long, preachy telling-off. The chapter's sermon deserves the first sense, because it is short, gentle and based on her own experience.

## Then and now {lvl=3 id=s-today label="Beyond the board"}

The chapter's discussion activity asks you to compare the Buddha's sermon with two recent texts on grief: a practical guide to coping with the death of a loved one, and a personal essay by a father who lost a son. Use a short table to organise your thinking.

| | The Buddha's sermon | A modern guide (as described in the activity) | A personal essay (as described in the activity) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voice | A teacher speaking to one grieving person | An impersonal guide for many readers | One person writing about his own losses |
| Main message | Death is common to all; clinging to grief gives no peace | Grief is natural and takes many forms; be patient with those who grieve | There is no one right way to grieve; doing things for each other is the comfort |
| Method | A task, then a short speech | Lists of common feelings and practical tips | Memory and personal experience |
| On the feeling itself | Do not cling to it | Expect many feelings (sadness, anger, guilt, loneliness) | Anger is allowed; keep busy for each other |

**Possible conclusion (one possible answer).** Modern texts add something the sermon lacks: they allow many feelings, treat each person's grief as unique, and tell the helper what to do. The Buddha adds something they sometimes lack: a way of seeing that loosens grief's hold by placing it among all human loss. The two are not rivals. A grieving person may need patience and company first, and a wider view later. This is a discussion question, so any reasoned view that uses the texts is acceptable.

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}
