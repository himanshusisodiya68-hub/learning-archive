## What the examiner is really asking {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Picture a friend on the phone. You are looking at a chart and she is not. "Read me all the numbers" would be a boring call. What she needs is: "Samosas sell the most by far, and fruit cups hardly sell at all." Writing an analytical paragraph is that phone call, put on paper.

The board's task has three features.

- You get **data**: a table, a bar graph, a line graph, a pie chart, a flowchart or a list of cues (short hints).
- You write **one paragraph** in a stated word limit, usually 100 to 120 words [Unverified for the current year's paper; check the latest sample paper].
- You are marked on **content** (the right points, with accurate figures) and **expression** (clear, correct English in your own words).

The key difference is between three levels of writing.

| Level | What it does | Example (canteen data, illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Describe the layout | Says how the chart looks | "The table has four rows." |
| Report the data | Repeats figures | "Samosas sold 240 and fruit cups sold 45." |
| **Analyse** | Compares, groups and explains | "Samosas outsold fruit cups more than five to one, which suggests students prefer fried snacks." |

Only the third gets full marks. The first is wasted words, and the second is the "listing" that examiners call a copy of the chart.

## Reading the data before you write {lvl=1 id=s-read label="Foundation"}

Good paragraphs are made before the pen moves. Spend two minutes asking the same five questions of any data.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| What is the title, and what are the units (per cent, number of students, litres)? | Your opening sentence and every figure need them. |
| Which is the highest and which is the lowest? | These are the first details an examiner expects. |
| What is the biggest change, or the biggest gap? | It usually holds the main story. |
| Is there an overall direction (up, down, steady, mixed)? | This becomes your "overall" sentence. |
| Does anything surprise me, or point to a cause? | This gives your reason and conclusion. |

Try it on a small table. All figures in this lesson are invented for practice.

**Canteen sales, one school week (illustrative data)**

| Item | Packets sold |
|---|---|
| Samosa | 240 |
| Sandwich | 150 |
| Juice | 90 |
| Fruit cup | 45 |
| **Total** | **525** |

- Highest: samosa, 240. Lowest: fruit cup, 45.
- Gap: 240 is a little more than five times 45.
- Share: samosas are 240 out of 525, nearly half (about 46 per cent).
- Surprise: the healthy item sells least. This may give a reason and a suggestion.

Notice that you have not written a sentence yet, but you already have the paragraph's middle.

:::qc
q: In the canteen table, which comparison is accurate?
options: ["Samosas sold about twice as many packets as sandwiches.", "Juice sold more than the sandwich.", "Fruit cups sold about half as many as juice.", "Sandwiches were the best-selling item."]
answer: 2
right: Right. Fruit cups sold 45 and juice sold 90, so fruit cups were exactly half the juice figure.
wrong: Check each pair. Samosa 240 is 1.6 times sandwich 150, not twice. Juice 90 is less than sandwich 150. Samosa, not sandwich, sold most. Only 45 against 90 is a half.
:::

## The language of data {lvl=1 id=s-words label="Foundation"}

Your reader needs precise words for movement, size and comparison. A small stock of each is enough.

**Words for change**

| Movement | Verbs and nouns | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Up | rose, climbed, increased, grew, went up; a rise, a growth | any increase |
| Reaching a top | reached a peak of, peaked at, touched a high of | the highest point on a graph |
| Down | fell, dropped, declined, decreased, dipped, slipped; a fall, a drop | any decrease |
| Steady | remained steady, stayed constant, levelled off, showed no change | no movement |
| Very fast | soared, shot up, plummeted, collapsed | a very large change; use sparingly |

**Words for speed and size**

| Idea | Words |
|---|---|
| Quick or large change | sharply, steeply, rapidly, dramatically |
| Slow or small change | gradually, steadily, slightly, marginally |
| Approximation | about, around, nearly, almost, just over, roughly |
| Fractions and ratios | double, twice as many as, half, a quarter, one in three, three-fifths, over five times |

**Words for comparing**

| Job | Words |
|---|---|
| Contrast | while, whereas, but, in contrast, however |
| Similarity | like, similarly, both, equally |
| Extremes | the highest, the lowest, the most, the least, the largest share |
| Result | therefore, as a result, so, hence |

Two points about accuracy:

- Say *from ... to ...* for a change: *rose from 20 to 35 per cent.* Say *by* for the size of the change: *rose by 15 percentage points* when the data is already in per cent [Unverified: the board may not insist on the distinction].
- Use *the number of* for things you count (students, books) and *the amount of* or *the percentage of* for quantities and shares. *The number of students has risen* takes a singular verb.

:::qc
q: The table shows 200 visitors in May and 800 in June. Which sentence is accurate?
options: ["Visitors halved between May and June.", "Visitors rose slightly between May and June.", "Visitors increased fourfold between May and June.", "Visitors were steady between May and June."]
answer: 2
right: Right. 800 is four times 200, so fourfold (or quadrupled) is accurate.
wrong: Divide the later figure by the earlier: 800 / 200 = 4. A rise of four times is fourfold, which is far from slight, steady or halved.
:::

## The five-part layout {lvl=2 id=s-layout label="Class 10"}

An analytical paragraph is not free writing. It runs on a layout that the examiner can see at once. The five parts below fit comfortably into 100 to 120 words.

| Part | Job | Words | Typical starters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Opening | Say what the data is, with place, period and units | 15 to 20 | "The table compares ..." "The bar graph shows ..." |
| 2. Main trend | Give the big picture in one sentence | 15 to 20 | "Overall, ..." "The most striking feature is ..." |
| 3. Key details | Quote the highest, lowest and biggest change, with figures; use comparison | 35 to 45 | "While ..., ..." "In contrast, ..." "from ... to ..." |
| 4. Reason | Offer a likely cause or meaning, hedged | 15 to 20 | "This may be because ..." "This suggests that ..." |
| 5. Conclusion | State the result, warning or suggestion | 15 to 20 | "Therefore, ... could ..." "It is clear that ..." |

Some rules that hold for every task:

- **One paragraph.** No heading, bullets or numbering, and no line space between the parts.
- **Third person, formal tone.** No "I think", no "we can see", no slang.
- **Own words.** Do not copy phrases from the question or the chart labels line by line.
- **Figures from the data only.** Do not invent a number or fact that the chart does not give. A well-known general fact can support the reason, but keep it short and hedged.
- **Order.** Go from the overall picture to details, not in the table's row order, unless the order happens to be the story.

:::qc
q: Which part comes immediately after the opening sentence?
options: ["The conclusion", "The main trend or overall picture", "The reason for the data", "A list of every figure in row order"]
answer: 1
right: Right. After saying what the data is, give the big picture, then support it with details.
wrong: The paragraph moves from general to specific: opening, main trend, key details, reason, conclusion.
:::

## A model sample {lvl=2 id=s-model label="Class 10"}

Here is a complete task, written the way a good answer reads. The data is invented for practice.

**Task:** Write an analytical paragraph in 100 to 120 words on the following table.

**How Class 10 students travel to school (per cent of students, illustrative data)**

| Mode | 2018 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Walk | 30 | 22 |
| Bicycle | 25 | 15 |
| School bus | 20 | 28 |
| Private vehicle | 25 | 35 |
| **Total** | **100** | **100** |

**Planning (the two minutes):** highest in 2018 is walking (30); highest in 2023 is private vehicles (35); biggest fall is cycling (25 to 15); biggest rise is private vehicles (up 10 points). Walk and bicycle are muscle power (55 per cent in 2018, 37 in 2023); bus and private are motorised (45 per cent, then 63). Reason: longer distances or unsafe roads. Conclusion: more traffic, so encourage buses and cycle lanes.

**Model answer (119 words):**

> The table compares how Class 10 students travelled to school in 2018 and 2023. It reveals a shift from muscle power to motor power. In 2018, walking led at 30 per cent, followed by cycling and private vehicles at 25 each, while buses carried only 20. By 2023, private vehicles led at 35 per cent and buses had climbed to 28 per cent, whereas walking slipped to 22 per cent and cycling fell most sharply, to 15 per cent. Overall, motorised travel grew from 45 to 63 per cent. This may be because homes are farther from school or roads feel unsafe for cyclists. More cars mean more traffic, so the school could promote buses and safer cycle lanes.

:::note title="How the paragraph maps onto the layout" tone=tip
Sentences 1 to 2 are the **opening** and **main trend**. Sentences 3 and 4 are the **key details**, with the highest, the lowest and the biggest change. Sentence 5 gives the summed-up figure that proves the trend. Sentence 6 is the **reason**, hedged by *may*. Sentence 7 is the **conclusion**. Every figure comes from the table or is simply added from it (28 + 35 = 63).
:::

:::qc
q: In the model answer, what does the phrase "This may be because" do?
options: ["States the cause as a proved fact", "Signals that the reason is a sensible guess", "Repeats the data", "Opens the conclusion"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The table cannot tell us why travel changed, so the writer offers possible reasons and marks them as guesses.
wrong: A table shows what changed, not why. "May" tells the reader that the reason is a guess.
:::

## Tense and voice in a data paragraph {lvl=2 id=s-tense label="Class 10"}

Mistakes in tense are the commonest slip in this task, and the cure is a simple rule: the tense follows the **time of the data**, not the time of your writing.

| What you are writing about | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The chart itself | Present simple | The graph **shows** ... The table **compares** ... |
| Past years or a past period | Past simple | Sales **rose** in 2021 and **fell** in 2022. |
| A change that links the past to now | Present perfect | The number of users **has doubled** since 2018. |
| One event before another in the past | Past perfect | By 2023 buses **had climbed** to 28 per cent. |
| Forecasts or projections | will, is expected to | The figure **is expected to** reach 60 per cent by 2030. |
| A general truth in the reason or conclusion | Present simple, modals | Heavy traffic **causes** pollution. Schools **could** promote buses. |

Two details help.

- Keep **one time frame** inside a section. A paragraph on 2018 to 2023 should not slide into the present because the writer feels close to the story.
- The **passive** suits a data paragraph when the doer does not matter: *Most books were issued on Thursday.* But do not overuse it; the active is usually clearer.

## Different kinds of data {lvl=2 id=s-types label="Class 10"}

The board may use any of these formats. The five-part layout stays the same; only the way you read the data changes.

| Format | What to look for | Useful phrases |
|---|---|---|
| Table | Rows and columns: compare across rows and down columns; check totals | "The table compares ...", "highest ... lowest ..." |
| Bar graph | Height of bars: tallest, shortest, equal | "The tallest bar shows ...", "twice as high" |
| Line graph | Direction over time: rise, fall, peak, plateau | "rose steadily from ... to ...", "peaked at ... in ..." |
| Pie chart | Shares of a whole: biggest slice, fractions | "accounts for nearly half", "one in five", "the smallest share" |
| Flow chart or map | Order of steps or places; cause and effect | "The process begins with ...", "which then leads to ..." |
| Cue list | Headings with short hints, but no numbers | Join cues into reasoning; do not just list them |

The cue list is slightly different. With no figures, your analysis lives in cause, effect and suggestion. A cue sheet on "online shopping among teenagers" might give *convenience, discounts, home delivery* under Reasons, and *fraud, overspending, returns* under Concerns, and *awareness, secure apps* under Solutions. The strong answer states the trend, links reasons to concerns and ends with a solution. It uses a linker to connect the cues: *Although online shopping is convenient and cheap, it also brings fraud and overspending, so users must ...*

:::qc
q: A pie chart shows "Education" as 25 per cent of a family budget. Which phrase is best?
options: ["Education accounts for one-quarter of the budget.", "Education is the fourth bar.", "Education rose by a quarter.", "Education is 25 times the budget."]
answer: 0
right: Right. A pie slice is a share of a whole, and 25 per cent is a quarter.
wrong: Pie charts show shares, not bars or changes over time. 25 per cent of the whole is one-quarter.
:::

## The marking checklist {lvl=2 id=s-mark label="Class 10"}

The board splits the marks between **content** and **expression** [Unverified: the exact breakdown and the maximum marks vary by year, so check the latest marking scheme]. The checklist below is written for this archive and follows those two ideas. Mark your own practice answers against it.

| Check | Question to ask yourself | Done |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Does the first sentence name the data, place, period and units? | |
| Trend | Is there one sentence on the overall pattern? | |
| Figures | Are the highest, lowest and biggest change quoted correctly? | |
| Comparison | Did I compare (more than, twice, while, whereas) instead of listing? | |
| Reason | Is there one hedged reason, based on the data? | |
| Conclusion | Is there a final so-what, suggestion or warning? | |
| Facts | Is every figure from the data, with nothing invented? | |
| Length | Is it in the word limit (count it)? | |
| Form | One paragraph, formal tone, no "I think", no bullets? | |
| Language | Are tenses, agreement, spelling and punctuation correct? | |

A simple scoring habit: for each "no", you have found a mark you can recover. The commonest losses are listing instead of analysing, wrong figures, no conclusion, and going far over or under the word limit.

:::worked title="From weak to strong in four edits"
**Data (illustrative):** plastic bags used per family per week: 2019: 24; 2021: 15; 2023: 6.

**Weak draft:** *The graph shows plastic bags. In 2019 it is 24. In 2021 it is 15. In 2023 it is 6. The graph shows it is going down.*

**Faults:** (1) the opening does not say what is measured, where or for whom; (2) the verbs are in the wrong tense (*is* for past years); (3) the figures are listed, not compared; (4) no reason or conclusion.

**Strong draft:** *The graph shows the number of plastic bags a family used each week in 2019, 2021 and 2023. Use fell steadily from 24 bags in 2019 to 15 in 2021 and just 6 in 2023, a drop of three-quarters in four years. This may be because of awareness campaigns or the spread of cloth bags. If the trend continues, plastic bags could soon be rare in homes.*

Each fault is fixed in turn: a full opening, the past simple, a comparison (three-quarters), and a hedged reason plus conclusion.
:::

:::qc
q: Which is the biggest mark-loser in an analytical paragraph?
options: ["Starting with 'The table shows'", "Listing figures one by one with no comparison or meaning", "Using the word 'overall'", "Ending with a short suggestion"]
answer: 1
right: Right. A list of figures copies the chart and does not analyse it.
wrong: Opening with 'the table shows' is fine, and 'overall' and a suggestion are good. The real loss is listing without comparison or meaning.

## Fact, inference and honest hedging {lvl=3 id=s-infer label="Beyond the board"}

The best paragraphs keep two kinds of statement apart.

- A **fact from the data**: *Cycling fell from 25 to 15 per cent.* Nobody can argue; look at the table.
- An **inference**: *Roads may feel unsafe for cyclists.* A reasonable guess, but the table does not say so.

The risk is that guesses slip into the writing wearing the clothes of facts. Three rules guard against it.

1. Give the inference its own sentence, after the facts.
2. Hedge it with a word that suits its strength.
3. Prefer an inference that the data itself supports, such as a pattern across groups, not a story you like.

| Strength | Hedge | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Weak guess | might, could, perhaps, one possible reason | Perhaps students had more free time. |
| Reasonable guess | may, probably, is likely to, suggests | Sales probably peaked because of the monsoon. |
| Strong support in the data | indicates, points to, clearly shows | The steady fall indicates a lasting change. |

Compare *Umbrella sales peaked in July because it rained* (stated as fact; the data never mentions rain) with *Umbrella sales peaked in July, which probably reflects the monsoon rains.* The second is safe, honest and still insightful.

A last point: a conclusion should follow from the reason, not from a new topic. If your reason was unsafe roads, a conclusion about safer cycle lanes is logical. A conclusion about fuel prices is a new idea and has no support.

:::qc
q: Which sentence is best hedged? (Data: umbrella sales peaked in July.)
options: ["Umbrella sales peaked in July because the monsoon arrived.", "Umbrella sales peaked in July, which probably reflects the monsoon rains.", "Umbrella sellers earned a lot of money in July.", "Umbrella sales were highest in July, definitely due to rain."]
answer: 1
right: Right. It states the fact, then adds a likely reason marked with probably.
wrong: Because and definitely present a guess as a fact, and the earnings sentence brings in a figure the data does not support.
:::

## Style: from correct to polished {lvl=3 id=s-style label="Beyond the board"}

Once the structure is sound, a few habits lift the paragraph from competent to memorable.

**Group the data.** Instead of five separate figures, cluster them: *Walking and cycling together fell from 55 to 37 per cent, while bus and private transport rose from 45 to 63 per cent.* One sentence now carries four figures and a pattern.

**Vary your openings.** If three sentences begin with *The table shows*, the paragraph feels like a drum. Mix subjects: *Walking led ...*, *By 2023, ...*, *Buses, in contrast, ...*, *Overall, ...*.

**Round sensibly.** *Nearly half* is better than *46.07 per cent* unless the exact figure matters. Use exact figures for the highest, the lowest and the main change; use fractions and approximations for the rest.

**Use percentage points for changes in a share.** From 25 to 35 per cent is a rise of ten percentage points, not "ten per cent" [Unverified: the board is unlikely to penalise this slip, but the careful form shows control].

**Make the conclusion do work.** A weak ending says *Thus the graph shows many things.* A strong ending looks ahead (*If this continues, ...*), recommends (*Schools could ...*) or warns (*However, ...*), and uses a modal to keep the claim modest.

**Check connection, not decoration.** Every linker should earn its place: *whereas* for a real contrast, *therefore* for a real result. A paragraph with five *however*s sounds argumentative even when the data is not.

:::qc
q: Which sentence is the strongest way to express this data? (Walk 30 to 22; Bicycle 25 to 15, in per cent.)
options: ["Walking went down and bicycle went down.", "Walking fell by eight points and cycling by ten, so the two together dropped from 55 to 37 per cent.", "Walk is 22 and bicycle is 15 in 2023.", "Fewer people walk and cycle than before, which is bad."]
answer: 1
right: Right. It groups the two, gives the changes, and adds a combined figure that proves the pattern, with no outside opinion.
wrong: The others are vague, list figures, or add an unsupported judgement (bad). The best sentence groups and quantifies.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. An analytical paragraph tells a reader who cannot see the chart what it shows and what it means; it does not copy the figures. {lvl=1}
2. Read first: units, highest, lowest, biggest change, overall direction, anything surprising. {lvl=1}
3. Use precise words for change (rose, fell, peaked), speed (sharply, steadily) and comparison (twice, whereas, nearly). {lvl=1}
4. The layout is opening, main trend, key details with figures, hedged reason, conclusion, all in one paragraph of about 100 to 120 words. {lvl=2}
5. Tense follows the time of the data: past simple for past years, present simple for what the chart shows, will for forecasts. {lvl=2}
6. Use only the figures given. Add no outside facts, no opinion and no "I think". {lvl=2}
7. For a cue list with no numbers, join the cues with linkers into cause, concern and solution. {lvl=2}
8. Check yourself against the list: opening, trend, figures, comparison, reason, conclusion, facts, length, form, language. {lvl=2}
9. Keep fact and inference apart: facts first, then a hedged guess (may, probably, suggests). {lvl=3}
10. Polish with grouped data, varied openings, sensible rounding and a conclusion that follows from the reason. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Copying the table row by row ("Samosa 240, sandwich 150 ..."): compare and group instead. {lvl=1}
- Describing the layout ("The table has two columns"): say what the data is about. {lvl=1}
- Forgetting units: write per cent, litres, thousands or crore as the data gives them. {lvl=1}
- Wrong figures or wrong comparisons (calling 1.6 times "twice"): recompute before you write. {lvl=1}
- Switching tense mid-paragraph ("sales rose in 2021 and fall in 2022"): keep the past for past data. {lvl=2}
- Writing "the number of students have risen": the number takes a singular verb, "has risen". {lvl=2}
- Bringing in outside facts or invented figures that the data does not give. {lvl=2}
- No conclusion, or a conclusion that opens a new topic. {lvl=2}
- Going far under or over the word limit; count the words in the draft. {lvl=2}
- Bullets, headings or "I think" in the answer: it must be one formal paragraph. {lvl=2}
- Stating a guessed cause as fact ("because it rained"): hedge it (may, probably, suggests). {lvl=3}
- Five "however"s or "therefore"s with no real contrast or result: a linker must earn its place. {lvl=3}
:::
