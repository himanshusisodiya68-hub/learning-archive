## Why English needs relative clauses {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

A noun on its own is often too vague. "The girl" could be any girl. To pin her down, you can add an adjective (*the tall girl*), but sometimes the useful fact needs a whole verb: she won the race, she lives next door, she lost her bag. A short sentence would do the job, but it would stand alone and break the flow.

A **relative clause** solves this. It hangs a small sentence onto a noun and uses a word to point back to that noun.

- *The girl **who won the race** is my cousin.*

Here "who won the race" is a clause because it has its own subject (the word *who* stands for the girl) and its own verb (*won*). It is called **relative** because it relates, or links, back to the noun, here *girl*. That noun is called the **antecedent**, meaning "the word that comes before".

Without such clauses we would speak in lists of choppy sentences:

- *I met a girl. She won the race. She is my cousin.*
- *I met a girl who won the race. She is my cousin.*

The relative pronoun does two jobs at once. It **replaces** the noun (so you do not repeat it) and it **joins** the two ideas. Many grammar books therefore call words like *who* and *which* **connectives that also act as pronouns**.

## The relative pronouns at a glance {lvl=1 id=s-pronouns label="Foundation"}

The right word depends on two questions. Is the noun a person or a thing? What job does the word do inside its own clause: subject, object, or possession?

| Word | Used for | Role in its clause | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| who | people | subject | *The doctor **who** treated me was kind.* |
| whom | people | object | *The doctor **whom** I met was kind.* |
| whose | people, things (belonging) | possession | *The boy **whose** bag was stolen cried.* |
| which | things and animals | subject or object | *The bag **which** was stolen was red.* |
| that | people, things, animals | subject or object, defining clauses only | *The bag **that** I bought was cheap.* |

Three ideas sit behind this table.

1. **People versus things.** English keeps *who* for people and *which* for things. Calling a person *which* sounds cold, as if they were an object.
2. **Subject versus object.** *Who* is used when the pronoun does the action (*the man who called*), and *whom* when the action falls on it (*the man whom I called*). In modern speech many people use *who* for both, but the board's formal English expects *whom* as an object.
3. **Belonging.** *Whose* means "of whom" or "of which". It is followed by a noun: *the girl whose brother is a pilot*.

:::note title="Quick test for who and whom" tone=tip
Replace the pronoun with he or him. If *he* fits, use *who*. If *him* fits, use *whom*. "The teacher (who/whom) you met": you met *him*, so *whom*. "The teacher (who/whom) teaches us": *he* teaches us, so *who*.
:::

:::qc
q: Choose the correct word: "The artist ___ painting won the prize is my neighbour."
options: ["who", "whom", "whose", "which"]
answer: 2
right: Right. The painting belongs to the artist, so whose is needed, and it is followed by the noun painting.
wrong: Ask what belongs to what. The painting belongs to the artist, so the word that shows belonging, whose, is the answer.
:::

## Where, when and why {lvl=1 id=s-wh label="Foundation"}

Besides pronouns, English also uses three **relative adverbs** to attach a clause to a noun of place, time or reason.

| Word | Noun it follows | Example |
|---|---|---|
| where | a place | *This is the school **where** my mother studied.* |
| when | a time | *I remember the day **when** we first met.* |
| why | the noun *reason* | *Nobody knows the reason **why** he left.* |

They do not replace a noun. They replace "in which", "on which" or "for which". *This is the school where my mother studied* means "This is the school in which my mother studied."

## Defining and non-defining clauses {lvl=2 id=s-defining label="Class 10"}

This is the most important distinction in the topic, and the one the board tests most. Compare:

- *Students **who cheat** will be punished.*
- *Mr Rao, **who teaches maths**, will be punished.*

In the first sentence, the clause is needed. Without it, the sentence says "Students will be punished", which means all students. The clause tells us **which** students. This is a **defining** (or restrictive) clause. It narrows the noun and carries no commas.

In the second sentence, we already know exactly who Mr Rao is. His name is enough. The clause only adds a spare fact. This is a **non-defining** (or non-restrictive) clause. It sits between **commas**, and the sentence still makes complete sense if you remove it.

| | Defining | Non-defining |
|---|---|---|
| Job | Tells which one is meant | Adds extra information |
| Can it be removed? | No: the meaning changes | Yes: the main idea stays |
| Commas | None | Before and after (or before, if at the end) |
| *that* allowed? | Yes | Never |
| Pronoun dropped (as object)? | Yes | Never |
| Typical noun | General: *a man, the book, students* | Already specific: a name, *my mother*, *the Taj Mahal* |

### Commas change the meaning

*My brother who lives in Pune is a doctor.* No commas: the clause defines. It suggests that I have more than one brother, and I mean the one who lives in Pune.

*My brother, who lives in Pune, is a doctor.* Commas: the clause only adds a fact. It suggests I have one brother.

So the commas are not decoration. They tell the reader how many people you are talking about.

### Rules that follow

- **Use that only in defining clauses.** *The bag that I lost was blue.* But *My bag, which I lost, was blue*, not *that*.
- **Names and one-of-a-kind nouns take non-defining clauses.** *Delhi, which is the capital, is crowded.* *The Sun, which is a star, gives us light.* A name is already specific, so a defining clause would be redundant.
- **A non-defining clause never uses that and keeps its commas on both sides.** A missing second comma is a common slip: *Mr Rao, who teaches maths will be punished* is wrong.

:::qc
q: Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
options: ["My aunt, who lives in Kochi, is visiting us.", "My aunt who lives in Kochi, is visiting us.", "My aunt, who lives in Kochi is visiting us."]
answer: 0
right: Right. The clause adds extra information about a specific person, so it needs a comma on each side.
wrong: A non-defining clause is fenced by commas on both sides. In the other options one of the commas is missing, which leaves the clause half-open.
:::

## Dropping the pronoun {lvl=2 id=s-drop label="Class 10"}

Often the relative pronoun can be left out. The rule is simple.

**The pronoun can be dropped only when it is the object of its own clause, and only in a defining clause.**

- *The book **that** I bought is old.* → *The book I bought is old.* (I bought *the book*: the book is the object.)
- *The man **whom** you met is my uncle.* → *The man you met is my uncle.*

It cannot be dropped when it is the **subject**:

- *The book **that** fell is mine.* (*Fell* needs a subject. The book did the falling.) Not *The book fell is mine.*
- *The girl **who** won the race is my cousin.* Not *The girl won the race is my cousin.*

How to test: after the pronoun, look at the next word. If it is a **verb**, the pronoun is the subject and must stay. If it is a **noun or pronoun** (*I*, *you*, *she*, *the teacher*), the pronoun is an object and may go.

| Sentence | Next word | Can the pronoun go? |
|---|---|---|
| The film that **she** made won a prize. | she (a noun/pronoun) | Yes |
| The film that **won** a prize was hers. | won (a verb) | No |
| The friend whom **I** trust lives abroad. | I | Yes |
| The friend who **trusts** me lives abroad. | trusts | No |

:::qc
q: In which sentence can the relative pronoun be left out?
options: ["The teacher who taught us last year has retired.", "The pen that I lost was a gift.", "The students who passed were happy."]
answer: 1
right: Right. In the second sentence the pronoun that is the object of lost (I lost the pen), so it can go: The pen I lost was a gift.
wrong: Check what follows the pronoun. In the first and third sentences a verb follows (taught, passed), so the pronoun is the subject and must stay. Only the pen sentence has a pronoun, I, after it.
:::

## Prepositions in relative clauses {lvl=2 id=s-prep label="Class 10"}

If the verb in the clause needs a preposition (*speak to*, *depend on*, *live in*), the preposition can go in two places.

1. **Informal: at the end of the clause.** *The girl **who** I spoke **to** is a singer.* (*That* or no pronoun also works.)
2. **Formal: just before the pronoun.** *The girl **to whom** I spoke is a singer.*

In the formal pattern, the pronoun must be **whom** (for people) or **which** (for things); *who* and *that* cannot follow a preposition.

- Right: *the house in which she lives*, *the friend with whom I travelled*, *the pen with which he wrote*.
- Wrong: *the friend with who I travelled*, *the house in that she lives*.

This formal pattern is a favourite in board "join the sentences" tasks and in the formal letters you write.

Whose also fits with a preposition: *the man **in whose** house we stayed* (we stayed in the man's house).

## Joining sentences with relative pronouns {lvl=2 id=s-join label="Class 10"}

The board asks you to **combine two sentences into one**, often with a hint (*Use who/which/whose*). Follow four steps.

1. Find the **noun in common**.
2. Decide whether the clause is a defining or a non-defining one (specific nouns get commas).
3. Pick the pronoun: person or thing, subject, object or belonging.
4. Put the clause **directly after its noun**. If the main sentence continues afterwards, close the clause with a comma if it is non-defining.

:::worked title="Four pairs, four pronouns"
(a) *I met a woman. She runs a bakery.* The common noun is *woman*, a person who does the running (subject). → *I met a woman who runs a bakery.*

(b) *The film was long. We watched it.* The common noun is *film*, a thing, and *we watched it* makes it the object. → *The film that we watched was long.* (Also correct: *The film we watched was long.*)

(c) *Priya is my classmate. Her father is a pilot.* The common noun is *Priya*, a name, so the clause is non-defining; her father belongs to her, so *whose*. → *Priya, whose father is a pilot, is my classmate.*

(d) *This is the village. My grandfather was born in this village.* The common noun is a place. → *This is the village where my grandfather was born.* (Or: *the village in which my grandfather was born.*)
:::

### Common errors in joining

- Repeating the noun: *The film that we watched it was long* is wrong. The pronoun already replaces *it*.
- Placing the clause far from its noun: *The man was shouting who lives next door* is wrong. Put it next to the noun: *The man who lives next door was shouting.*
- Using *which* for a person, or *who* for a thing.

## Relative clauses in the board paper {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

The paper tests this topic in three places.

1. **Sentence joining or "combine using"**, as above.
2. **Gap filling** with a choice of pronouns (*who/whom/whose/which/that*).
3. **Editing**, where one line has a wrong pronoun, a missing comma or a repeated noun.

A routine that works:

- Underline the noun just before the gap or the pronoun.
- Is it a person or thing? Choose the family (*who* or *which*).
- Check the next word. A verb means a subject pronoun; a noun or *I/we/you* means an object pronoun; a noun owned by the antecedent means *whose*.
- Check the commas: a name or a one-of-a-kind noun needs commas around the clause.

:::worked title="An editing line in thirty seconds"
Error spotting: *The watch, that my father gave me, has stopped working.*
The noun is *watch*, which is a specific watch (my father gave it to me), and the clause sits between commas. A non-defining clause cannot use *that*. Correction: *The watch, which my father gave me, has stopped working.*
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "The woman whom lives opposite is a lawyer."
options: ["whom should be who", "lives should be live", "opposite should be opposed"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The pronoun is the subject of lives (she lives opposite), so who is needed.
wrong: Apply the test: she lives opposite, not her lives opposite. The pronoun is the subject, so it must be who, not whom.
:::

## Beyond the board: finer points {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### That after certain words

Writers prefer **that** after words such as *all*, *everything*, *nothing*, *something*, *only*, *the same*, *the first*, and after superlatives.

- *Everything that glitters is not gold.*
- *It is the best film that I have seen.*
- *All that she wanted was a quiet room.*

*Which* is possible but sounds heavier here. After a mix of a person and a thing (*the boy and his dog that ran past*), *that* also works neatly because *who* and *which* would clash.

### Which that refers to a whole clause

Sometimes *which* does not point to one noun but to the entire idea before it. It always follows a comma.

- *He passed the test, **which** surprised everyone.* (What surprised everyone was the fact that he passed.)
- *The train was late, **which** made us miss the meeting.*

Be careful: such a sentence can be unclear if a noun is closer to *which* than the real idea. In *He sold his old car, which was a relief*, readers may think the car was a relief.

### Reduced relative clauses

A relative clause can be shortened by dropping the pronoun and a helper verb.

| Full | Reduced |
|---|---|
| The man **who is standing** at the gate is my uncle. | The man **standing** at the gate is my uncle. |
| The letters **that were written** in 1990 are in the box. | The letters **written** in 1990 are in the box. |
| Delhi, **which is the capital**, is crowded. | Delhi, **the capital**, is crowded. (an appositive) |

The reduced form is common in formal writing, and board writing tasks reward it as concise style. The test: you can reduce it only if the pronoun is the subject and the verb is a form of *be* or an active verb that gives a participle.

### Agreement inside the clause

The verb in the clause agrees with its **antecedent**, not with the nearest noun.

- *She is one of the students **who are** chosen.* (*Who* stands for *students*, so *are*.)
- *He is the only one of the students **who is** chosen.* (*Only one* is the real antecedent here, so *is*.) This pair is a classic trap, and usage varies. [Unverified: some usage guides accept either form in the first pattern.]
- With a first-person antecedent: *It is I who am responsible* is formal; many speakers say *It is I who is*. Stay with the formal form in exams.

### Whose with things

Some teachers insist *whose* is for people only. Standard usage allows it for things: *a house whose roof was damaged*. The alternative, *a house the roof of which was damaged*, is correct but clumsy. Both are safe in the board paper.

:::qc
q: "He lost his keys, ___ made him late for work." Which word fits?
options: ["who", "which", "that", "whom"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The pronoun refers to the whole fact that he lost his keys, and a comma plus which is the way to point at a whole clause.
wrong: The missing word points back to the whole idea (the losing of keys), not to one person. That job belongs to which after a comma. That cannot follow a comma.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A relative clause hangs a small sentence on a noun (the antecedent) using a relative pronoun that both replaces the noun and joins the clauses. {lvl=1}
2. Who and whom are for people, which for things and animals, whose for belonging; that can replace who or which in defining clauses. {lvl=1}
3. Who does the action in its clause (subject); whom receives it (object). Test: he fits who, him fits whom. {lvl=1}
4. Where, when and why join a clause to a noun of place, time or reason. {lvl=1}
5. A defining clause identifies the noun and takes no commas; a non-defining clause adds extra information between commas. {lvl=2}
6. That is used only in defining clauses, never after a comma. {lvl=2}
7. The pronoun may be dropped only if it is the object of its clause in a defining clause: look at what follows it. {lvl=2}
8. A preposition goes at the end (informal) or before the pronoun; after a preposition use whom or which, not who or that. {lvl=2}
9. To join sentences: find the common noun, choose the pronoun, place the clause right after the noun, never repeat the noun. {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: that after all, only and superlatives; comma plus which for a whole clause; reduced clauses with participles; the verb agrees with the antecedent. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Using which for people ("the girl which won"): use who. {lvl=1}
- Repeating the noun ("the film which we watched it"): the pronoun already replaces it. {lvl=1}
- Putting the clause far from its noun: attach it directly after the noun it describes. {lvl=1}
- Who when the object is meant ("the man who I met" in formal writing): use whom, or drop the pronoun. {lvl=2}
- That in a non-defining clause ("Delhi, that is big"): use which or who. {lvl=2}
- Missing a comma, or only one of the pair, around a non-defining clause. {lvl=2}
- Dropping a subject pronoun ("the book fell is mine"): only an object pronoun can be dropped. {lvl=2}
- Who or that after a preposition ("with who I travelled"): use whom or which. {lvl=2}
- Putting a comma before a defining clause, which changes the meaning to "all of them" or "only one". {lvl=2}
- Whose without a noun after it: whose is always followed by the thing owned. {lvl=2}
- Reading "which" after a comma as pointing at the nearest noun when it points at the whole idea. {lvl=3}
:::
