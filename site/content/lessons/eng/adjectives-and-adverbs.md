## Why English needs describing words {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

A noun names a thing and a verb names an action. On their own they say very little: *Dogs bark.* A reader still does not know which dogs, how many, how loudly or when. Describing words fill those gaps, and English splits the job in two so that the listener always knows what is being described.

- An **adjective** goes with a **noun** (or pronoun) and says what kind, which one or how many: *a **black** dog, **three** dogs, **that** dog.*
- An **adverb** goes with a **verb** (or with an adjective or another adverb) and says how, when, where or how much: *barks **loudly**, barks **often**, **very** loud.*

The split exists because the same quality needs a different form depending on what it is attached to. *A quick reply* describes a thing. *She replied quickly* describes an action. Using the wrong one makes the reader stumble, because the grammar points at the wrong target.

:::note title="One test that settles most cases" tone=tip
Find the word that is being described. Noun or pronoun: adjective. Verb, adjective or adverb: adverb. In *She sings beautiful*, the describing word is attached to the verb *sings*, so it must be the adverb *beautifully*.
:::

## Adjectives: the kinds {lvl=1 id=s-adj label="Foundation"}

Adjectives are sorted by the question they answer.

| Kind | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | What kind? | a **clever** girl, **cold** water, an **honest** man |
| Quantity | How much? | **some** milk, **enough** time, **little** hope |
| Number | How many? | **five** books, **many** friends, **every** child, the **second** floor |
| Demonstrative | Which one? | **this** book, **those** shoes |
| Interrogative | Which? What? Whose? | **Which** road? **Whose** bag? |
| Possessive | Whose? | **my** pen, **their** house |

Notice that the same word can change class with its job. In *This is mine*, *mine* stands alone as a pronoun. In *This is my pen*, *my* describes a noun, so it is an adjective. In *Which is yours?*, *which* is a pronoun, but in *Which road?* it is an adjective. Class depends on the work the word does in the sentence, not on the word.

### Where adjectives stand

- **Before the noun** (attributive): *a heavy bag.*
- **After a linking verb** (predicative): *The bag is heavy.* *She seems tired.* *The milk turned sour.*

Linking verbs (be, seem, become, look, feel, smell, taste, sound, grow, turn, stay) join the subject to a description of itself, so what follows them is an adjective.

- *The cake tastes sweet.* (describes the cake)
- *She tastes the cake carefully.* (here *tastes* is a real action, so an adverb describes it)

Compare: *The room looks clean* (the room's quality) and *He looked carefully at the map* (his action).

## Adjectives made from other words {lvl=1 id=s-form label="Foundation"}

Many adjectives are built from nouns and verbs by endings.

| Ending | From | Example |
|---|---|---|
| -ful | noun | care, **careful**; help, **helpful** |
| -less | noun | hope, **hopeless**; fear, **fearless** |
| -y | noun | rain, **rainy**; luck, **lucky** |
| -ous | noun | danger, **dangerous**; fame, **famous** |
| -able / -ible | verb | enjoy, **enjoyable**; reverse, **reversible** |
| -al | noun | nation, **national**; music, **musical** |
| -ive | verb | act, **active**; create, **creative** |

Two spelling tips: *-y* after a consonant becomes *-i* before an ending (*beauty, beautiful*), and silent *e* often drops (*fame, famous*; but *notice, noticeable* keeps it).

### -ed and -ing adjectives

Verbs also give two adjectives that are often mixed up.

- **-ing** describes the thing that causes the feeling: *a **boring** lecture, a **surprising** result.*
- **-ed** describes the person who has the feeling: *a **bored** student, a **surprised** teacher.*

*The film was **frightening**, so the children were **frightened**.* Wrong: *I am very boring in this class* (it means you are a dull person). Right: *I am bored.*

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["The match was very excited.", "The match was very exciting.", "The match was very excite."]
answer: 1
right: Right. The match causes the feeling, so the -ing adjective exciting is needed.
wrong: The match is the cause of the excitement, not the one who feels it. The cause takes -ing; the person feeling takes -ed.
:::

## Adverbs: the kinds and how they are made {lvl=1 id=s-adv label="Foundation"}

Adverbs are sorted by the question they answer.

| Kind | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Manner | How? | *quickly, carefully, well, loudly* |
| Time | When? | *now, yesterday, soon, already, then* |
| Frequency | How often? | *always, usually, often, seldom, never, twice* |
| Place | Where? | *here, there, outside, upstairs, everywhere* |
| Degree | How much? To what extent? | *very, quite, too, almost, rather, so* |
| Reason or purpose | Why? | *therefore, hence, so* |

### Forming adverbs of manner

Most adverbs of manner are made by adding **-ly** to the adjective.

| Adjective | Adverb | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| quick | quickly | add -ly |
| happy | happily | y becomes i, then -ly |
| gentle | gently | -le becomes -ly |
| true | truly | e drops |
| full | fully | -ll takes just -y |

Some adverbs have the same form as the adjective. Learn these as a group.

| Word | As an adjective | As an adverb |
|---|---|---|
| fast | a fast train | The train runs fast. |
| hard | a hard problem | She works hard. |
| early | an early bus | The bus came early. |
| late | a late reply | We arrived late. |
| daily | a daily paper | The paper comes daily. |

Not every word ending in -ly is an adverb. *Friendly, lovely, lonely, elderly, silly* are adjectives: *a friendly dog.* To make an adverb from one of these, use a phrase: *She spoke in a friendly way.*

### Adverbs of degree work on other describers

An adverb of degree sits before the word it strengthens or weakens: *very **tall**, quite **slowly**, almost **ready**, too **heavy**.* This is the part adjectives cannot do: adjectives never describe other adjectives, so *a **terrible** hot day* is wrong but *a **terribly** hot day* is right.

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["She is a real good singer.", "She is a really good singer.", "She is a real well singer."]
answer: 1
right: Right. Good is an adjective, so the word that strengthens it must be an adverb, really. Real is an adjective.
wrong: A word that describes the adjective good has to be an adverb. Use really, not real.
:::

## Comparison: three degrees {lvl=2 id=s-comp label="Class 10"}

We compare things to say that one has more of a quality. English uses three forms, called degrees.

| Degree | Used for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | one thing, or equal things | Mohan is **tall**. Mohan is **as tall as** Sohan. |
| Comparative | two things | Mohan is **taller than** Sohan. |
| Superlative | three or more things | Mohan is **the tallest** boy in the class. |

### Forming comparatives and superlatives

| Word | Comparative | Superlative | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| tall | taller | tallest | short words: add -er, -est |
| large | larger | largest | final e: add -r, -st |
| big | bigger | biggest | one short vowel then one consonant: double it |
| happy | happier | happiest | consonant + y: y becomes i |
| careful | more careful | most careful | long words: more, most |
| beautiful | more beautiful | most beautiful | long words: more, most |

A rule of thumb: one-syllable adjectives take *-er/-est*; adjectives of three or more syllables take *more/most*; two-syllable adjectives go either way (*clever, cleverer* or *more clever*), but those ending in *-y* take *-er* and those ending in *-ful, -ing, -ed* take *more*. Adverbs follow the same pattern: *fast, faster, fastest* but *carefully, more carefully, most carefully*.

### Irregular forms

These are very common and must be learned as they are.

| Positive | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| good / well | better | best |
| bad / badly / ill | worse | worst |
| much / many | more | most |
| little (amount) | less | least |
| far | farther / further | farthest / furthest |
| late | later (time) / latter (order) | latest (time) / last (order) |
| old | older / elder | oldest / eldest |

*Elder* and *eldest* are used for members of a family and only before a noun or after a linking verb without *than*: *my elder sister; she is the eldest.* For a comparison with *than*, use *older*: *She is older than me.*

### Rules the board likes to test

1. **Than goes with the comparative; the goes with the superlative.** *Delhi is bigger than Jaipur. Delhi is the biggest city in the north.* Wrong: *She is more smarter than him* (double comparative) and *the most tallest* (double superlative).
2. **Compare like with like.** *The climate of Shimla is cooler than **that** of Delhi* (not *than Delhi*, which would compare a climate to a city). Use *that of* for a singular noun and *those of* for plural.
3. **Exclude the thing from its own group.** To say a thing is the greatest in its group, compare it with *other* members: *Everest is higher than **any other** mountain in the world.* Wrong: *higher than any mountain in the world* (Everest is itself a mountain).
4. **Latin comparatives take *to*, not *than*.** Senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior, anterior, posterior and prefer / preferable: *He is senior to me. This brand is superior to that one. I prefer tea to coffee.* Wrong: *senior than*.
5. **Equal comparison uses as ... as.** *He is as tall as his brother.* In a negative: *not so tall as* or *not as tall as*.
6. **Parallel changes use the ... the.** *The more you practise, the better you play.*
7. **Two things, comparative; many things, superlative.** *Of the two sisters, Rina is the **taller**.* (Informal speech often says *tallest*; the exam key wants the comparative with "of the two".)

### Changing the degree without changing the meaning

Board papers ask you to rewrite a sentence in another degree. The three forms below are all equivalent.

- Superlative: *Everest is the highest peak in the world.*
- Comparative: *Everest is higher than any other peak in the world.*
- Positive: *No other peak in the world is as high as Everest.*

Also: *Very few peaks are as high as Everest.* is positive degree with a different shade of meaning (it allows a few).

:::worked title="Changing degree"
Rewrite in the comparative: *Tara is the most careful girl in her class.*

Step 1: The superlative says Tara has more care than every other girl. Step 2: Use the comparative with *than any other* and a singular noun. Answer: *Tara is more careful than any other girl in her class.*

Rewrite in the positive: *No other girl in her class is as careful as Tara.* The positive uses *no other ... as ... as*.
:::

:::qc
q: "Mumbai is larger than ___ city in India." Choose the best option.
options: ["any", "any other", "all other", "the other"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Mumbai is itself a city in India, so it must be compared with the others: any other city.
wrong: Mumbai belongs to the group of cities in India. If you say "any city" you compare it with itself. Exclude it with any other.
:::

## Order of adjectives {lvl=2 id=s-order label="Class 10"}

When two or more adjectives stand before a noun, native speakers follow an order without thinking. The order runs from the opinion of the speaker to the nature of the thing itself, from least permanent to most permanent.

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion | Size | Age | Shape | Colour | Origin | Material | Purpose |
| lovely | small | old | round | red | Indian | wooden | toy (box) |

*A lovely small old round red Indian wooden toy box* is an extreme example; in practice you use two or three.

- *a beautiful old house* (opinion, age)
- *two large brown dogs* (number, size, colour)
- *a thin silver chain* (size, material)
- *an expensive French leather bag* (opinion, origin, material)

Number words come first: *the first three days, my two younger brothers.* Between adjectives of the same kind, *and* is used after a linking verb: *The sky was blue and clear.*

:::note title="Why the order feels right" tone=tip
The adjective closest to the noun tells you what the thing is made of or for, a fixed part of the thing. The adjectives farthest from the noun are the speaker's opinion, which is the most changeable. You do not need to memorise a list for the exam; say the phrase aloud and a wrong order will usually sound odd.
:::

## Position of adverbs {lvl=2 id=s-pos label="Class 10"}

Adjectives are easy to place. Adverbs move, and each type has a usual home.

| Type | Usual position | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Manner | after the verb, or after the object | *She walks slowly. She closed the door gently.* |
| Place | after the verb or object | *They live nearby. He put the bag there.* |
| Time | at the end (or at the start for emphasis) | *We will meet tomorrow. Yesterday it rained.* |
| Frequency | before the main verb, after be | *He often reads. She is always late.* |
| Degree | before the word modified | *very cold, quite well, almost done* |

### Details to remember

- **Not between a verb and its object.** Wrong: *She speaks fluently English.* Right: *She speaks English fluently.*
- **Frequency adverbs and auxiliary verbs:** between the auxiliary and the main verb: *I have never seen it. She does not usually eat late.*
- **Order when more than one is at the end:** manner, place, time. *She sang beautifully in the hall yesterday.*
- **Only** and **even** go directly before the word they limit. *Only Ravi won the prize* (nobody else) and *Ravi won only the first prize* (nothing more) differ in meaning.

### Too, enough and very

| Word | Meaning | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| very | a high degree, no result implied | *The tea is very hot, but I can drink it.* |
| too | more than is wanted or possible; a negative result | *The tea is too hot to drink.* (too + adjective + to + verb) |
| enough | as much as needed | *The tea is cool enough to drink.* (adjective + enough; enough + noun) |

*Enough* stands **after** an adjective or adverb (*tall enough*) but **before** a noun (*enough water*). *Too* is not used for a positive meaning: wrong *I am too glad to see you*; right *I am very glad.* Wrong: *too much* + adjective as in *too much cold*; right: *too cold*.

## Pairs that trip up students {lvl=2 id=s-pairs label="Class 10"}

### Good and well; bad and badly

*Good* and *bad* are adjectives; *well* and *badly* are adverbs.

- *She is a good dancer. She dances well.*
- *The news was bad. He behaved badly.*

*Well* can also be an adjective meaning healthy: *I am well, thank you* (not *I am good*, which, in strict grammar, describes character, not health). *Feel bad* means to feel unwell or sorry; *feel badly* is used in speech but the exam key prefers *feel bad* after a linking verb.

### Hard and hardly; late and lately; near and nearly

These look related but are not.

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| hard (adv.) | with great effort | She studied **hard**. |
| hardly | almost not | She **hardly** studied. (She studied almost nothing.) |
| late (adv.) | after the proper time | He came **late**. |
| lately | recently | I have not seen him **lately**. |
| near (adv.) | close | Come **near**. |
| nearly | almost | I **nearly** fell. |
| high (adv.) | far up | The kite flew **high**. |
| highly | very much | She is **highly** respected. |

### Other common pairs

- **Few / a few / the few; little / a little / the little.** *Few* and *little* mean almost none (negative). *A few* and *a little* mean some (positive). *Few* goes with countable nouns (*few friends*), *little* with uncountable nouns (*little water*). *I have a few books and a little time, so I can help.*
- **Much and many.** *Many* with countable nouns (*many books*), *much* with uncountable (*much water*). In positive sentences use *a lot of* or *plenty of*: *There is a lot of water.*
- **Less and fewer.** *Less* with uncountable (*less sugar*), *fewer* with countable (*fewer sweets*).
- **So and such.** *So* goes with an adjective or adverb: *so tired.* *Such* goes with a noun (with or without an adjective): *such a tiring day.*
- **Either, neither** (two things) and **any, none** (more than two).

:::qc
q: "She was ___ tired that she could ___ keep her eyes open."
options: ["so, hardly", "such, hard", "so, hard", "very, hardly"]
answer: 0
right: Right. So + adjective + that is the pattern, and hardly means almost not: she could almost not keep her eyes open.
wrong: Use so before an adjective in the so ... that pattern. Hard is an adverb meaning with effort; here we need hardly (almost not).
:::

## Board questions on adjectives and adverbs {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

The board tests this leaf in three forms.

1. **Gap-filling with options.** Often a pair such as *good/well*, *hard/hardly*, *few/a few*, or a comparative form with *than/to*.
2. **Editing or error correction.** Typical lines: *She sings very good* (well); *He is the most intelligent of the two* (more intelligent); *I prefer coffee than tea* (to); *Rohan is more taller than Sohan*.
3. **Transformation.** Change the degree (positive, comparative, superlative), or combine with *too ... to* and *so ... that*.

A reliable routine for an error-correction line:

- Underline every describing word.
- For each, name what it describes.
- Check the form (adjective or adverb) against that.
- If it is a comparison, check *than/to*, double comparatives and the *any other* rule.

:::worked title="Transformation with too ... to and so ... that"
Rewrite using *too ... to*: *The box is so heavy that I cannot lift it.*

Step 1: Find the quality (heavy) and the result (cannot lift). Step 2: Put the quality after *too* and the result after *to*. Answer: *The box is too heavy for me to lift.* (No *not*: *too* already carries the negative result.)

Reverse: *She is too weak to walk* becomes *She is so weak that she cannot walk.*
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "He is the more intelligent of all the students."
options: ["more should be most", "of should be in", "the should be a", "no error"]
answer: 0
right: Right. More than two students are being compared, so the superlative most intelligent is needed.
wrong: The sentence compares one boy with all students (many). Many things need the superlative.
:::

## Beyond the board: finer points {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### Absolute and non-gradable adjectives

Some adjectives already name an extreme or a yes/no quality: *unique, perfect, dead, empty, impossible, entire.* Careful writers do not compare them: *the most unique idea* is questioned because something is either unique or not. [Inference] Many usage guides accept *more nearly perfect* for the idea of getting close. In exam answers, avoid modifying them with *very* or *more*.

### Adverbs that modify whole sentences

Some adverbs describe the speaker's attitude to the whole sentence, not one verb: *Fortunately, nobody was hurt. Surprisingly, she refused.* These sentence adverbs usually stand at the start and are set off by a comma. Compare the meaning of *clearly*: *He spoke clearly* (manner) with *Clearly, he was wrong* (it is obvious).

### Adverb changing the meaning by position

Position can change what a sentence says. *She only said that she liked it* (she merely said it); *She said that she only liked it* (she did not love it); *Only she said that she liked it* (no one else did). The rule: put *only* directly before the word it limits.

### Adjectives with fixed prepositions and participles

Many adjectives take a fixed preposition: *good at, afraid of, interested in, fond of, aware of, keen on, different from, jealous of.* Past and present participles used as adjectives follow the -ed/-ing rule shown earlier. Compound adjectives join with a hyphen and keep the first part singular: *a ten-year-old boy, a five-rupee note* (but *The boy is ten years old*).

### Flat adverbs and style

Words like *slow, quick, loud* are used as adverbs in speech and signs (*Drive slow, Don't talk so loud*). [Unverified] Whether a given dictionary or board key accepts them in formal answers varies; in an exam, use *slowly, quickly, loudly*.

### Adjective or adverb after "look", "sound", "feel"

*She looked angry* (appeared angry) versus *She looked angrily at him* (the manner of her looking). The linking use gets the adjective; the action use gets the adverb.

:::qc
q: Which is the better sentence for a formal answer?
options: ["Rohan is more unique than Sohan.", "Rohan's idea is unique.", "Rohan's idea is very unique."]
answer: 1
right: Right. Unique already means one of a kind, so it is not usually compared or intensified in formal writing.
wrong: Unique names an extreme quality. Formal writing avoids more, very or most with it.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. An adjective describes a noun or pronoun; an adverb describes a verb, an adjective or another adverb. {lvl=1}
2. After linking verbs (be, seem, look, feel, smell, taste, sound, become) use an adjective: "The soup smells delicious." {lvl=1}
3. -ing adjectives describe the cause of a feeling (boring); -ed adjectives describe the person who feels it (bored). {lvl=1}
4. Most adverbs of manner add -ly, but fast, hard, early, late and daily have the same form as the adjective, and friendly, lovely and lonely are adjectives. {lvl=1}
5. Three degrees: positive, comparative (two things, than), superlative (three or more, the). {lvl=2}
6. Short words take -er/-est, long words take more/most; never use both: not "more taller". {lvl=2}
7. Compare like with like (that of / those of) and exclude the subject with "any other". {lvl=2}
8. Senior, junior, superior, inferior and prefer take to, not than. {lvl=2}
9. Adjective order: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose. {lvl=2}
10. Adverbs of manner, place and time follow the verb or object, in that order; frequency adverbs go before the main verb and after be. {lvl=2}
11. Good/well, bad/badly, hard/hardly, late/lately, few/a few, little/a little differ in form or meaning: check each. {lvl=2}
12. Too gives a negative result (too hot to drink); enough follows an adjective but precedes a noun. {lvl=2}
13. Sentence adverbs (fortunately, clearly) comment on the whole sentence; only goes directly before the word it limits. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "She sings beautiful", "He runs quick": an adverb is needed to describe a verb. {lvl=1}
- "The soup tastes deliciously": after a linking verb use an adjective: delicious. {lvl=1}
- "I am boring", when you mean you feel bored: -ed for the person who feels. {lvl=1}
- "A terrible hot day", "a real good singer": an adverb must modify an adjective (terribly, really). {lvl=1}
- Assuming every -ly word is an adverb: friendly, lovely and lonely are adjectives. {lvl=1}
- "More taller", "most tallest": a double comparative or superlative. {lvl=2}
- "Senior than", "prefer coffee than tea": use to. {lvl=2}
- "Mumbai is larger than any city in India": exclude the subject with any other. {lvl=2}
- "The climate of Shimla is cooler than Delhi": compare like with like (than that of Delhi). {lvl=2}
- "The elder of the two" is fine, but "elder than me" is wrong: use older. {lvl=2}
- "I am too glad to see you": too carries a negative result, so use very. And "I studied hardly" when you mean with effort: hardly means almost not, so use hard. {lvl=2}
- Putting an adverb between the verb and its object: "speaks fluently English" should be "speaks English fluently". {lvl=2}
- "Few friends" when you mean some: a few is positive, few is negative. {lvl=2}
- "The most unique", "very perfect": absolute adjectives are not compared in formal writing. {lvl=3}
