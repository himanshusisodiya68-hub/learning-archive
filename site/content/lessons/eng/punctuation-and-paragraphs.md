## Why we need marks at all {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

When you talk, your voice does a great deal of unseen work. It drops at the end of a statement, rises for a question, pauses between ideas and shouts for surprise. Written words arrive without any of it. **Punctuation** is the set of small signs that put that voice back. Each mark answers one question for the reader: should I stop, pause, expect a question, or hear someone speaking?

| Mark | Name | Its main job |
|---|---|---|
| . | full stop | the sentence is finished |
| ? | question mark | the sentence asks something |
| ! | exclamation mark | strong feeling or a command with force |
| , | comma | a short pause or separation inside a sentence |
| ; | semicolon | a longer pause between two closely linked clauses |
| : | colon | "here comes the explanation or the list" |
| ' | apostrophe | a missing letter or ownership |
| " " | quotation marks | the exact words someone said |
| - | hyphen | joins parts of a word (well-known) |
| – | dash | a sudden turn or an extra remark |

(Brackets, ellipsis and others come later.) The rest of the lesson takes these marks in order of how often a Class 10 writer needs them.

## Capitals, full stops and the two other enders {lvl=1 id=s-ends label="Foundation"}

Every sentence has two borders: it begins with a **capital letter** and ends with one of three marks.

- **Full stop** after a statement or a polite command: *The bus is late. Please sit down.*
- **Question mark** after a direct question: *Where is the library?* An indirect question is a statement and takes a full stop: *She asked where the library was.*
- **Exclamation mark** after a real exclamation or a sharp command: *What a catch! Stop!* Use it sparingly. If every sentence shouts, none is heard.

Capital letters are also needed for: the word *I*; names of people, places and months; days of the week; titles used with a name (*Dr Rao*, *Prime Minister Nehru*); the main words of a title (*Gulliver's Travels*); and the first word of a quotation that is a sentence. Seasons (*summer*) and general directions (*go north*) do not take capitals, though a named region such as *the North-East* does.

Notice that the "stop" marks are decided by meaning, not by length. A short sentence like *Run!* is complete; a long group of words with no verb may not be.

:::qc
q: Which sentence is correctly punctuated?
options: ["She asked me where I was going?", "She asked me where I was going.", "she asked me where I was going."]
answer: 1
right: Right. This is an indirect question, reported inside a statement, so it ends with a full stop and starts with a capital.
wrong: The question is reported, not asked directly, so no question mark is needed. The sentence also needs a capital S.
:::

## The comma: five jobs {lvl=1 id=s-comma label="Foundation"}

The comma is the busiest mark, so it helps to learn its five regular jobs rather than guess.

1. **Separating items in a list.** *We bought mangoes, guavas, bananas and grapes.* The last two items are joined by *and*, and a comma before *and* is optional (more on this in the last section).
2. **After an opening word or phrase.** *However, the match went ahead. After the rain stopped, we played.* The comma tells the reader the main part of the sentence is about to start.
3. **Around extra information.** *My cousin, who lives in Madurai, visited us.* The commas mark a remark that could be removed without breaking the sentence (see the Relative clauses lesson).
4. **Before a joining word between two full clauses.** *The bus was late, but we reached on time.* Each side can stand alone; the comma plus *and, but, or, so, yet* does the join.
5. **Addressing a person or marking the speaker.** *Come here, Raju.* *Yes, sir.* *Thank you, Meera.*

A good test: read the sentence aloud and listen for a natural short pause. If the pause is real and a rule applies, add a comma. If you are adding a comma only because you took a breath, leave it out.

:::note title="The comma that does not belong" tone=warn
Do not put a comma between a subject and its verb, or between a verb and its object: *The tall boy, won the race* is wrong. Do not put a comma before *that* when it begins a clause that completes a verb: *He said, that he was ill* is wrong.
:::

:::qc
q: Where does the comma go? "After the final bell the students rushed out"
options: ["After the final bell, the students rushed out", "After the final bell the students, rushed out", "After, the final bell the students rushed out"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The comma closes the opening phrase "After the final bell" before the main clause starts.
wrong: The comma belongs where the opening phrase ends and the main clause starts, never between the subject (the students) and its verb.
:::

## The apostrophe {lvl=1 id=s-apos label="Foundation"}

The apostrophe has only two jobs, and neither is to make a plural.

**Contraction** shows that letters are missing: *don't* (do not), *it's* (it is), *we've* (we have), *o'clock*.

**Ownership** shows who owns something.

| The owner is | Add | Example |
|---|---|---|
| one person or thing | 's | the girl's bag, Asha's pen, the dog's tail |
| a plural ending in s | ' only | the girls' hostel, the teachers' room |
| a plural not ending in s | 's | the children's park, men's wear |
| a name ending in s | 's (or ' only; be consistent) | James's book, Charles' book |

**Its and it's** trip up many readers. *It's* always means *it is* or *it has*. *Its* (no apostrophe) means *belonging to it*, like *his* and *hers*, none of which take an apostrophe. Test: say "it is" in the gap. If it fits, use *it's*.

- *It's raining.* (It is raining.)
- *The cat licked its paw.* (the paw of the cat)

**No apostrophe for ordinary plurals.** *Two mangoes*, not *two mango's*. Plural of numbers and abbreviations usually add just s: *the 1990s*, *three DVDs*. Possessive pronouns (*yours, ours, theirs, whose*) also take none.

:::qc
q: "The ___ playground was flooded." (a playground belonging to many boys)
options: ["boys'", "boy's", "boys", "boys's"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The owners are many boys, so the apostrophe follows the plural s.
wrong: Many owners means a plural noun, and a plural ending in s takes only an apostrophe after the s.
:::

## Direct speech and its marks {lvl=2 id=s-speech label="Class 10"}

**Direct speech** repeats a speaker's exact words. It is written inside **quotation marks** (also called inverted commas). Indian and British writing often uses single marks ('...'), American writing double ("..."); either is correct, but choose one and keep it for the whole answer. This lesson uses double marks.

The rules are few.

1. Only the spoken words go inside the marks.
2. The first spoken word has a capital letter if it begins a sentence.
3. The final comma, full stop, question mark or exclamation mark of the speech goes **inside** the closing quotation mark.
4. If the speaker is named first, put a comma before the opening mark: *Meena said, "I am ready."*
5. If the speaker is named after the speech, the speech ends with a comma (not a full stop) unless it ends in ? or !: *"I am ready," said Meena.* *"Are you ready?" asked Meena.*
6. If the speaker's name interrupts the speech, use two pairs of marks and commas: *"I am," said Meena, "quite ready."*
7. **Start a new paragraph each time the speaker changes.** That is what makes a dialogue easy to follow without repeated "he said".

Example of a short dialogue:

> "Where are you going?" asked Karan.
>
> "To the library," said Tara. "Will you come?"
>
> "Not today. I have football."

Three speakers' turns, three lines. A reader can tell who is speaking from the paragraph breaks alone.

Direct speech and reported speech express the same idea in two forms; the changes between them are in the Reported speech lesson. Here, only the marks matter.

:::qc
q: Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
options: ["\"Come early\", said Dad.", "\"Come early,\" said Dad.", "\"Come early\" said Dad."]
answer: 1
right: Right. The comma that ends the speech goes inside the closing quotation mark, before "said Dad".
wrong: When the speaker is named after the speech, the speech ends with a comma placed inside the quotation marks.
:::

## Semicolon, colon, dash, hyphen and brackets {lvl=2 id=s-more label="Class 10"}

These marks carry more weight than a comma. Each has a clear job.

| Mark | Use it to | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ; semicolon | join two complete clauses that are closely linked in meaning; or to separate items in a list when the items already contain commas | *The train was late; we missed the first lecture.* / *Guests came from Pune, Maharashtra; Indore, Madhya Pradesh; and Kochi, Kerala.* |
| : colon | introduce a list, an explanation or a quotation after a complete clause | *Bring three things: a pen, a ruler and a smile.* |
| – dash | add a sudden turn, an afterthought or a strong extra remark | *He opened the box – and gasped.* |
| - hyphen | join the parts of a compound word | *well-known author, mother-in-law, twenty-one* |
| ( ) brackets | give extra information that can be dropped | *The Taj Mahal (in Agra) is white marble.* |

Two checks help.

- **Semicolon test:** put a full stop in its place. If both halves are complete sentences, a semicolon is allowed. If one half is not, use a comma or nothing. Do not write a semicolon before a joining word such as *and* or *but*; use a comma there.
- **Colon test:** what comes before a colon must be a complete statement. *My favourite fruits are: mangoes and guavas* is wrong; *My favourite fruits are mangoes and guavas* or *I have two favourite fruits: mangoes and guavas* is right.

A hyphen is different from a dash: the hyphen is short and joins, the dash is longer and separates. In handwriting, a plain short dash is acceptable, but do not use it as a replacement for every other mark.

:::qc
q: Which sentence uses the semicolon correctly?
options: ["The match was cancelled; because of rain.", "The match was cancelled; it had rained all night.", "The match; was cancelled."]
answer: 1
right: Right. Both halves are complete sentences with a close link in meaning.
wrong: The part after the semicolon must be a complete clause. "Because of rain" is a fragment, and a semicolon never splits a subject from its verb.
:::

## Run-ons, comma splices and fragments {lvl=2 id=s-faults label="Class 10"}

Most punctuation mistakes in answers are not wrong marks but wrong sentence borders. There are three.

**Comma splice**: two complete sentences joined by only a comma. *The sun set, we went home.* Repairs:

| Repair | Result |
|---|---|
| full stop and a capital | The sun set. We went home. |
| comma + joining word | The sun set, so we went home. |
| semicolon | The sun set; we went home. |
| subordinate word | When the sun set, we went home. |

**Run-on sentence** (or fused sentence): two complete sentences with no mark at all. *The sun set we went home.* Fix it with the same four repairs.

**Fragment**: a group of words punctuated as a sentence but lacking a subject or a finite verb, or hanging on a joining word. *Because it was raining.* The reader waits for the rest. Join it to its main clause: *We stayed in because it was raining.*

Fragments are useful in speech and advertising (*Hot. Fresh. Now.*) and in some creative writing. In a formal letter or article for the board, use complete sentences.

Linking words such as *however, therefore, moreover* are not conjunctions, so a comma before them does not join two sentences. *It rained, however we went* is a splice. Write *It rained; however, we went.* or *It rained. However, we went.* (See the Linkers and connectors lesson.)

:::qc
q: Which one fixes the comma splice "The shop was closed, we waited outside"?
options: ["The shop was closed, so we waited outside.", "The shop was closed we waited outside.", "The shop was closed, we, waited outside."]
answer: 0
right: Right. A comma plus the joining word "so" correctly links the two clauses.
wrong: The second option has no mark at all (a run-on) and the third adds a stray comma. A joining word such as so, and, but or a semicolon repairs the splice.
:::

## The paragraph: one idea in one block {lvl=2 id=s-para label="Class 10"}

If punctuation marks the borders of sentences, the **paragraph** marks the borders of ideas. A page that is one unbroken block tires the reader before they begin; a page of single lines scatters the ideas. A paragraph sits in between: **one main idea, developed in a few sentences, in one block.**

A good paragraph has three working parts.

| Part | What it does | Example (paragraph on school libraries) |
|---|---|---|
| Topic sentence | names the one idea, usually first | A school library does more than store books. |
| Supporting sentences | give reasons, examples, numbers or detail | It gives students a quiet place to study, lets them borrow books they cannot afford, and teaches them to search for information. |
| Closing sentence | rounds off or leads to the next paragraph | A school with a good library is a school that teaches its students to keep learning. |

Three qualities make it work.

1. **Unity.** Every sentence serves the topic sentence. If a sentence is about something else, cut it or give it its own paragraph.
2. **Order.** Choose a plan: general to specific, in time order, in order of importance, or problem to solution.
3. **Coherence.** The sentences are linked, so each one grows out of the one before it. Use linkers (*first, in addition, as a result, however*), pronouns that point back (*this, they, such a place*), and repeated key words.

**Where to start a new paragraph.** When the idea changes, when the speaker changes, when time or place changes, or when a long paragraph naturally divides into two points. In answers, use a blank line or an indent consistently, not both mixed.

**Paragraph length.** There is no fixed length, but for board writing of about 100 to 120 words, a paragraph is usually three to six sentences. One short paragraph can be used for emphasis.

:::qc
q: In a paragraph on the benefits of walking, which sentence should be cut for unity?
options: ["Walking strengthens the heart and bones.", "Cricket was first played in England.", "It also clears the mind after a long day of study."]
answer: 1
right: Right. The cricket sentence does not support the topic sentence about walking.
wrong: Check each sentence against the topic. Two of them give a benefit of walking; the cricket sentence changes the subject.
:::

## Joining paragraphs and ordering sentences {lvl=2 id=s-order label="Class 10"}

When you must put jumbled sentences into a paragraph, or write one yourself, the same structure shows up in clues.

| Clue | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| a general statement with no pronoun that points back | probably the topic sentence, so it comes first |
| a sentence beginning with *This*, *These*, *Such*, *He/She/They* | follows the sentence that names the person or thing |
| a linker such as *However*, *As a result*, *Finally* | comes after the idea it contrasts with, follows from, or closes |
| a sentence that sums up or looks ahead | probably last |
| a sentence beginning with an article (*the*) when the noun was introduced earlier with *a* | later than the sentence with *a* |
| a number or time order (*first, then, later, in 1950*) | arrange in that order |

Method: find the opener, then the closer, then chain the middle by pronouns and linkers. Read the finished paragraph once for sense.

Between paragraphs, a bridge helps: the last sentence of one paragraph or the first sentence of the next can carry a linker or a repeated word (*Another reason is...*, *Besides these benefits...*). Do not use bridging words so heavily that the writing sounds mechanical.

:::qc
q: Which sentence best opens this set? (a) They also sell pencils and notebooks. (b) Our school has a small shop near the gate. (c) It opens at eight each morning.
options: ["(a)", "(b)", "(c)"]
answer: 1
right: Right. (b) introduces the shop with no pronoun that points back. (a) and (c) rely on "they" and "it", which need (b) first.
wrong: Look for the sentence that names the thing without a pronoun pointing back. Sentences (a) and (c) say "they" and "it", so something must come before them.
:::

## Punctuation and paragraphs in the board paper {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

Punctuation and paragraphing are not usually a separate question in the paper; they are marked inside other tasks. [Unverified: check the latest CBSE sample paper for the exact scheme.]

| Where | What is checked |
|---|---|
| Writing section (letter, article, analytical paragraph) | accuracy of spelling, grammar and punctuation, and paragraph structure; most schemes award separate marks for content, expression and accuracy |
| Editing (error correction in a passage) | missing or wrong capital letters, a comma splice, an apostrophe in a wrong place; usually one error per line |
| Reordering or jumbled sentences | the paragraph structure clues from the previous section |
| Literature answers (long answers) | clear paragraphs with a topic sentence |
| Reported speech | the marks of direct speech must be removed in the reported form |

A quick routine for any written answer:

1. **Plan the paragraphs** before writing: one line of notes per paragraph.
2. **Write the topic sentence first** in each paragraph.
3. **Check each sentence's borders**: capital at the start, mark at the end, no comma splice.
4. **Check quotation marks and apostrophes** last, because they are easy to forget.
5. **Count words** against the limit; a 100 to 120 word task fits about three paragraphs.

:::worked title="Editing a four-line passage"
Passage: *the school library open at nine, it has over five thousand books. Students can borrow two books, for a week. Its a quiet place; where everyone work.*

Corrections: **The** school library **opens** at nine**, and** it has over five thousand books. (a capital at the start, the verb agrees with *library*, and the comma splice is repaired with *and*.) Students can borrow two books for a week. (the comma before the time phrase is removed.) **It's** a quiet place where everyone **works**. (apostrophe for *it is*, the semicolon is removed because a clause that describes the place needs no break before it, and the verb agrees with *everyone*.)

Notice that most of the repairs are punctuation, and two need a verb change as well.
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "The children's, books were on the table."
options: ["children's should be childrens'", "Remove the comma after children's", "books should be book's"]
answer: 1
right: Right. A comma never separates a possessive noun from the noun it owns. The apostrophe in children's is already correct.
wrong: "Children's" is correct (a plural not ending in s takes 's). The only fault is the stray comma between the owner and what is owned.
:::

## Beyond the board: punctuation as style {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

Once the rules are secure, punctuation becomes a tool for voice.

### The serial (Oxford) comma

In a list of three or more, a comma before the final *and* is the serial comma. Many British and Indian writers leave it out, many American writers include it. It can remove doubt: *I thank my parents, the principal, and the Prime Minister* lists three parties, while without the last comma it could be read as parents who are the principal and the Prime Minister. Pick one policy, and break it only to avoid confusion.

### Rhythm and speed

- A long sentence with commas slows down and gathers detail.
- A short sentence after it hits hard. Like this.
- A **dash** can mimic a sudden thought; a **colon** builds anticipation (*There was only one thing left to do: run*).
- An **ellipsis** (...) shows hesitation or something unsaid, and fits dialogue, rarely formal writing.

### Fragments by choice

Professional writers use a fragment for effect when the meaning is still clear: *The monsoon at last. Grey sky, wet roads, the smell of earth.* The skill is knowing the rule well enough to break it on purpose. In board answers, play safe.

### Paragraph shape as emphasis

A one-sentence paragraph stands out, so use it for a turn or a punch line. A long paragraph builds a sustained argument. Vary them. If every paragraph has the same length, the reader senses a formula.

### Two spellings of the same convention

British and American practice differ in small ways: single vs double quotation marks, *full stop* vs *period*, and *Dr Rao* vs *Dr. Rao*. Either is acceptable in the board paper if used consistently.

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. Punctuation replaces the voice in writing: it shows stops, pauses, questions and speech. {lvl=1}
2. Begin each sentence with a capital letter and end it with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark; an indirect question ends with a full stop. {lvl=1}
3. The comma has five regular jobs: list, after an opening phrase, around extra information, before a joining word between two clauses, and addressing someone. {lvl=1}
4. The apostrophe is for contraction and ownership only: girl's, girls', children's. Its (belonging) and it's (it is) are different. {lvl=1}
5. In direct speech, the speech goes inside quotation marks, its final mark sits inside the closing mark, and a new speaker means a new paragraph. {lvl=2}
6. A semicolon joins two complete, closely linked clauses; a colon follows a complete statement and introduces a list or explanation. {lvl=2}
7. A comma splice, a run-on and a fragment are errors of sentence borders; fix them with a full stop, a comma plus a joining word, a semicolon or a subordinate word. {lvl=2}
8. A paragraph holds one idea: topic sentence, supporting sentences, closing sentence; keep it unified, ordered and coherent. {lvl=2}
9. To order jumbled sentences, find the opener (no back-pointing pronoun), then the closer, then chain the middle with pronouns and linkers. {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board, punctuation shapes rhythm: the serial comma, dashes, fragments by choice and one-line paragraphs are tools, used on purpose. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Forgetting the capital letter after a full stop, or for I, names and months. {lvl=1}
- A question mark after an indirect question: "She asked where I lived?" should end with a full stop. {lvl=1}
- Apostrophe in a plain plural: "mango's for sale" should be "mangoes for sale". {lvl=1}
- Mixing up its and it's: use it's only when you can say it is or it has. {lvl=1}
- A comma between the subject and the verb: "The boy, ran home" has no reason for the comma. {lvl=1}
- Comma splice: "It rained, we stayed in." Use a full stop, a semicolon or a joining word. {lvl=2}
- Quotation marks closing before the final comma or full stop of the speech: the mark goes inside. {lvl=2}
- A colon after an incomplete clause: "My friends are: Asha and Ravi." {lvl=2}
- A semicolon before a fragment: both sides must be complete clauses. {lvl=2}
- One long block of text with several ideas, or a paragraph break after every sentence. {lvl=2}
- Putting a sentence on another topic inside a paragraph: it breaks unity. {lvl=2}
- Overusing exclamation marks and dashes in formal writing, which dulls their effect. {lvl=3}
:::
