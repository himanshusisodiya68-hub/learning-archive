options: ["blunt", "dull", "soft"]
answer: 1
right: Right. A sharp mind is quick, so its opposite is dull. Blunt belongs to a knife or an edge, and soft belongs to touch or sound.
wrong: Think about what the sentence says. A sharp mind is quick and clever, so we need a word for slow and unclever. The opposite depends on the sense, and for a mind that word is dull.

## What an opposite really is {lvl=1 id=s-what label="Foundation"}

An **antonym** is a word whose meaning is opposite to another word's. Hot and cold, rise and fall, brave and cowardly.

Notice the small phrase in the middle: *in a given sense*. A word is rarely opposed to another word for every use. "Cold" is the opposite of "hot" for tea, but the opposite of "warm" for a welcome, and the opposite of "friendly" for a manner. Opposites live inside senses, not inside spellings.

Two quick rules hold from the start.

1. **Same kind of word.** An adjective needs an adjective (*tall, short*), a verb needs a verb (*arrive, depart*), a noun needs a noun (*victory, defeat*).
2. **Not every word has one.** What is the opposite of *table*, or *banana*, or *run* in "run a shop"? Nothing neat. Words for plain objects usually have no antonym, so do not force one.

## Three kinds of opposites {lvl=1 id=s-kinds label="Foundation"}

If you ask "is something neither of the two?", opposites split into three kinds.

| Kind | How it behaves | Examples | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gradable** | Two ends of a scale; there are middle points | hot / cold (warm, cool), big / small, rich / poor, early / late | "Not hot" does not mean cold |
| **Complementary** | One or the other; nothing in between | alive / dead, true / false, present / absent, odd / even | "Not alive" means dead |
| **Relational** (converse) | Each word needs the other to make sense | buy / sell, teacher / student, parent / child, above / below | One cannot happen without the other |

Look at how each one is felt. If a bucket of water is not hot, you cannot say it is cold; it might be lukewarm. But if a number is not odd, it is even. And a shopkeeper cannot sell unless someone buys.

Gradable opposites can also be compared: *hotter, coldest, very small, rather poor.* Complementary ones cannot, at least in careful English: *a more dead fish* sounds wrong.

:::qc
q: Which pair is a complementary pair, with nothing possible in between?
options: ["hot and cold", "alive and dead", "buy and sell", "big and small"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Something is either alive or dead. Hot and cold have warm and cool between them, big and small have a middle size, and buy and sell are relational.
wrong: Test each pair with "not". Not hot may still be warm, and not big may still be medium. Not alive, however, means dead. Buy and sell need each other, which makes them relational.
:::

## Making opposites with prefixes {lvl=1 id=s-prefix label="Foundation"}

Often you do not need to learn a new word at all. A **prefix** (letters added at the front) can flip a word. The common negative prefixes are below.

| Prefix | Use | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **un-** | most adjectives, many verbs | happy / unhappy, kind / unkind, tie / untie |
| **in-** | words starting with most letters | visible / invisible, active / inactive, correct / incorrect |
| **im-** | before p, b and m | possible / impossible, balance / imbalance, mature / immature |
| **il-** | before l | legal / illegal, logical / illogical, literate / illiterate |
| **ir-** | before r | regular / irregular, responsible / irresponsible, relevant / irrelevant |
| **dis-** | verbs and adjectives | agree / disagree, honest / dishonest, appear / disappear |
| **non-** | nouns and adjectives | fiction / non-fiction, stop / non-stop |
| **a-** / **an-** | technical words | moral / amoral, symmetrical / asymmetrical |
| **anti-** | against | clockwise / anticlockwise, social / antisocial |
| **de-** | undoing or removing | code / decode, motivate / demotivate |
| **mal-** / **mis-** | bad or wrong | nutrition / malnutrition, understand / misunderstand |

Why do im-, il- and ir- exist when in- would do? It is about ease of speaking: saying "inpossible" makes your lips jump, so English lets the n slide into the sound that follows. A handy rule is to say the word aloud and let your mouth choose.

:::note title="Prefix check" tone=tip
Before you add a prefix, glance at the first letter of the word. **p, b, m** need **im-**. **l** needs **il-**. **r** needs **ir-**. Anything else usually takes **un-**, **in-** or **dis-**, and the dictionary has the final say.
:::

:::qc
q: Which is the correct opposite of "responsible"?
options: ["unresponsible", "inresponsible", "irresponsible", "disresponsible"]
answer: 2
right: Right. Responsible starts with r, so the prefix is ir-: irresponsible.
wrong: Look at the first letter of the base word. It is r, and the prefix before r is ir-.
:::

## Opposites with -ful, -less and a new word {lvl=1 id=s-suffix label="Foundation"}

Prefixes are not the only tool.

**Suffixes.** The ending **-ful** means "full of" and **-less** means "without", so the two often make a pair: careful / careless, hopeful / hopeless, useful / useless, painful / painless, helpful / helpless. Check the sense: *harmful* and *harmless* work as a pair, but *helpful* and *helpless* are only roughly opposite, so test each pair in a sentence.

**A completely different word.** Many of the most common opposites share no letters at all: good / bad, rise / fall, victory / defeat, give / take, begin / end, praise / blame. For these there is no rule; you learn them as pairs.

**A change of one ending.** Some pairs differ by an ending or a letter: *increase / decrease, inhale / exhale, ascend / descend, import / export.* Notice that the parts that differ (in-/ex-, a-/de-) are small prefixes with opposite meanings.

:::qc
q: The opposite of "hopeful", made with a suffix, is
options: ["unhopeful", "hopeless", "dishopeful", "hoping"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The pair is hopeful (full of hope) and hopeless (without hope).
wrong: Hopeful has the ending -ful, which means full of. Its opposite swaps that ending for -less, which means without.
:::

## One word, several opposites {lvl=2 id=s-sense label="Class 10"}

Go back to *sharp*. A dictionary lists several meanings of a word, and each meaning has its own opposite. Choosing an antonym means choosing a sense first.

| Word | Sense in the sentence | Antonym |
|---|---|---|
| sharp | a sharp knife | blunt |
| sharp | a sharp mind | dull |
| sharp | a sharp bend | gentle |
| light | a light bag | heavy |
| light | a light room | dark |
| hard | a hard stone | soft |
| hard | a hard question | easy |
| fair | a fair judge | unfair (or biased) |
| fair | fair weather | foul |
| right | the right answer | wrong |
| right | the right hand | left |
| strong | a strong rope | weak |
| strong | strong tea | weak |
| open | an open door | closed |
| open | an open person | reserved |

The method for any exam question is the same. Read the whole sentence, name the sense in your own words (*what does "light" mean here?*), then find the opposite of that meaning.

A trap hides here. The first opposite that comes to mind is often the wrong one for the sentence. "Hard" and "soft" are the famous pair, but in "a hard question", soft makes no sense.

:::qc
q: In "The box was too light to hold the books", which word is the opposite of "light"?
options: ["dark", "heavy", "dim", "bright"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Light here means of little weight, so its opposite is heavy. Dark, dim and bright all go with the other sense, about brightness.
wrong: Light has two senses: little weight and plenty of brightness. This sentence is about holding books, so it is about weight, and the opposite is heavy.
:::

## When the prefix does not flip the meaning {lvl=2 id=s-false label="Class 10"}

A negative-looking beginning is not a guarantee. Three groups fool students and even examiners.

| Group | Examples | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **Same meaning, despite the prefix** | flammable and **inflammable** both mean easy to burn; valuable and **invaluable**, where invaluable means extremely valuable | The in- here is not negative. In inflammable it comes from the Latin sense of "into", as in inflame; in invaluable it means beyond measure [Unverified] |
| **Not a real prefix at all** | unity, uniform, disease, invent, import, impress, discover | The letters only look like a prefix. Unity is not the opposite of "ity" |
| **A prefix that does not give a clean opposite** | mistake, misfortune, disinterested | Mis- means wrongly, not the opposite. Disinterested is covered below |

**The safety angle.** Because *inflammable* was misread as "not flammable", safety signs on tankers and chemicals now use *flammable* instead.

**Un- can mean undo, not just not.** In *unhappy* the prefix means "not". In *untie, unlock, undo, unpack* it means "reverse the action". Both are common, and exam answers usually accept either kind.

**Disinterested and uninterested.** *Uninterested* means not interested: "She was uninterested in cricket." *Disinterested* in careful English means impartial, with nothing to gain: "We need a disinterested judge." Many people use the two as the same word in speech, but a formal answer should keep them apart.

:::qc
q: "The pilot's help was invaluable to the rescue team." What does invaluable mean here?
options: ["of no value", "extremely valuable", "not yet valued", "cheap"]
answer: 1
right: Right. In invaluable the in- does not mean not. It means too valuable to measure, so the word means extremely valuable. Its opposite is worthless.
wrong: Do not let the in- trick you. The sentence is praising the pilot. Invaluable means so valuable that it cannot be priced, and the opposite is worthless.
:::

## Antonyms in the board paper {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

In the Class 10 English paper, opposites usually appear inside the Reading section, as part of a comprehension passage, and sometimes inside grammar or vocabulary-based items. [Unverified] on exact mark splits, which change; check the latest sample paper. The usual forms are:

1. **From the passage: "Find a word in the passage that is the opposite of ..."**. The answer is always in the passage, so you pick, never invent.
2. **Multiple choice: "Select the option that is closest in meaning (or opposite in meaning) to the word ..."**. Four options, one right, often one synonym planted as bait.
3. **Word building: "Add a suitable prefix to form the opposite"** or fill a gap with the opposite of a word given in brackets.

A routine that works:

- Find the given word in the passage and read its whole sentence.
- Say its meaning in your own words.
- Scan for a word with the same part of speech and the opposite meaning, usually nearby.
- Test: put your answer in place of the original. The sentence should still be correct but say the reverse.
- Watch the form: if the given word is an adjective, your answer must be an adjective.

:::worked title="Using the passage"
Passage line: *Some neighbours were doubtful at first, but they were soon convinced as the walls rose.*

Question: Pick a word from the passage that is the opposite of "doubtful".

Step 1: doubtful means not sure. Step 2: look for a word meaning sure. Step 3: "convinced" means persuaded and sure, and it describes the same neighbours as "doubtful" did. Test: "Some neighbours were convinced at first" reverses the sense. Answer: convinced.

If two choices seem possible, prefer the word that sits in the same sentence or the one next to it.
:::

:::qc
q: Which pair gives a correct opposite of the first word?
options: ["scarce / abundant", "scarce / rare", "scarce / small", "scarce / costly"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Scarce means in short supply, and abundant means in plentiful supply.
wrong: Scarce and rare are close to the same meaning, not opposite. Abundant means plentiful, which is the true reverse of scarce.
:::

## Degrees, double negatives and meaning {lvl=3 id=s-degrees label="Beyond the board"}

### Not unhappy is not happy

Negating a gradable opposite does not flip you to the other end. *Not hot* is not cold. In the same way, *not unhappy* is not the same as *happy*; it usually means "reasonably content, though I would not shout about it". Speakers use this weak double negative, called **litotes**, to sound modest, polite or cautious.

| Statement | Likely meaning |
|---|---|
| The food was not bad. | Fairly good |
| She is not unkind. | Often rather kind, said with understatement |
| It is not a small amount. | A large amount |
| I am not unaware of the problem. | I know about it (formal) |

When you write for the board, use the plain word unless you have a reason; use litotes in an essay or a letter for a polite or ironic tone.

### Opposites of different strength

A good antonym matches the strength of the word. The opposite of *delighted* is *miserable*, not just *not pleased*. The opposite of *whisper* is *shout*, not just *speak*. A weak match loses marks even when it points the right way.

### Words that have no clean opposite

Words with a fixed identity (*table*, *wheat*, *Delhi*) have no antonym. Abstract words may have several: the opposite of *freedom* may be *slavery*, *captivity* or *restriction*, depending on which side of freedom you mean.

:::qc
q: "He is not unfriendly" most likely means
options: ["He is warm and very friendly", "He is fairly friendly, in a quiet way", "He is hostile", "He is rude"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The double negative understates. It usually means he is reasonably friendly, without being outgoing.
wrong: A double negative softens. It does not simply flip back to a strong "friendly". It points toward friendly, but says it modestly.
:::

## Words that are their own opposite {lvl=3 id=s-contronym label="Beyond the board"}

A **contronym** is a word with two opposite meanings. The sentence tells you which one is meant.

| Word | Meaning 1 | Meaning 2 |
|---|---|---|
| sanction | to allow (the committee sanctioned the plan) | to punish (the country was sanctioned) |
| cleave | to split apart | to cling together |
| dust | to remove dust from a shelf | to sprinkle dust on a cake |
| oversight | careful supervision | a mistake from not noticing |
| bolt | to fasten a door | to run away |
| fast | moving quickly | fixed firmly in place, as in stuck fast |
| left | remaining | departed |
| screen | to show a film | to hide something |

Do not memorise the list as a trick; use it as a reminder. A word's meaning is found in the sentence around it, which is the same lesson as with *sharp*. In your own writing, if a contronym could be read two ways, choose a clearer word.

## Opposites as a style device {lvl=3 id=s-style label="Beyond the board"}

Writers and speakers use opposites on purpose.

- **Antithesis** puts two opposite ideas side by side in a balanced sentence: *United we stand, divided we fall.* *To err is human, to forgive divine.* (Pope; the line is quoted from memory in the common form.) The balance makes the idea memorable.
- **Oxymoron** joins two opposite words into one phrase: *deafening silence, bitter-sweet, open secret, cruel kindness.* The clash makes the reader stop and think.
- **Contrast in description**: a story often puts a quiet character beside a loud one so that each seems clearer.

You will meet these again in the literature chapters, where the author's craft is a favourite board question. When asked about a writer's use of contrast, name the two opposite ideas, say which words show them, and explain what the contrast does for the reader.

:::qc
q: "A deafening silence" is an example of
options: ["antithesis", "oxymoron", "simile", "synonym"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Deafening (very loud) and silence (no sound) are opposite words joined in one phrase, which is an oxymoron.
wrong: Look for the opposite words inside one phrase: deafening and silence clash. That is an oxymoron. Antithesis balances opposites across a whole sentence.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. An antonym is a word with the opposite meaning in a given sense; same part of speech, same sense. {lvl=1}
2. Three kinds: gradable (a scale with middle points), complementary (one or the other), relational (each needs the other). {lvl=1}
3. Prefixes make opposites: un-, in-, dis-, non-, and im- before p, b, m; il- before l; ir- before r. {lvl=1}
4. Suffix pairs: -ful and -less (careful, careless). Many common opposites are unrelated words (good, bad). {lvl=1}
5. Many words have more than one antonym, one for each sense: sharp (blunt, dull, gentle), light (heavy, dark). {lvl=2}
6. A negative-looking prefix is not a guarantee: inflammable means flammable, invaluable means very valuable. {lvl=2}
7. In the board passage, find the word, read its sentence, name its sense, then pick a word with the same part of speech and the opposite sense. {lvl=2}
8. Not unhappy is not happy: double negatives (litotes) understate. Match the strength of the word, too. {lvl=3}
9. Contronyms (sanction, cleave, left) are their own opposites; the sentence decides the meaning. {lvl=3}
10. Antithesis and oxymoron use opposites for style: United we stand, divided we fall; deafening silence. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Forcing an antonym on a word that has none (table, banana): there is no neat opposite for every word. {lvl=1}
- Giving a noun for an adjective ("hot" and "winter"): keep the part of speech the same. {lvl=1}
- Wrong prefix: "inpossible", "unlegal", "unregular". Check the first letter: im- before p, b, m; il- before l; ir- before r. {lvl=1}
- Treating a gradable pair as complementary: not hot does not mean cold. {lvl=1}
- Giving "hard" and "soft" for every sense of hard: check the sentence first. {lvl=2}
- Believing inflammable means not flammable, or invaluable means worthless: both are trick words. {lvl=2}
- Offering a synonym as an antonym because it feels negative: scarce and rare are close in meaning, not opposite. {lvl=2}
