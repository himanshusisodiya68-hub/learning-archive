## Why a sentence is built around a clause {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

A single word, such as "Fire!", can carry a message, but it leaves the listener to guess the rest. Who? Where? What now? A sentence solves this by saying **who or what** (the subject) and **what happens or is true** (the predicate). The heart of the predicate is a verb that carries time, which you met in the lesson on tenses. A group of words with both a subject and such a verb is a **clause**.

Because a clause is a complete little statement of fact, it is the natural building block of writing:

- One clause gives one idea: *The river rose.*
- Joining clauses lets you show how ideas relate: *The river rose because the dam gates were opened.*

That is the whole reason this topic exists. A sentence is not just a string of words; it is one or more clauses joined in a way that tells the reader how the ideas fit together: one thing **and** another, one thing **but** another, one thing **because** of another. Every rule below follows from that.

### Phrase or clause?

| Group of words | Subject + finite verb? | Type |
|---|---|---|
| in the garden | no | phrase |
| a tall boy with a red cap | no verb | phrase |
| to win the race | no finite verb (to + verb) | phrase |
| the dog barked | yes | clause |
| when the bell rang | yes | clause |

A **finite verb** is one that has a subject and shows tense (*barked*, *rang*, *is*). The to-forms and -ing forms alone (*to win*, *winning*) are non-finite. A phrase is a piece of a clause; a clause is a piece of a sentence.

## Four sentence types by purpose {lvl=1 id=s-purpose label="Foundation"}

Before looking at structure, ask what the sentence is **for**. English marks the four purposes with word order and punctuation.

| Type | Job | Example | Mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Declarative | gives information (statement) | *The shop opens at nine.* | full stop |
| Interrogative | asks a question | *When does the shop open?* | question mark |
| Imperative | gives a command, request or instruction | *Please close the door.* | full stop (or !) |
| Exclamatory | shows strong feeling | *What a beautiful morning it is!* | exclamation mark |

Notes that clear up common confusion:

- A declarative sentence can be positive or negative: *She plays tennis. She does not play tennis.*
- An interrogative needs inverted order or a do-helper: *Is she ready?* *Does she play?* The statement form *She is ready?* with a rising voice is speech, not formal writing.
- An imperative has no stated subject: the subject *you* is understood. *Open your books.* means *You open your books.* With a negative: *Do not run.*
- Exclamatory sentences often begin with **what** or **how**: *What a surprise!* *How cold it is!* A mere interjection (*Alas!*, *Hurrah!*) is not a full sentence.

You can change the type without changing the topic: *The shop opens at nine* (declarative); *Does the shop open at nine?* (interrogative); *Open the shop at nine* (imperative).

:::qc
q: What type of sentence is "Please pass the salt."?
options: ["Imperative", "Declarative", "Interrogative", "Exclamatory"]
answer: 0
right: Right. It asks someone to do something. The understood subject is "you".
wrong: It does not state a fact, ask a question or express strong feeling; it requests an action. That is an imperative, even though it is polite.
:::

## The clause: subject and verb {lvl=1 id=s-clause label="Foundation"}

Every clause answers two questions.

1. **Who or what is it about?** The subject: *the baby*, *Neha and Ravi*, *it*.
2. **What is said about it?** The predicate, which starts with the finite verb: *laughed*, *are playing*, *had been closed*.

A sentence with no finite verb or no subject is a **fragment**. A fragment may be a perfectly good phrase, but it has no standalone meaning.

- *Running down the road.* (no subject, no finite verb) Fragment.
- *The boy running down the road.* (subject, but no finite verb) Fragment.
- *The boy was running down the road.* A sentence.

### Main and subordinate clauses

Clauses fall into two ranks.

- A **main (independent) clause** makes complete sense by itself: *The match was cancelled.*
- A **subordinate (dependent) clause** cannot stand alone; it hangs on a main clause and works as one part of it: *because it rained*.

*The match was cancelled because it rained.* The first clause is the main clause. The clause *because it rained* depends on it: it answers the question "why?" about the main clause. Take it away and the sentence is still complete; leave it by itself and a listener waits for the rest.

A word that opens a subordinate clause is a **subordinator**: *because, although, if, when, while, that, who, which, whether*. It marks the clause as dependent, much as a capital letter marks a sentence start.

:::qc
q: Which group of words is a subordinate clause?
options: ["after the lights went out", "in the dark hall", "The lights went out.", "to switch off the lights"]
answer: 0
right: Right. "After the lights went out" has a subject (the lights) and a finite verb (went), but the word "after" makes it depend on a main clause.
wrong: "In the dark hall" and "to switch off the lights" have no finite verb, so they are phrases. "The lights went out." can stand alone, so it is a main clause. Only option 1 has a subject and verb and still needs more.
:::

## Joining clauses: simple, compound, complex {lvl=2 id=s-struct label="Class 10"}

Now sort sentences by **structure**: how many clauses, and how they relate.

| Structure | Clauses | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | one main clause | *The train arrived late.* |
| Compound | two or more main clauses | *The train arrived late, but we caught it.* |
| Complex | one main + at least one subordinate | *We caught the train although it arrived late.* |
| Compound-complex | two or more main + at least one subordinate | *The train arrived late, but we caught it because we had waited.* |

### Simple sentences

A simple sentence has **one finite verb group**, but it can still be long. It may have a compound subject, a compound predicate or many phrases.

- *Asha and Karan went to the fair.* (compound subject)
- *The cat jumped on the table and knocked over the vase.* (one subject, two verbs: a compound predicate, still one clause)
- *Hearing the bell, the students ran to the hall in the rain.* (long, but only one finite verb)

Length is not the test. Count finite verbs with their own subjects.

### Compound sentences: equal partners

A compound sentence joins clauses that are **equally important** with a **coordinating conjunction**, remembered as FANBOYS: **f**or, **a**nd, **n**or, **b**ut, **o**r, **y**et, **s**o.

| Conjunction | Relation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| and | addition | *The sun set, and the birds fell silent.* |
| but, yet | contrast | *He tried hard, but he failed.* |
| or | choice | *Leave now, or you will miss the bus.* |
| so | result | *It was cold, so we lit a fire.* |
| for | reason (formal) | *We stayed in, for it was raining.* |
| nor | second negative | *He did not call, nor did he write.* |

A comma goes before the conjunction when it joins two full clauses. When the subject is shared and only the verb is repeated, no comma is needed: *She sang and danced.* Note the inverted order after *nor*: *nor did he write*, not *nor he wrote*.

Without a conjunction, a **semicolon** can join two closely related main clauses: *The room was silent; nobody dared to speak.*

### Complex sentences: one idea depends on another

A complex sentence puts one idea in charge and makes the rest of the information work for it. The helper idea is a subordinate clause, and it has a job. The three jobs of the subordinate clause give the three kinds.

**1. Noun clause: does a noun's job (subject, object, complement).**
Opened by *that, what, whether, if, who, why, how* and similar words.

- *I know **that the exam is difficult**.* (object of *know*)
- ***What she said** surprised us.* (subject)
- *The question is **whether we can finish in time**.* (complement)

Test: you can replace the clause with *it* or *something* (*I know it*; *It surprised us*).

**2. Adjective (relative) clause: describes a noun.**
Opened by *who, whom, whose, which, that, where, when*.

- *The girl **who won the medal** is my cousin.*
- *This is the house **where I grew up**.*

Test: it answers "which one?" about a noun. The next lesson on relative clauses covers punctuation and the choice of word.

**3. Adverb clause: tells when, where, why, how or under what condition.**

| Meaning | Subordinators | Example |
|---|---|---|
| time | when, while, before, after, until, as soon as | *Call me **when you arrive**.* |
| place | where, wherever | *Sit **where you like**.* |
| reason | because, since, as | *He stayed home **because he was ill**.* |
| contrast | although, though, even though, whereas | ***Although it was late**, they kept working.* |
| condition | if, unless, provided that | ***If you heat ice**, it melts.* |
| purpose | so that, in order that | *He spoke slowly **so that everyone could follow**.* |
| result | so ... that, such ... that | *It was **so** hot **that we stayed indoors**.* |
| comparison | as ... as, than | *She runs faster **than I do**.* |

Punctuation tip: an adverb clause that **comes first** takes a comma after it (*Although it was late, they kept working*); one that comes after the main clause usually does not (*They kept working although it was late*).

### Compound-complex sentences

Mix both ideas: two main clauses joined by a coordinator, and at least one subordinate clause.

*The guard blew the whistle, and the train moved off **before the last passengers had settled**.*
Main 1: *The guard blew the whistle.* Main 2: *the train moved off.* Subordinate (adverb of time): *before the last passengers had settled.*

### A routine to name the structure

1. Underline every finite verb and give it its subject.
2. For each clause, ask: could it stand alone?
3. Count: all can stand alone and there is one clause, so **simple**; several, **compound**; one cannot stand alone, **complex**; both kinds, **compound-complex**.

:::worked title="Analysing a sentence in four steps"
Sentence: *Mira studied late, so she was tired when the exam began.*

Step 1: Finite verbs and subjects: *Mira studied*; *she was*; *the exam began*.
Step 2: *Mira studied late* stands alone. *she was tired* stands alone. *when the exam began* cannot stand alone.
Step 3: Two main clauses joined by *so*; one subordinate clause opened by *when*.
Step 4: Structure: **compound-complex**. The subordinate clause is an adverb clause of time modifying *was tired*.
:::

:::qc
q: Name the structure: "Although the road was blocked, we reached on time."
options: ["Complex", "Compound", "Simple", "Compound-complex"]
answer: 0
right: Right. "Although the road was blocked" is subordinate; "we reached on time" is the one main clause.
wrong: Count the clauses: two finite verbs (was blocked, reached). Only "we reached on time" can stand alone, so there is one main and one subordinate clause: complex.
:::

:::qc
q: In "She said that she was tired", what is "that she was tired"?
options: ["Noun clause", "Adjective clause", "Adverb clause", "Main clause"]
answer: 0
right: Right. It is the object of "said", answering "said what?". A noun clause.
wrong: Ask "said what?". The clause answers it as an object, which is a noun's job. It is a noun clause.
:::

## Sentences that go wrong {lvl=2 id=s-errors label="Class 10"}

Because the clause is the basic unit, most sentence-level errors are about getting the number of clauses and their joins wrong.

### Fragments

A fragment is a clause-shaped piece that is missing something. The most common cause is a subordinate clause punctuated as a sentence.

- Wrong: *I missed the bus. Because I woke up late.*
- Right: *I missed the bus because I woke up late.*

The reader sees the full stop and expects a complete thought. *Because I woke up late* leaves a gap: "so what?".

### Run-on sentences and comma splices

Two main clauses jammed together with no join (run-on) or with only a comma (comma splice) leave the reader with no signal where one idea ends.

- Wrong: *The rain stopped, we went out.* (comma splice)
- Wrong: *The rain stopped we went out.* (run-on)

Four fixes:

1. Full stop: *The rain stopped. We went out.*
2. Semicolon: *The rain stopped; we went out.*
3. Coordinating conjunction: *The rain stopped, so we went out.*
4. Subordinate one: *When the rain stopped, we went out.* (Often the best, because it also says how the ideas relate.)

### Faulty linking

- **Double conjunctions.** Wrong: *Although he was ill, but he came.* Use one: *Although he was ill, he came.* or *He was ill, but he came.* (The same applies to *because ... so*.)
- **Dangling modifiers.** *Walking to school, the rain started.* (Who was walking? Not the rain.) Right: *Walking to school, I felt the rain start.*
- **Mixed tenses across clauses.** Link the clauses with the sequence rules from the lesson on tenses.

## Combining and transforming sentences {lvl=2 id=s-synth label="Class 10"}

The board paper often asks you to **combine** short sentences into one, or **transform** a sentence without changing its meaning. The skill is to move between structures.

### Combining into a simple sentence

Use a participle, an infinitive, a preposition phrase or an appositive to turn a clause into a phrase.

| Two sentences | One simple sentence |
|---|---|
| She opened the door. She saw a stranger. | *Opening the door, she saw a stranger.* |
| He wanted to win. He practised daily. | *To win, he practised daily.* |
| It was raining. We stayed at home. | *Because of the rain, we stayed at home.* |
| Mr Rao is our principal. He spoke today. | *Mr Rao, our principal, spoke today.* |

### Combining into a compound sentence

Use a coordinating conjunction (or a semicolon): *She opened the door, and she saw a stranger.*

### Combining into a complex sentence

Turn one idea into a subordinate clause.

- *She opened the door. She saw a stranger.* becomes *When she opened the door, she saw a stranger.*
- *He is rich. He is not happy.* becomes *Although he is rich, he is not happy.*
- *This is the pen. I bought it yesterday.* becomes *This is the pen that I bought yesterday.*

### Moving between the three structures

The same meaning can wear three shapes.

| Structure | Sentence |
|---|---|
| Simple | *Because of his illness, he missed the match.* |
| Compound | *He was ill, so he missed the match.* |
| Complex | *He missed the match because he was ill.* |

| Structure | Sentence |
|---|---|
| Simple | *In spite of being tired, she kept working.* |
| Compound | *She was tired, yet she kept working.* |
| Complex | *Although she was tired, she kept working.* |

A rule of thumb: a **simple** sentence uses a phrase for the reason or contrast (*because of, in spite of, on account of, too ... to, after + -ing*); a **compound** sentence uses and/but/so/yet; a **complex** sentence uses a subordinate clause (*because, although, when, if, that*).

### Other common transformations

- **Affirmative to negative, same meaning.** *He is the tallest boy.* becomes *No other boy is as tall as he.* *Everyone likes her.* becomes *There is no one who does not like her.* (Check the meaning stays the same.)
- **Declarative to exclamatory.** *It is a very beautiful sight.* becomes *What a beautiful sight it is!* Statement to rhetorical question: *Nobody can forget that day.* becomes *Who can forget that day?*
- **Too ... to / so ... that.** *The box is too heavy for me to lift.* becomes *The box is so heavy that I cannot lift it.* (The meaning of *too* is negative: the lifting does not happen.)
- **Active and passive, direct and reported speech.** These have their own lessons, but the same principle applies: the clause structure may change, the meaning must not.

:::worked title="One idea, three shapes"
Task: Rewrite *Despite being poor, he was generous* as (a) compound and (b) complex.

(a) Compound: *He was poor, but he was generous.* The contrast word *despite* becomes *but*, and the phrase *being poor* becomes a full clause.
(b) Complex: *Although he was poor, he was generous.* Here *although* introduces an adverb clause of contrast.

Check meaning: all three say that poverty did not stop his generosity. If a version suggests that poverty caused the generosity, the meaning has changed.
:::

:::qc
q: Which is the correct complex form of "He was ill, so he stayed at home"?
options: ["Because he was ill, he stayed at home.", "Because he was ill, so he stayed at home.", "He was ill because he stayed at home.", "Although he was ill, he stayed at home."]
answer: 0
right: Right. The reason (illness) becomes the subordinate clause and the result is the main clause. Only one linking word is used.
wrong: Check the meaning and the linking. Option 2 uses both because and so; option 3 reverses cause and effect; option 4 changes the relation to contrast.
:::

## Sentences in the board's questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

Clause and sentence-type knowledge shows up in several board tasks. [Unverified] The exact format and weighting of the paper changes between sessions, so use your own sample papers to check the current pattern.

1. **Identify the clause or sentence type.** *Underline the subordinate clause and say what kind it is.* Find the finite verbs, find the subordinator, then ask what job the clause does (noun, adjective or adverb) by using the "what / which / when, why" tests above.
2. **Combine sentences** with a given word (*when*, *although*, *as soon as*, *because*). Put the idea you want to stress in the main clause, and check the tense after time words and *if* (present for future).
3. **Transform sentences** (simple, compound, complex) or **change the type** (statement to question, exclamation to statement). Keep the meaning and do not add or drop information.
4. **Editing for punctuation and linking.** Look for comma splices, fragments and double conjunctions.
5. **Writing tasks.** Mix simple and complex sentences. A paragraph of only short simple sentences sounds choppy; one of only long ones is hard to follow.

A routine for synthesis questions:

- Read both sentences and find the **relation** between the ideas: addition, contrast, reason, result, time, condition.
- Choose the joining word that expresses it.
- Decide which idea is main and which is subordinate, then write one sentence.
- Check the tense, the pronouns (do not leave *he ... he* if one noun is enough) and the punctuation.

:::worked title="Combine, then name"
Combine with *as soon as*: *The bell rang. The students rushed out.*

Answer: *As soon as the bell rang, the students rushed out.* The clause *As soon as the bell rang* is an adverb clause of time (subordinate) and *the students rushed out* is the main clause. The sentence is complex. A comma follows the subordinate clause because it comes first.
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "It was getting dark, we decided to go home."
options: ["A comma splice: join with a conjunction, semicolon or full stop", "Wrong tense in the first clause", "Missing subject in the second clause", "No error"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Two main clauses are joined by a comma alone. Write "It was getting dark, so we decided to go home."
wrong: Both clauses have subjects and correct tenses. The problem is the join: a comma alone cannot link two main clauses.
:::

## Beyond the board: finer points of the clause {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### Non-finite clauses

Modern grammar also calls some verb phrases with their own objects or modifiers **clauses**, even without a finite verb.

- To-infinitive: *She went home to rest.* (purpose)
- -ing: *Hearing the news, he smiled.*
- -ed: *Tired from the journey, they slept early.*

They pack in the same relations as a full subordinate clause in fewer words, which is why a polished writer uses them. The danger is the dangling modifier: the unstated subject of the non-finite clause should be the subject of the main clause.

### Coordination versus subordination as a choice of emphasis

Both of these report the same facts, yet they say different things.

- *He won, but he was injured.* (equal weight: two facts)
- *Although he won, he was injured.* (the injury is the main point)
- *Although he was injured, he won.* (the win is the main point)

The idea in the main clause is what the writer wants the reader to remember. Used with care, subordination is a tool for guiding a reader's attention, not only a matter of structure.

### Conjunctive adverbs are not conjunctions

*However, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, hence* link ideas, but they do not join clauses by themselves. After a comma alone, they still produce a comma splice.

- Wrong: *It was late, however we went on.*
- Right: *It was late; however, we went on.* or *It was late. However, we went on.*

### Embedded and nested clauses

Clauses can sit inside other clauses. *The book that the teacher recommended was hard.* The adjective clause *that the teacher recommended* sits inside the main clause, between its subject and verb. Writers use this to build rich sentences, but too many nested levels make a sentence difficult to read. A useful limit: two levels of embedding in most writing.

### Sentence variety and the reader

A mix of lengths keeps the reader's attention: a short simple sentence after a long complex one gives emphasis. *The committee had weighed the costs, argued over the schedule and, after three long meetings, reached a decision. It failed.* Reading your own work aloud shows where a sentence is dragging.

:::qc
q: Which punctuation of the following is correct?
options: ["The paper was easy; nevertheless, many students lost marks.", "The paper was easy, nevertheless many students lost marks.", "The paper was easy nevertheless, many students lost marks.", "The paper was easy, nevertheless, many students lost marks."]
answer: 0
right: Right. A semicolon (or a full stop) separates the two main clauses, and the conjunctive adverb nevertheless takes a comma after it.
wrong: Nevertheless is not a coordinating conjunction, so a comma alone cannot join the clauses. Use a semicolon or full stop before it.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A clause is a group of words with its own subject and finite verb; a phrase has no finite verb of its own. {lvl=1}
2. By purpose there are four sentence types: declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory. {lvl=1}
3. A main clause can stand alone; a subordinate clause depends on a main clause and is opened by a subordinator such as because, although, if, when, that, who. {lvl=1}
4. A fragment lacks a subject or finite verb, or is a dependent clause punctuated as a sentence. {lvl=1}
5. By structure: simple (one main clause), compound (two or more main clauses), complex (main plus subordinate), compound-complex (both). {lvl=2}
6. Coordinating conjunctions (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) join equal clauses; a comma usually precedes them between two main clauses. {lvl=2}
7. Subordinate clauses do three jobs: noun clause (a noun's job), adjective clause (describes a noun), adverb clause (when, where, why, how, condition, contrast, purpose, result). {lvl=2}
8. Two main clauses joined only by a comma is a comma splice; fix with a full stop, semicolon, conjunction or subordination. {lvl=2}
9. To transform, keep the meaning: phrases for simple, and/but/so/yet for compound, because/although/when/if/that for complex. {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: non-finite clauses, choice of emphasis between coordination and subordination, conjunctive adverbs (however, therefore) need a semicolon or full stop, not a comma alone. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Calling a long sentence "complex" because it is long: count clauses, not words. {lvl=1}
- Punctuating a subordinate clause as a sentence: "Because I was late." is a fragment. {lvl=1}
- Treating an imperative as having no subject: the subject "you" is understood. {lvl=1}
- Calling any sentence with "and" a compound sentence: "Asha and Karan left" is simple. Join clauses, not just words. {lvl=2}
- Comma splice: "It was late, we left." Use a conjunction, semicolon or full stop. {lvl=2}
- Double conjunction: "Although he was tired, but he worked." or "Because it rained, so we stayed in." Use one. {lvl=2}
- Putting will in an if or when clause when combining: "If it will rain" should be "If it rains". {lvl=2}
- Changing the meaning during transformation: cause and effect reversed, or contrast turned into reason. {lvl=2}
- Forgetting the comma after a subordinate clause that comes first. {lvl=2}
- Using however or therefore with just a comma between two main clauses. {lvl=3}
:::
