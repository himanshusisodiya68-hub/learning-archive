## Why the verb matches its subject {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

A sentence is a message about someone or something. The **subject** is the one the message is about, and the **verb** says what that subject does or is. In a long sentence the two can end up far apart, and the reader may lose the link. English therefore lets the verb carry a small signal of who the subject is: whether it is **one** (singular) or **more than one** (plural). This signal is called **agreement** or **concord**.

Compare:

- The girl **sings** well.
- The girls **sing** well.

The *-s* on *sings* is the verb telling you, "my subject is a single person". When you read *The girls sing*, the bare verb says "my subject is plural". If the signal and the subject disagree, as in *The girls sings well*, the reader feels a small jolt, and the sentence looks careless.

Two facts shape everything that follows.

1. **English hides the signal.** Only a few verb forms change. In the present tense, the *-s* or *-es* appears only with a singular third-person subject (he, she, it, the boy). In the past tense, only the verb **be** (was/were) changes. So agreement mistakes happen mostly with the present simple, the verb *be* (is/are, was/were), and the helpers *has/have* and *does/do*.
2. **The noun nearest the verb is not always the subject.** Phrases can sit between them. The skill in this topic is to find the real subject and ignore the distractions.

## The basic rule {lvl=1 id=s-basic label="Foundation"}

**Singular subject, singular verb. Plural subject, plural verb.**

| Subject | Present simple | *be* | *have* | *do* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I walk | am / was | have | do |
| you | you walk | are / were | have | do |
| he, she, it, the boy | he walks | is / was | has | does |
| we, they, the boys | they walk | are / were | have | do |

Notice the odd ones. **I** takes *am* and *have*, and **you** always takes *are/were*, even when it means one person. Only the third-person singular (he, she, it, any single noun) takes *-s*, *is*, *has*, *does*.

Singular and plural nouns:

- Singular: *dog, teacher, idea, the Sun*. Verb: *The dog barks. The idea works.*
- Plural: *dogs, teachers, ideas, children, mice, feet*. Verb: *The dogs bark. The children play.*

Careful with the **irregular plurals** (children, men, women, mice, geese, teeth, feet, people). They do not end in *-s*, but they are plural, so they take the plural verb: *The mice are in the cupboard.* Likewise, a few nouns have the same form in singular and plural (sheep, deer, fish, series), and the verb decides which is meant: *The sheep is limping* (one) and *The sheep are grazing* (many).

:::qc

answer: 1
right: Right. Children is plural even though it does not end in -s, so the verb is play.

:::

### Questions and negatives

In questions and negatives the helper (do/does, is/are, has/have) carries the agreement, and the main verb stays in its base form.

- *Does she **like** tea?* (not *Does she likes*)
- *Doesn't the bus **stop** here?*
- *Have the boys **finished**? Has the teacher **finished**?*

## Phrases between subject and verb {lvl=1 id=s-between label="Foundation"}

This is the most common trap. A phrase in the middle contains a noun of the other number, and the verb is wrongly matched to it. The rule: **the verb agrees with the head noun, not with the noun inside the phrase.**

- *The **box** of chocolates **is** on the table.* (not *are*)
- *The **price** of vegetables **has** gone up.* (not *have*)
- *The **students** in this class **are** very bright.* (not *is*)
- *A **list** of names **was** pinned to the board.*

Method: cross out the phrase that starts with *of, in, on, with, from, near* and read what remains. *The box ... is on the table.*

### Along with, as well as, together with, in addition to, besides

These words look like *and* but they do not join subjects. They start a **side phrase**, so the verb still agrees with the first subject.

- *The captain, along with his team, **is** waiting.* (not *are*)
- *Rohan, as well as his sisters, **was** invited.*
- *The teacher, together with the students, **has** left.*

If you want a plural verb, join the subjects with real *and*: *The captain and his team **are** waiting.*

:::qc
q: "The quality of these mangoes ___ excellent."
options: ["are", "is", "were", "have been"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The head noun is quality, which is singular. The phrase of these mangoes does not decide the verb.
wrong: Cross out "of these mangoes". What remains is "The quality ... ___ excellent". Quality is singular, so is.
:::

## Joined subjects: and, or, nor {lvl=1 id=s-joined label="Foundation"}

### With *and*: plural

Two or more subjects joined by **and** make a plural subject.

- *Ravi and Sita **are** cousins.*
- *Rice and curry **are** on the menu.* (two dishes)

**Exception: one idea.** If the two words name one thing or one person, the verb is singular.

- *Bread and butter **is** my usual breakfast.* (one dish)
- *The poet and singer **has** arrived.* (one person with two roles; the repeated *the* would make it two people: *The poet and the singer **have** arrived.*)

**Each, every, many a** before joined subjects make the verb singular.

- *Every boy and every girl **was** given a prize.*
- *Many a student and teacher **has** praised the library.*

### With *or*, *nor*, *either ... or*, *neither ... nor*: look at the nearer subject

When subjects are joined by a word that offers a **choice** rather than adding them together, the verb agrees with the subject **nearest to it**. This is the **proximity rule**.

- *Neither the teacher nor the students **were** late.* (students is nearest)
- *Neither the students nor the teacher **was** late.* (teacher is nearest)
- *Either you or he **is** wrong.*
- *Either he or you **are** wrong.*
- *The manager or his assistants **are** present.*

A tip for easy reading: put the plural subject last, so the plural verb sounds natural.

### Summary table

| Joining word | Verb agrees with |
|---|---|
| and | both together: plural (unless one idea) |
| or, nor, either ... or, neither ... nor | the nearer subject |
| along with, as well as, together with, with | the first subject only |
| not only ... but also | the nearer subject |

:::qc
q: "Neither the coach nor the players ___ ready."
options: ["was", "is", "were", "has been"]
answer: 2
right: Right. With neither ... nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject, players, which is plural, so were.
wrong: Look at the subject nearest to the verb. It is players, plural. So the verb must be plural: were.
:::

## Collective nouns, units and other tricky nouns {lvl=2 id=s-nouns label="Class 10"}

### Collective nouns

A **collective noun** (team, family, class, committee, crowd, jury, audience, army, staff) names a group as one unit.

- When the group acts **as one**, the verb is singular: *The team **is** practising. The committee **has** decided.*
- When the members act **separately**, the verb is plural: *The team **are** arguing over the captain.* *The committee **were** divided in their opinions.*

In most Class 10 exercises the single-unit reading is safe, and it is the one the board's keys usually follow. If the sentence has a plural pronoun later (*the family ... their*), keep the same number throughout: *The family **is** proud of **its** son.* [Unverified: some regional usage allows *are ... their*, but exam keys tend to prefer consistency.]

**Always plural** (they stand for people): *The police **are** searching. The people **are** angry. The cattle **are** in the field.* **Always singular in meaning**: *furniture, luggage, information, advice, equipment, scenery*. These are uncountable, so they take a singular verb: *The furniture **is** new. The information **was** useful.* They cannot take *a*, *an* or a number (not *an advice, three furnitures*).

### Singular in form, plural in meaning, and vice versa

| Group | Examples | Verb |
|---|---|---|
| Plural in form, singular in meaning | news, mathematics, physics, economics, politics, athletics, measles, billiards | singular: *The news **is** good. Physics **is** difficult.* |
| Plural, always (things with two parts) | scissors, trousers, spectacles, pliers, jeans, tongs | plural: *The scissors **are** sharp.* |
| With "a pair of" | a pair of scissors, a pair of trousers | singular, because the subject is "pair": *A pair of scissors **is** on the table.* |
| Titles and names | *Gulliver's Travels, The Arabian Nights, the United States* | singular: *Gulliver's Travels **is** a famous book.* |

Subjects that end in *-ics* are singular when they name a field of study (*Mathematics **is** my best subject*) but may be plural when they mean practical matters (*His politics **are** unclear*, *Her ethics **were** questioned*). [Unverified: textbooks differ; the board mostly asks about the subject names.]

### Amounts: money, time, distance, weight

When an amount is thought of as **one total**, the verb is singular, even if the number is plural.

- *Five hundred rupees **is** a lot of money for me.*
- *Ten kilometres **is** a long way to walk.*
- *Two hours **is** enough.*
- *Ten years **have** passed since then* counts separate years and is plural; *Ten years **is** a long time to wait* treats the period as one block and is singular. Follow the sense: one total, singular; separate units, plural.

### Fractions, percentages and *all, some, most, half*

The verb follows the noun that comes after *of*.

- *Two-thirds of the **cake** **has** been eaten.* (cake: singular)
- *Two-thirds of the **students** **have** passed.* (students: plural)
- *Half of the water **is** gone. Half of the apples **are** rotten.*
- *Forty percent of the **land** **is** barren; forty percent of the **farmers** **are** poor.*

### A number of, the number of

- *A number of students **are** absent.* (*A number of* means "several": plural)
- *The number of students **is** small.* (*The number* is one count: singular)
- *A variety of colours **are** available.* (several), but *The variety of colours **is** striking.* (the range, one thing)

### One of, each of, none of

- **One of the** + plural noun takes a singular verb: *One of my friends **lives** in Kochi.* (The subject is *one*.)
- **Each of, either of, neither of** + plural noun take a singular verb: *Each of the boys **has** a bat. Neither of the answers **is** correct.*
- **None of** is flexible. It is acceptable to say *None of the boys **has** come* (strictly, "not one") or *None of the boys **have** come* (informal, "not any"). For exam answers, the singular is the safer choice. [Unverified: usage guides differ, and how a board key treats *none of* is not checked.]

### Indefinite pronouns

| Always singular | Always plural | Depends on the noun |
|---|---|---|
| each, every, everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, anyone, anybody, no one, nobody, nothing, either, neither | both, few, many, several | all, some, any, most, none |

- *Everyone **has** finished.* (not *have*)
- *Nobody **knows** the answer.*
- *Both of them **are** here. Few **were** present.*
- *Some of the milk **is** spilt. Some of the cups **are** broken.*

Quick test: *every*, *any*, *some*, *no* + *-one / -body / -thing* are all singular.

## Verb before subject, relative clauses and linking verbs {lvl=2 id=s-order label="Class 10"}

### *There is / there are*

Here the word *there* is just a place-holder. The **real subject comes after the verb**, so look ahead and match it.

- *There **is** a pen on the desk.*
- *There **are** three pens on the desk.*
- *There **is** a pen and two pencils on the desk.* (informal speech often matches the first item; careful writing uses *are* because the subject is compound: *There **are** a pen and two pencils.*)

The same applies to **inverted sentences**: *Beside the river **stands** a temple. Beside the river **stand** two temples. Here **comes** the bus. Here **come** the buses.* Find the subject, wherever it sits.

### Relative pronouns: who, which, that

A relative pronoun takes the number of the noun (**antecedent**) it refers to.

- *I know a boy who **plays** the sitar.* (boy: singular)
- *I know boys who **play** the sitar.*
- *She is one of those students who **work** hard.* (who refers to *students*, the plural: the group of students who work hard.)
- *He is the only one of the students who **works** hard.* (here the subject is *the only one*, singular)

The pattern **one of those ... who/that + plural verb** is a board favourite; the pattern **the only one of ... who + singular verb** is its mirror.

### The verb agrees with the subject, not the complement

After a linking verb (be), the **subject** decides the verb, not the noun that follows.

- *My biggest problem **is** the exams.* (subject: problem)
- *The exams **are** my biggest problem.* (subject: exams)
- *The boys' favourite games **are** cricket and football.* (subject: games, plural)

### Pronoun *it* and *what*

- *It **is** the children who **are** to blame.* (*it is* stays singular even with plural complement)
- *What I need **is** more books.* / *What I need **are** more books.* Both occur [Unverified which the board prefers; avoid in your own writing by rewriting].

### Titles, names and expressions with *I* or *you*

- *Either you or I **am** wrong.* (nearest subject: I)
- *It is I who **am** responsible.* (formal; *who* takes the person of *I*) [Unverified: modern usage also accepts *who is*]

:::qc
q: "There ___ many reasons for his failure."
options: ["is", "are", "has been", "was"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The real subject, reasons, comes after the verb and is plural, so there are.
wrong: In there is / there are, the real subject follows the verb. Reasons is plural, so the verb must be are.
:::

:::qc
q: "She is one of those singers who ___ perfect pitch."
options: ["has", "have", "is having", "was having"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Who refers to singers, so the verb is plural, have. The sentence names the group of singers who have perfect pitch, and she is one of them.
wrong: Who points back to singers, which is plural. So have, not has.
:::

## Agreement in the board's questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

Agreement appears in three common board forms.

1. **Error correction (editing).** A passage with a line like *"The number of students are increasing."* You must spot the line, mark the faulty word and write the correct one.
2. **Fill-in-the-blank with options** (*"Neither of the boys ___ present"* with *is/are/were/have*).
3. **Gap filling with the verb in brackets**, where tense and agreement are tested together.

A method that works in all three:

- Underline the verb.
- Ask "Who or what is doing it?" The answer is the real subject.
- Cross out any phrase with *of, in, with, along with, as well as* and any *who/which* clause.
- Decide the number: singular or plural. Check the special cases (or/nor, each/every, news, a number of).
- Match the verb. Then confirm the **tense** agrees too.

:::worked title="Mark the error in thirty seconds"
Find the error: *The list of items to be purchased for the party were handed over to Mr Rao.*

Step 1: the verb is *were handed*.
Step 2: who was handed over? A *list*, not the items.
Step 3: cross out *of items to be purchased for the party*. What remains: *The list ... were handed over.*
Step 4: the list is singular, so the verb should be *was handed*.

Correction: *were* should be *was*.
:::

:::worked title="A mixed set from a typical editing passage"
Correct the verb in each: (a) *Each of the players have a medal.* (b) *Neither the captain nor his men was present.* (c) *Mathematics are a difficult subject.* (d) *The scissors is on the shelf.* (e) *A number of books was missing.*

(a) *have* should be *has*: subject *Each*. (b) *was* should be *were*: nearest subject *men*. (c) *are* should be *is*: a subject name, singular. (d) *is* should be *are*: scissors is plural in form and meaning. (e) *was* should be *were*: *a number of* means several.
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "The committee has decided that their members will meet on Monday."
options: ["has decided should be have decided", "their should be its", "will meet should be would meet"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The committee acted as one unit (has decided), so the matching pronoun is singular, its. Mixing has and their is inconsistent in number.
wrong: The verb treats the committee as one unit. A later pronoun must agree: its, not their.
:::

## Beyond the board: notional agreement and finer points {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

Grammar books give rules by form (is the subject singular or plural?). Real writers sometimes follow **meaning** instead. This is **notional agreement**: the verb agrees with the idea, not the grammatical form.

### Collective nouns again

*The jury **are** still arguing* stresses the individual jurors; *The jury **has** reached a verdict* treats them as one body. In some varieties of English the plural is used more freely than in others, so a plural verb after a collective noun is not wrong in itself; it signals the sense. In the board exam, a clean, consistent pairing is safer.

### Singular *they*

*They* can refer to a single person whose gender is unknown or irrelevant: *Someone left their umbrella.* *If a student is absent, they must bring a note.* The verb stays plural after *they* (*they are*, *they were*) even when it refers to one person. This use is old in English and is now accepted by many style guides, but some exam keys still prefer *he or she* or a plural subject. [Unverified: the board's stance is not stated in the texts I know; follow your teacher's key.]

### Proximity pulling the verb

In speech and informal writing, a verb often drifts to match the nearest noun even in a sentence where the head noun is a different number: *The key to the doors **are** missing.* This is called **attraction**, and it is the very mistake the board's error-correction items test. Good writers rewrite when the sentence sounds awkward: *The doors' keys are missing* or *The key to the doors is missing.*

### Plural subject, singular idea: quotes and phrases

When a phrase or clause is the subject, the verb is singular: *Eating late **is** unhealthy. To err **is** human. What he said **was** true. That the Earth moves **is** a fact.* A gerund phrase with a plural noun inside is still singular: *Reading novels **is** my hobby.*

### Style and clarity

Agreement is partly a courtesy to the reader. If a sentence is hard to agree (*Either Mira or her cousins ... ?*), the cleanest fix is to **reorder** (*Either her cousins or Mira **is** ...*) or to **recast** (*Mira, or her cousins, will attend*). Strong writers choose the structure so the agreement is obvious.

:::qc
q: Which is the best reading? "The audience ___ clapping and cheering in different ways." (members acting separately)
options: ["was", "were", "has been", "is"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The members act separately and in different ways, so notional agreement allows the plural, were.
wrong: The phrase "in different ways" shows individuals acting separately, so the plural were is the better match to the meaning.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. The verb agrees with its subject in number: singular with singular, plural with plural. Only the third-person singular present takes -s, is, has, does. {lvl=1}
2. I takes am and have; you always takes are and were, even for one person. {lvl=1}
3. Ignore phrases in between (of ..., in ..., along with ..., as well as ..., who ...); the verb matches the head noun. {lvl=1}
4. Subjects joined by and are plural, unless they name one thing or person. {lvl=1}
5. With or, nor, either ... or, neither ... nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject. {lvl=1}
6. Each, every, everyone, nobody, either, neither, one of the ... take a singular verb. Both, few, many, several take a plural. {lvl=2}
7. News, mathematics, physics, and book titles are singular; scissors, trousers, spectacles are plural; a pair of scissors is singular. {lvl=2}
8. A number of means several (plural); the number of is one count (singular). {lvl=2}
9. In there is / there are, and in inverted sentences, the real subject follows the verb. {lvl=2}
10. A relative pronoun (who, which, that) takes the number of the noun it refers to: one of those who ... + plural; the only one who ... + singular. {lvl=2}
11. Collective nouns take a singular verb for one unit and a plural verb for separate members; keep pronouns consistent. {lvl=2}
12. Beyond the board: notional agreement, singular they, and recasting a sentence to avoid awkward agreement. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "The box of apples are on the table": the subject is box, not apples. Cross out the phrase. {lvl=1}
- "She don't like tea": with he, she, it use doesn't. {lvl=1}
- "The children plays": children is plural, so play. {lvl=1}
- "You was late": you always takes were. {lvl=1}
- "The teacher, along with the students, are waiting": along with does not make a plural subject; use is. {lvl=2}
- "Neither of the boys are present": neither is singular; use is. {lvl=2}
- "Everyone have finished": everyone is singular; use has. {lvl=2}
- "Mathematics are difficult", "The news are bad": these look plural but are singular. {lvl=2}
- "A pair of scissors are on the table": the subject is pair, so is. {lvl=2}
- "The number of students are rising": the number of is singular; a number of is plural. {lvl=2}
- "He is one of the students who works hard": who refers to students, so work. {lvl=2}
- "There is two pens on the desk": the real subject is two pens, so there are. {lvl=2}
- Using their with a collective noun treated as one (has decided ... their): choose one number and keep it. {lvl=3}
:::
