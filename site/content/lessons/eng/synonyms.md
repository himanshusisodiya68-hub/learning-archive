## Why English has so many near-twins {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Ask for another word for "big" and English offers large, huge, enormous, vast and giant, and it has dozens of such sets. Why so many?

The usual explanation is history. English began as a Germanic language. Then, after the Norman conquest in 1066, French became the language of the court and the law, and Latin was the language of church and learning. English kept the old words and added the new ones, so one idea often has a plain Germanic word, a French word and a Latin word. [Unverified: this is the standard account, summarised from memory.]

| Everyday (Germanic) | Courtly (French) | Learned (Latin) |
|---|---|---|
| ask | question | interrogate |
| help | aid | assist |
| end | finish | conclude, terminate |
| kingly | royal | regal |

Notice that the three columns are not three copies of one word. Each has a different flavour. That is the first clue that synonyms are never exactly the same.

## What a synonym really is {lvl=1 id=s-meaning label="Foundation"}

A **synonym** is a word whose meaning is close to that of another word. The word "close" matters. Take "big" and "large": you can say "a big house" or "a large house", and the sentences feel the same. But "big sister" is not the same as "large sister", and "a large number of people" does not become "a big number of people". Even the pair that seem most alike fail somewhere.

Every word has two layers.

- **Denotation**: the plain meaning in the dictionary. *Thrifty* and *stingy* both denote "careful about spending money".
- **Connotation**: the feeling or suggestion that comes with the word. *Thrifty* suggests wise; *stingy* suggests unpleasantly mean.

Two words can share the first layer and differ in the second. This is why a synonym that is "correct" in the dictionary sense can still be wrong in a sentence.

| Plain idea | Positive feeling | Neutral | Negative feeling |
|---|---|---|---|
| careful with money | thrifty, prudent | economical | stingy, miserly |
| firm in opinion | determined, resolute | firm | stubborn, obstinate |
| not shy about speaking | confident | assertive | arrogant, pushy |
| young in spirit | childlike | youthful | childish |
| talks a lot | talkative, chatty | communicative | gossipy, long-winded |

:::note title="Dictionary labels" tone=tip
Many dictionaries mark words as *approving*, *disapproving*, *formal*, *informal* or *literary*. Those labels are the connotation and the register written down. Get into the habit of reading them whenever you look up a word.
:::

:::qc
q: Which pair of words shares the same denotation but a different connotation?
options: ["slim and skinny", "hot and cold", "run and walk", "table and chair"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Both describe a thin body, but slim is usually a compliment and skinny is often unkind or neutral at best.
wrong: Hot and cold are opposites, run and walk are different actions, and table and chair are different objects. Slim and skinny describe the same thing with different feelings.
:::

## The substitution test {lvl=1 id=s-test label="Foundation"}

How can you tell whether a word is a good replacement? Try it. Replace the word and read the sentence again, slowly, asking three questions.

1. **Does it mean the same thing here?** Not in general, but in this sentence.
2. **Does it have the same part of speech?** A noun cannot stand in for an adjective.
3. **Does the grammar around it still work?** Some words need a particular preposition or sentence pattern.

For example, *The teacher praised the **brave** soldier.* Replace brave with **courageous** and everything fits. Replace it with **bravery** and the sentence breaks, because bravery is a noun, not an adjective.

Here is a harder case, a grammar trap.

- *The thief **stole** the jewellery.* (steal takes the thing taken)
- *The thief **robbed** the shop.* (rob takes the place or person robbed)

Both words are about taking things unlawfully, yet *The thief robbed the jewellery* is wrong. The two words want different objects after them.

:::qc
q: In "The farmer ___ his crops from the flood", which word fits and keeps the grammar correct?
options: ["saved", "rescue", "rescuing", "salvation"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Saved is a past tense verb that takes an object, just as the sentence needs. The other three are the wrong form or the wrong part of speech.
wrong: The gap needs a past tense verb. Rescue and rescuing are the wrong forms here, and salvation is a noun.
:::

## Reading context clues {lvl=1 id=s-clues label="Foundation"}

When a passage uses a word you do not know, the sentence often tells you its meaning in disguise.

| Clue type | What it looks like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Restating | the meaning is repeated in plainer words | *The soil was **barren**, unable to grow anything.* |
| Contrast | a word like but, however, unlike signals the opposite | *Unlike his **frugal** father, he spent money freely.* |
| Example | a list shows what the word covers | *She loves **pastimes**, such as chess, painting and gardening.* |
| Cause and effect | the result hints at the cause | *The **drought** left the fields cracked and the wells dry.* |

Once you guess a meaning, try the substitution test. If your guess slides into the sentence without changing anything, it is probably right.

## Where the board tests synonyms {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

In the Class 10 English paper, synonym skills appear mainly inside reading comprehension and the literature extracts. Questions typically ask you to find a word in the passage that means the same as, or opposite to, a word given in the question, and sometimes to choose the closest meaning of a word as it is used in the extract. [Unverified: marks and exact question forms vary from year to year; check the current sample paper.]

Three rules protect your marks.

1. **Copy the word exactly as it appears in the passage.** If the passage says "diligent", write "diligent", not "diligently" or "diligence".
2. **Match the part of speech.** If the given word is an adjective, your answer must be an adjective.
3. **Match the sense in the passage.** A word like "plain" can mean simple, clear or flat land. Read the sentence.

:::worked title="Finding the word in a passage"
Passage: *The villagers had grown weary of the long march, but the leader, a resolute woman, urged them on.*

Question: Find a word from the passage that means "determined".

Step 1: "determined" is an adjective, so look for an adjective.
Step 2: Scan the adjectives: weary (tired), long, resolute.
Step 3: Weary means tired, not determined. Resolute means firm in purpose.
Step 4: Substitute: "a determined woman" fits. Answer: **resolute**.
:::

:::qc
q: "The new teacher was quick to *grasp* the problem." Which word is closest in meaning to *grasp* as used here?
options: ["understand", "hold tightly", "seize", "grab"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Grasp a problem means to understand it. The other options are physical actions.
wrong: Grasp can mean to hold tightly, but you cannot hold a problem in your hand. Here it is used for the mind, so the meaning is understand.
:::

## The intensity ladder {lvl=2 id=s-ladder label="Class 10"}

Many synonyms are the same idea at different strengths. Putting them on a ladder helps you choose the right rung.

| Idea | Weaker ... stronger |
|---|---|
| anger | annoyed, irritated, angry, furious, livid, enraged |
| happiness | content, pleased, happy, delighted, thrilled, ecstatic |
| size (small) | small, little, tiny, minute, minuscule |
| tiredness | sleepy, tired, weary, exhausted, drained |
| fear | nervous, afraid, frightened, terrified, petrified |
| hunger | peckish, hungry, famished, starving |

Using a word too strong for the situation sounds comic or dishonest. "I was *terrified* of the quiz" would make the listener wonder whether you are exaggerating. Using one too weak undersells the situation: "I was a bit annoyed" when you had in fact been furious.

### Verbs of the same action

Verbs carry shades too, because the verb tells how the action was done.

| Action | Family of verbs |
|---|---|
| walk | stroll and amble (slow, relaxed), stride and march (firm, fast), trudge (heavy, tired), tiptoe (quiet), stagger (unsteady) |
| look | glance and glimpse (quick), peep (secret), stare and gaze (long), glare (angry), scan (searching) |
| laugh | smile, grin, chuckle (quiet), giggle (silly), guffaw (loud), sneer (cruel) |
| say | whisper, murmur, mutter (low), shout, yell, bellow (loud), stammer (broken) |

*He walked in* gives a fact. *He trudged in* gives the fact and the weariness, in one word. This is why good writers prefer a precise verb to a plain verb plus an adverb ("walked tiredly").

:::qc
q: Which word is the strongest in meaning?
options: ["livid", "annoyed", "cross", "irritated"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Livid is at the top of the anger ladder, while the others describe milder irritation.
wrong: Annoyed, cross and irritated all show mild to moderate anger. Livid describes intense, barely controlled fury.
:::

## Positive, neutral, negative {lvl=2 id=s-feel label="Class 10"}

Words about people are especially loaded. When you describe someone, you are always also giving an opinion.

| Positive | Neutral | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| slim, slender | thin | skinny, scrawny, gaunt |
| curious | inquisitive | nosy |
| confident | self-assured | cocky, arrogant |
| plain-spoken | direct | blunt, tactless |
| elderly | old | ancient, past it |
| aroma, fragrance | smell | stench, reek |

Two pairs are notorious for confusion.

- **Famous** (known by many, often for good reasons) and **notorious** (known for something bad): *a famous scientist*, *a notorious smuggler*.
- **Childlike** (innocent, curious: good) and **childish** (immature: bad): *her childlike wonder*, *his childish temper*.

A useful trick when you write is to ask whether you mean to praise, to describe or to blame, and then to choose from the matching column.

:::qc
q: Which sentence uses a word with a positive feeling?
options: ["The room smelled of fresh jasmine, a lovely aroma.", "The room had a stench of rotten fish.", "The room reeked of smoke.", "The room was full of fumes."]
answer: 0
right: Right. Aroma carries a pleasant feeling, while stench and reeked are negative and fumes is usually unpleasant.
wrong: Look for the word that suggests a pleasant smell. Stench, reeked and fumes all suggest something unpleasant.
:::

## Formal and informal words {lvl=2 id=s-register label="Class 10"}

The third thing a synonym carries is **register**: how formal it sounds. Formal letters, applications and reports prefer the formal column. Chats and speech prefer the everyday column.

| Everyday | Formal |
|---|---|
| buy | purchase |
| ask | enquire, request |
| start | commence |
| need | require |
| show | display, exhibit |
| get | obtain, receive |
| enough | sufficient |
| tell | inform |

In a formal letter, *I am writing to enquire about the vacancy* sounds right; *I am writing to ask about the job* sounds a bit casual. Equally, in a message to a friend, *I shall commence my homework* would sound as if you were joking.

:::note title="Do not overdo it" tone=warn
Big words do not make a good letter. If the formal word is one the reader has to pause over, the plain word is the better one. Aim for the word that fits the reader, not the word that impresses them.
:::

## One word, many senses {lvl=2 id=s-sense label="Class 10"}

Most words are **polysemous**: they have several related meanings. That means a word does not have one list of synonyms, it has a different list for each sense.

| Word | Sense in the sentence | Synonym |
|---|---|---|
| dull | a dull knife | blunt |
| dull | a dull lecture | boring, tedious |
| dull | a dull sky | overcast, grey |
| bright | a bright lamp | shining, brilliant |
| bright | a bright student | clever, intelligent |
| fast | a fast runner | quick, speedy |
| fast | a door held fast | firmly fixed, secure |
| plain | a plain meal | simple |
| plain | a plain answer | clear, direct |
| cheap | a cheap ticket | inexpensive |
| cheap | a cheap trick | mean, despicable |

So the first move is never "what is a synonym of dull?" but "what does dull mean here?"

:::qc
q: In which sentence does "fast" mean the same as "firmly fixed"?
options: ["The boat was stuck fast in the mud.", "She is a fast bowler.", "He ran fast to the bus.", "We fast on Ekadashi."]
answer: 0
right: Right. Stuck fast means held firmly. The other sentences use fast for speed or for going without food.
wrong: Check each sentence. Fast bowler and ran fast are about speed, and to fast is to go without food. Only "stuck fast" means firmly held.
:::

## A synonym inside a bigger sentence {lvl=2 id=s-practice label="Class 10"}

A short routine for questions that ask you to pick or supply a synonym:

1. Read the whole sentence, not just the word.
2. Name the part of speech and the sense.
3. List two or three candidates.
4. Run the substitution test on each.
5. Keep the one that also fits the feeling and the register.

:::worked title="Choosing between three candidates"
Sentence: *The manager was ___ in his refusal to change the rule, even when everyone asked him to.*

Candidates: determined, obstinate, firm.

All three suggest holding to a position. Check the feeling: "even when everyone asked him to" implies he was unreasonably firm, which is a criticism. *Determined* is usually approving. *Firm* is neutral. *Obstinate* is disapproving. Best fit: **obstinate**.
:::

## Beyond the board: where words come from {lvl=3 id=s-roots label="Beyond the board"}

Why do the synonym columns feel different? Often because of their origin. [Unverified: patterns summarised from memory.]

- **Germanic words** (ask, help, end, house) are short and everyday. They are the words you learn first.
- **French words** (question, aid, finish, mansion) often feel polite or courtly.
- **Latin and Greek words** (interrogate, assist, conclude, domicile) often feel learned, technical or official.

This is only a tendency. It helps explain why legal and scientific writing is full of long Latin-derived words, while plain short words often feel more direct in speeches.

A useful habit: if two synonyms feel different and you cannot say why, look at the dictionary's origin note. It often reveals the layer.

## Beyond the board: euphemism and spin {lvl=3 id=s-euph label="Beyond the board"}

A **euphemism** replaces a harsh word with a softer one. Often it is kind: *passed away* for died, *visually impaired* for blind. Sometimes it hides the truth: a company "releases talent" or "rightsizes" when it sends people home from their jobs, and a product is "pre-owned" when it is second-hand.

The opposite also exists. A **dysphemism** replaces a neutral word with a harsh one on purpose, to insult or to shock.

Reading carefully means asking what the word is doing.

| Plain event | Softened | Harsher |
|---|---|---|
| to die | pass away, depart | kick the bucket |
| to fire an employee | let go, downsize | sack, boot out |
| a small flat | compact, cosy | cramped, poky |
| an old car | pre-owned, vintage | wreck, banger |

When you meet a soft phrase in a report or advertisement, translate it back to the plain version and see whether the message changes.

## Beyond the board: precision and variety {lvl=3 id=s-precise label="Beyond the board"}

Two opposite mistakes spoil writing.

**Repetition.** Using *good* eleven times in a paragraph tires the reader. A richer word, chosen for the exact shade, solves it: a *generous* gift, a *sound* argument, a *skilful* player.

**Elegant variation.** The opposite mistake is changing a word just to avoid repeating it. In a science report, if you call a liquid "the solution" in one sentence, then "the fluid", then "the mixture", the reader will wonder if you mean three different things. In technical or legal writing, a fixed term should stay fixed. (The phrase *elegant variation* was used by the style writer H. W. Fowler as a criticism. [Unverified: attribution from memory.])

| Situation | Better practice |
|---|---|
| Story, descriptive piece | Vary and choose precise words |
| Science, law, instructions | Keep one term for one thing |
| Argument or essay | Vary for style, but never in a way that changes the claim |

:::qc
q: A science report calls the same liquid "the solution", then "the fluid", then "the mixture". What is the main problem?
options: ["The reader may think three different things are meant.", "The words are too simple.", "The report uses too few adjectives.", "The words are all spelled wrongly."]
answer: 0
right: Right. In technical writing a changed word signals a changed thing, so keep one term for one thing.
wrong: Solution, fluid and mixture are not interchangeable in science. Switching between them makes the reader doubt what is being described.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A synonym has nearly the same meaning as another word in a given sense, but almost never exactly the same. {lvl=1}
2. Every word has a denotation (its plain meaning) and a connotation (its feeling): thrifty and stingy share the first and differ in the second. {lvl=1}
3. Use the substitution test: the new word must fit the meaning, the part of speech and the grammar of the sentence. {lvl=1}
4. Context clues (restating, contrast, example, cause and effect) reveal the meaning of unknown words. {lvl=1}
5. In passage questions, copy the word exactly as written, match the part of speech and match the sense in the passage. {lvl=2}
6. Many synonyms form an intensity ladder (annoyed, angry, furious, livid); pick the rung that fits the situation. {lvl=2}
7. Synonyms carry a positive, neutral or negative feeling: slim, thin, skinny; famous, notorious; childlike, childish. {lvl=2}
8. Register: use formal words (purchase, commence) in formal writing and everyday words in speech and informal writing. {lvl=2}
9. One word has different synonyms for different senses (dull: blunt, boring, overcast); decide the sense first. {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: origin affects feeling, euphemisms soften and can hide the truth, and technical writing needs one fixed term, not elegant variation. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Treating two synonyms as perfectly interchangeable: always check the feeling and the strength. {lvl=1}
