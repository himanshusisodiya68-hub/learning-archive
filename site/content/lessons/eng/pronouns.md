## Why pronouns exist {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Imagine a language with no pronouns. A story would sound like this:

*Asha lost Asha's pen, so Asha borrowed Meera's pen from Meera, and Asha thanked Meera.*

Nobody speaks or writes that way. English has a small set of stand-in words so that a noun is named once and then pointed back to:

*Asha lost her pen, so she borrowed Meera's and thanked her.*

That is the whole idea. A **pronoun** is a word used in place of a noun (or a noun phrase) that has already been named, or that the speaker and listener both understand. The noun a pronoun stands for is called its **antecedent** ("the one that came before").

This gives pronouns two jobs, and every rule in this lesson comes from one of them.

1. **Point to the right noun.** The pronoun must match its antecedent in number (one or many), person (speaker, listener, someone else) and gender. This is called **agreement**.
2. **Do the right job in its own sentence.** A noun like *Asha* does not change whether it is the doer or the receiver. A pronoun does: *she* is the doer, *her* is the receiver. This change of form is called **case**.

Keep these two questions in mind. When a pronoun feels wrong, one of the two has failed.

## The kinds of pronoun {lvl=1 id=s-kinds label="Foundation"}

Grammar books sort pronouns by what they do. You do not need to memorise the names for their own sake, but they help you pick the right form.

| Kind | What it does | Words | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal | Stands for a person or thing by role in the conversation | I, me, we, us, you, he, him, she, her, it, they, them | *They told us the news.* |
| Possessive | Shows who owns something | mine, ours, yours, his, hers, theirs (and its) | *This pencil is mine.* |
| Reflexive | Turns the action back on the doer | myself, ourselves, yourself, yourselves, himself, herself, itself, themselves | *She taught herself chess.* |
| Emphatic | Same words, added for stress | the same list | *I myself saw the accident.* |
| Demonstrative | Points to something | this, that, these, those | *Those are my shoes.* |
| Interrogative | Asks a question | who, whom, whose, which, what | *Who broke the vase?* |
| Relative | Joins a clause to a noun it describes | who, whom, whose, which, that | *The boy who sat beside me was kind.* |
| Indefinite | Stands for no particular person or thing | someone, anybody, everyone, nobody, something, all, some, few, many, one, none | *Nobody knew the answer.* |
| Distributive | Treats the members of a group one at a time | each, either, neither | *Each of the girls got a medal.* |
| Reciprocal | Shows two or more doing the same to each other | each other, one another | *The twins help each other.* |

:::note title="Same word, different kind" tone=tip
Many words are pronouns only when they stand alone. In *This is my book* the word this is a pronoun. In *This book is mine* it describes the noun book, so it is acting as an adjective. The kind depends on the job in that sentence, not on the word itself. The same applies to *who* (interrogative in *Who called?*, relative in *the man who called*).
:::

## Personal pronouns and case {lvl=1 id=s-personal label="Foundation"}

Case is the form a pronoun takes according to its job. English keeps three working cases for personal pronouns.

| Person | Subject (doer) | Object (receiver) | Possessive before a noun | Possessive standing alone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st singular | I | me | my | mine |
| 1st plural | we | us | our | ours |
| 2nd (both) | you | you | your | yours |
| 3rd masc. | he | him | his | his |
| 3rd fem. | she | her | her | hers |
| 3rd neuter | it | it | its | (its) |
| 3rd plural | they | them | their | theirs |

(First person is the speaker, second is the listener, third is everyone and everything else.)

**Subject form** goes where a noun would be the doer: *She sings. They arrived.* **Object form** goes where a noun would be the receiver of the action, or after a preposition: *Ravi called her. The gift is for them.*

### The test for compound phrases

Mistakes mostly appear when two people are joined with *and*. The trick is to drop the other person and read the sentence with just the pronoun.

- *Me and Ravi went to the market.* Drop Ravi: *Me went to the market.* Wrong. So: *Ravi and I went to the market.*
- *The teacher praised Ravi and I.* Drop Ravi: *The teacher praised I.* Wrong. So: *The teacher praised Ravi and me.*
- *This is a secret between you and I.* Drop you: *between I.* Wrong, because between is a preposition. So: *between you and me.*

The reverse mistake is also common. People learn that "me and Ravi" is wrong and then overcorrect to "I" everywhere, even after a preposition. "Between you and I" and "for Ravi and I" are the classic cases.

### Possessives: with and without an apostrophe

A possessive pronoun already shows ownership, so it never takes an apostrophe.

- Wrong: *The decision is their's. The book is your's. The cat licked it's paw.*
- Right: *The decision is theirs. The book is yours. The cat licked its paw.*

**Its** is the possessive. **It's** is only a short form of *it is* or *it has*. If you can replace the word with *it is* and the sentence still works, use *it's*.

Also notice the two columns of possessives. **My, our, your, her, their** come before a noun (*my bag*). **Mine, ours, yours, hers, theirs** stand without one (*the bag is mine*). Never mix them: *This is mine book* and *The bag is my* are both wrong.

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["The dog wagged it's tail.", "The dog wagged its tail.", "The dog wagged its' tail."]
answer: 1
right: Right. Its is the possessive and takes no apostrophe.
wrong: It's means it is. The possessive of it is its, with no apostrophe at all.
:::

## Reflexive and emphatic pronouns {lvl=1 id=s-reflexive label="Foundation"}

A **reflexive pronoun** is used when the doer and the receiver of an action are the same person. It "bends back" (the word reflexive means this).

- *She cut herself while slicing vegetables.* (the cutter and the one cut are the same)
- *We enjoyed ourselves at the fair.*

The form is built as follows: my, our, your + self/selves (myself, ourselves, yourself, yourselves), and him, her, it, them + self/selves (himself, herself, itself, themselves). Note two forms are not words: *hisself* and *theirselves* are wrong.

The same words are used for **emphasis**. Remove them and the sentence still stands.

- *I myself saw the accident.* (I and nobody else)
- *The principal herself gave the prize.*

Two rules keep these safe:

1. A reflexive pronoun needs a doer to bend back to. It cannot be the subject by itself: *Myself Ravi, I study in Class 10* is wrong. Say *I am Ravi, and I study in Class 10.*
2. Do not use a reflexive where an ordinary object pronoun is needed: *He gave the book to Neha and myself* is wrong; *to Neha and me* is right.

### Verbs that do not need the reflexive

In many languages you "wash yourself" or "dress yourself" as a matter of course. English usually drops it when the meaning is clear: *He washed and dressed.* *She sat down.* Keep the reflexive when it adds sense: *He taught himself to swim.* *Don't blame yourself.*

## Matching the noun: agreement {lvl=2 id=s-agree label="Class 10"}

A pronoun must match its antecedent in number, person and gender.

- *The girls left their bags in the van.* (plural, plural)
- *Neha lost her umbrella.* (singular feminine, singular feminine)
- *The company announced its results.* (a company is treated as one thing)

### Indefinite pronouns are singular

Some pronouns feel plural because they point to many people, but grammatically they are singular. Verbs and later pronouns follow the singular.

| Always singular | Examples |
|---|---|
| each, either, neither | *Each of the boys has a ticket. Neither of the answers is correct.* |
| everyone, everybody, everything | *Everyone is waiting.* |
| someone, anyone, no one, nobody | *Nobody was hurt.* |
| one | *One must respect one's elders.* |

The words that follow *each of* and *neither of* are plural nouns (*of the boys*), which tempts students to write *have*. The subject is each or neither, so the verb is singular: *has, is*.

A few words depend on what they refer to. *All, some, any, none* are singular when the noun is a mass (*All of the milk has been used*) and plural when it is a count (*All of the books have been returned*). Traditional school grammar often treats **none** as singular (*None of the answers is correct*), but modern usage accepts a plural verb. [Unverified] which of the two a particular board key prefers; the safe course in an exam is to follow the form your textbook shows.

### The pronoun after the indefinite

*Everyone must bring their own lunch* is now common in speech and writing, because English has no single neutral word for he or she. Traditional exam keys, however, prefer a pronoun that matches the singular strictly: *his or her*, or *his* if the group is known to be all boys.

- A group of boys only: *Each of the boys collected his certificate.*
- A mixed or unknown group: *Each student must bring his or her own book.* Many writers avoid the clumsiness by recasting in the plural: *All students must bring their own books.*

[Inference] The recasting trick is safe in any exam because it avoids the singular-they debate entirely. Whether a particular examiner accepts singular *their* is [Unverified].

### Nearest antecedent

If a pronoun can refer to two nouns, the reader will take the nearer one. *The wire touched the pipe, and it caught fire.* (the wire or the pipe?) This is the reason for the ambiguity warnings later in this lesson.

## The order of pronouns {lvl=2 id=s-order label="Class 10"}

School grammar has a convention for the order when pronouns of different persons come together.

- Normally: second person, third person, first person. *You, he and I will go.* The mnemonic is 2-3-1.
- When admitting a fault or something unpleasant: first person first. *I, you and he were late.* The mnemonic is 1-2-3.
- Plural: first person, second, third. *We, you and they shall meet.*

This is an Indian school-grammar convention of courtesy (putting yourself last), not a rule of English grammar. [Unverified] how strictly a given board marks it. It is useful for composition and some sentence-ordering questions.

For nouns and pronouns together, the pronoun *I* also goes last: *Ravi and I*, not *I and Ravi*.

## Relative pronouns: who, whom, whose, which, that {lvl=2 id=s-relative label="Class 10"}

A **relative pronoun** does two things at once: it stands for a noun that has just been named, and it joins a clause to that noun. *I met a boy who plays the sitar.* Here who stands for boy and joins "plays the sitar" to it.

| Word | Refers to | Case / job | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| who | people | subject | *The girl who won is my cousin.* |
| whom | people | object | *The girl whom we met is my cousin.* |
| whose | people (and sometimes things) | possession | *The girl whose bag was stolen is crying.* |
| which | things and animals | subject or object | *The bag which was stolen was blue.* |
| that | people, things, animals | subject or object, no comma | *The bag that she lost was blue.* |

### Who or whom

Who does the action in its own clause; whom receives it, or follows a preposition. A reliable test is to answer the clause with he/she or him/her.

- *The boy (who/whom) won the race is my neighbour.* Answer: *he won.* So **who**.
- *The boy (who/whom) we praised is my neighbour.* Answer: *we praised him.* So **whom**.
- *To (who/whom) did you give the key?* Answer: *to him.* So **whom**.

In everyday speech people say *Who did you meet?* and *the boy who we praised*. Board exercises and formal writing still expect whom in these places, so use it in exams.

### That or which: two kinds of clause

A **defining clause** says which one you mean. Remove it and the sentence loses its point. It takes no comma, and *that* or *who/which* fits. *The book that I borrowed is torn.* (This book, as against the others.)

A **non-defining clause** adds extra information. Remove it and the main sentence still makes sense. It is set off by commas, and it takes *which* (or who), but not *that*. *My new book, which I borrowed from Ravi, is torn.*

- Wrong: *The movie, that we watched yesterday, was long.*
- Right: *The movie, which we watched yesterday, was long.* / *The movie that we watched yesterday was long.*

### When that is preferred

After superlatives, the words *all, only, same, any, no, every* and after both a person and a thing together, *that* is the usual choice. *It is the best film that I have seen. All that glitters is not gold. He spoke of the boy and the dog that had been lost.*

### Who, which and prepositions

A relative pronoun after a preposition has a formal pattern. *The house in which we lived* (formal) is the same as *The house that we lived in* (everyday). With people the formal pattern needs whom: *The man to whom I spoke*; the everyday one: *The man I spoke to.* Never write *the man to who I spoke.*

:::qc
q: "She is the teacher ___ I admire most."
options: ["who", "whom", "whose", "which"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The pronoun is the object of admire (I admire her), so whom is the formal choice.
wrong: Answer the clause: I admire her. Her is the object form, so the relative pronoun takes the object form, whom.
:::

:::qc
q: Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
options: ["My brother, that lives in Pune, is a doctor.", "My brother, who lives in Pune, is a doctor.", "My brother who lives in Pune, is a doctor."]
answer: 1
right: Right. The clause adds extra information about a person already identified, so it takes commas and who, not that.
wrong: A non-defining clause is set off by commas on both sides and cannot use that. For a person, the choice is who.
:::

## Demonstrative, interrogative and other pronouns {lvl=2 id=s-others label="Class 10"}

**Demonstrative: this, that, these, those.** This and these point to what is near, that and those to what is far. Use the plural for plural: *These are my books. Those are yours.* Mistakes: *This are my friends* (should be *These*), *Those book* (the word those is an adjective here and needs a plural noun: those books).

**Interrogative: who, whom, whose, which, what.** *Who* asks about a person as doer, *whom* as receiver, *whose* about ownership, *which* about a choice from a known set, *what* about an open set.

- *Which of the two shirts do you prefer?* (known set)
- *What do you want for breakfast?* (open choice)

**Distributive: each, either, neither.** Each means every one of a group, taken individually. Either and neither refer to two. *Either of the two roads leads to the lake.* (one or the other) *Neither of the roads is safe.* For more than two, use *any one of* and *none of*.

**Reciprocal: each other, one another.** Traditional grammar uses *each other* for two and *one another* for more than two: *The twins help each other. The students respected one another.* [Unverified] how strictly an examiner marks the difference; modern usage often mixes them.

**One** as a pronoun means any person in general: *One must obey the law.* Keep it consistent: *One should do one's duty*, not *One should do his duty.* This is formal, and not common in speech.

## Pronouns in the board's questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

Pronoun questions appear in three forms.

1. **Error correction.** A line in a passage has a wrong pronoun: a wrong case (*me and him went*), a wrong possessive (*its' wheels*), a wrong reflexive (*hisself*), or a mismatch of number (*Everyone brought their...*). Check each pronoun for its antecedent and its job.
2. **Gap filling.** Fill in a pronoun; the options will contain the subject, object and possessive forms of the same word (I, me, my, mine). Decide the job first: doer, receiver, owner before a noun, or owner standing alone.
3. **Sentence combining.** Join two sentences with a relative pronoun, or rewrite a sentence to remove a pronoun problem.

A routine that works:

- Underline each pronoun.
- Name its antecedent. Does it agree in number, person and gender?
- Name its job in its clause: subject, object, after a preposition, owner.
- For compounds, drop the other person and test the pronoun alone.
- Check apostrophes (*its* and *it's*, *theirs*, *yours*).

:::worked title="Six errors in four lines"
Find and correct the pronoun errors: *Me and my brother went to the fair. We spent all our pocket money on rides, and there was nothing left for hisself. The man whom sold us the tickets smiled at we two. Its a day I will never forget.*

1. *Me and my brother* is a subject, so it takes I: **My brother and I went to the fair.**
2. *hisself* is not a word, and the antecedent is *we* (both boys), so the reflexive must be **ourselves**, not himself.
3. *whom sold* fails the who/whom test. He sold the tickets, so he is the doer: **who**.
4. *smiled at we two* follows a preposition, so the object form is needed: **us two**.
5. *Its a day* means it is a day, so it needs the apostrophe: **It's**.

Five corrections. Note that (2) is the antecedent test, not the case test: the pronoun must match *we*, not *my brother*.
:::

## Beyond the board: finer points {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### Unclear reference

The antecedent should be obvious. A pronoun pointing to two possible nouns, or to none, confuses the reader.

- *Rita told Seema that she had won.* (Rita or Seema?) Repair: *Rita told Seema, "You have won."* or *Rita told Seema that Seema had won.*
- *In the report, it says the plant was closed.* (what says?) Repair: *The report says the plant was closed.*
- *Ravi failed the test, which surprised everyone.* (which stands for the whole fact; it is acceptable in speech, but be sure the meaning is clear).

When revising, read each pronoun and ask whether a reader who was not in your head would know what it stands for.

### The pronoun before a gerund

When an -ing word acts like a noun (a gerund), the pronoun in front of it traditionally takes the possessive form. *I was surprised at his coming so early.* *Please excuse my being late.* Compare *I saw him coming* (him is the object of saw, and coming describes it). [Unverified] how often modern usage keeps this distinction; in an exam, follow the possessive form for the noun-like -ing.

### After as and than

A pronoun after *than* or *as* takes the case it would have in the clause that is understood.

- *She is taller than I.* (than I am tall). Formal. *Than me* is common in speech, and most dictionaries record it as acceptable, but a formal key may mark it wrong.
- *He likes you more than me.* (more than he likes me.) *He likes you more than I.* (more than I like you.) The case changes the meaning.

### It is I

*It is I* is the traditional formal pattern (the verb to be links two equal things, so the same case on both sides). *It is me* is the spoken norm. For the board, write *It is I* or *It was he who...* only if the key demands it; the safe choice is to recast and avoid the issue (*I am the one who...*).

### Whoever and whomever

The same who/whom test works on the clause inside a longer sentence. The case is set by the job inside its own clause, not by the main sentence. *Give the prize to whoever finishes first.* (Whoever is the doer of finishes.) *Give the prize to whomever the judges choose.* (the judges choose him.)

### The generic they

When we do not know the gender or want to avoid choosing one, many writers now use singular *they*: *Someone left their umbrella.* It is centuries old in English use and appears in major style guides. [Inference] Examination keys are slower to adopt it; prefer recasting in the plural when marks depend on it.

### Pronouns and politeness

English has one form of *you* for every listener, unlike Hindi's *tu, tum, aap*. The distinction of respect is carried by tone, titles and phrasing (*Sir, would you...?*). This is a place where a translation from Hindi can sound too blunt. A useful composition habit: choose the polite frame rather than relying on the pronoun.

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A pronoun replaces a noun already named (its antecedent) so that names need not be repeated. {lvl=1}
2. Two checks catch most errors: agreement with the antecedent (number, person, gender) and case for the job in its own clause. {lvl=1}
3. Subject forms (I, we, he, she, they) go where a noun would be the doer; object forms (me, us, him, her, them) go after verbs and prepositions. {lvl=1}
4. For compounds like "Ravi and me/I", drop the other person and test the pronoun alone. {lvl=1}
5. My, our, your, her, their come before a noun; mine, ours, yours, hers, theirs stand alone. Possessive pronouns take no apostrophe: its, yours, theirs. {lvl=1}
6. Reflexive pronouns bend the action back to the doer (myself, himself, themselves). There is no hisself or theirselves. {lvl=1}
7. Each, either, neither, everyone, everybody, someone, anybody and nobody are singular. {lvl=2}
8. Who is for the doer, whom for the receiver or after a preposition, whose for ownership. {lvl=2}
9. Defining clauses take no commas and may use that; non-defining clauses take commas and which (or who), never that. {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: avoid unclear reference, use the possessive before a gerund, set who/whom by the job inside its own clause, and recast to dodge the singular-they debate in exams. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "Me and Ravi went" should be "Ravi and I went": test with the pronoun alone. {lvl=1}
- "Between you and I", "for you and I": after a preposition, use me. {lvl=1}
- "it's" for the possessive, "their's", "your's": possessive pronouns have no apostrophe. {lvl=1}
- "hisself", "theirselves", "Myself Ravi": reflexive pronouns need a doer and the correct forms. {lvl=1}
- "This is mine book", "The book is my": my before a noun, mine standing alone. {lvl=1}
- "Each of the girls have their book": each and neither are singular and take has, is. {lvl=2}
- "The boy who we praised", "the girl whom won": who for the doer, whom for the receiver. {lvl=2}
- "The movie, that I saw, was long": a clause set off by commas takes which, not that. {lvl=2}
- Putting I first ("I and Ravi") or ignoring the 2-3-1 order. {lvl=2}
- A pronoun that could refer to two nouns: repeat the noun or recast the sentence. {lvl=3}
- Forcing "whom" into spoken English or writing "than me" in a strict formal key without checking what is expected. {lvl=3}
:::
