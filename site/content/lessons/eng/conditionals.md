## Why English needs "what if" sentences {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Humans do not only describe what is. We also reason about what could be: "If I study, I will pass." "If I were taller, I could reach that shelf." "If you had told me, I would have helped." Without such sentences we could not plan, warn, regret or promise.

A conditional sentence joins two ideas with a hinge:

- the **condition** (the if-clause): the situation being imagined;
- the **result** (the main clause): what follows if the condition is true.

*If the bell rings, the class ends.* Here the bell is the condition and the class ending is the result. The if-clause can come first (with a comma) or second (no comma): *The class ends if the bell rings.*

The interesting part is what the verbs do. English signals **how real** the condition is by changing the verb form. This is the first-principles idea behind the whole topic:

| How real is the condition? | What the verbs do |
|---|---|
| A fact that is always true, or a real possibility | Stay in the present: the situation is close to reality |
| Imagined, unlikely or contrary to the present | Step one tense back (present becomes past): distance from reality |
| Imagined about the past, now impossible to change | Step two tenses back (past perfect): distance from reality, and from the present |

So the "past" in a conditional is often not about time. It marks distance from what is actually true. Keep that single idea in mind and the four types become easy to rebuild whenever you forget them.

## The four basic types {lvl=1 id=s-types label="Foundation"}

| Type | If-clause | Main clause | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero | present simple | present simple | a general truth or a rule |
| First | present simple | will + base verb | a real, likely future |
| Second | past simple | would + base verb | unreal or unlikely, now or in the future |
| Third | past perfect | would have + V3 | unreal past, can no longer change |

### Zero conditional: always true

Here *if* can be replaced by *whenever* without changing the meaning. Both clauses are in the present simple.

- *If you heat ice, it melts.*
- *If you press this button, the lift comes.*
- *Plants die if they get no water.*

### First conditional: a real possibility

The speaker thinks the condition may well happen. It is used for plans, warnings, promises and threats.

- *If it rains tomorrow, we will stay at home.*
- *If you touch that wire, you will get a shock.*
- *I will help you if you ask me.*

Remember the earlier rule from the tenses lesson: **no will in the if-clause**. *If it will rain* is wrong; *if it rains* is right.

### Second conditional: imagined, unlikely or untrue now

The speaker pictures something that is not true at present or is very unlikely to happen. The verb in the if-clause looks like the past, but the time is now or the future.

- *If I had a car, I would drive you to the station.* (I do not have a car.)
- *If she lived nearer, she would visit us daily.* (She does not live nearer.)
- *If I won a lottery, I would buy a school.* (Unlikely.)

**Were for every person.** In careful English the verb *be* becomes *were* for all subjects, including *I*, *he*, *she* and *it*:

- *If I were you, I would apologise.*
- *If the shop were closer, we would walk.*

*Was* is common in speech, but exam keys and formal writing prefer *were*. The classic advice sentence, *If I were you, I would ...*, always uses *were*.

### Third conditional: imagined past

The speaker looks back at something that did not happen and imagines the result. It often expresses regret or criticism.

- *If she had studied harder, she would have passed.* (She did not study harder; she did not pass.)
- *If we had left earlier, we would not have missed the bus.*
- *I would have helped you if you had asked me.*

:::note title="Reading the tenses back" tone=tip
Think of a staircase. Real condition: stand on the present. One step back (past simple) gives the imagined present. Two steps back (past perfect) gives the imagined past. The main clause with *would* just mirrors the step: *would + base* for imagined now, *would have + V3* for imagined past.
:::

## Choosing the right type {lvl=1 id=s-choose label="Foundation"}

Ask two questions in this order.

1. **Is the condition real or imagined?** If it is a fact or a real possibility, use zero or first. If it is imagined or untrue, use second or third.
2. **Is it about the past?** If it is imagined and about the past, use the third. Otherwise use the second.

| Situation | Type | Sentence |
|---|---|---|
| A fact or rule | zero | *If you heat water to 100 degrees Celsius at sea level, it boils.* |
| A plan for tomorrow | first | *If the weather is fine, we will go on a picnic.* |
| A dream or advice | second | *If I had more time, I would learn the violin.* |
| A regret | third | *If I had listened, I would not have made this mistake.* |

Quick test for the first versus the second: does the speaker think it is *likely*? "If I get the job, I will move to Pune" (I might get it) is first. "If I got the job, I would move to Pune" (it is unlikely, or I am only imagining) is second. The speaker's attitude decides, not the clock.

## Other words that make conditions {lvl=2 id=s-words label="Class 10"}

Several words do the work of *if* with a twist of meaning.

### Unless

**Unless** means *if ... not*. It brings the idea of an exception.

- *Unless you hurry, you will miss the train.* = *If you do not hurry, you will miss the train.*
- *I will not go unless you come with me.* = *I will not go if you do not come with me.*

Unless already carries the negative, so the verb after it is **positive**. *Unless you do not hurry* is a double negative and is wrong.

Unless is usually used with the first conditional. It is rarely used with the second and third because it sounds awkward there; use *if ... not*.

### Others

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| provided / providing (that) | only if | *You may go out provided that you finish your homework.* |
| as long as, so long as | only if | *You can borrow it as long as you return it soon.* |
| in case | because it may happen | *Take an umbrella in case it rains.* |
| otherwise | if not (starts a new sentence or follows a semicolon) | *Hurry; otherwise you will miss the bus.* |
| suppose, supposing | what if | *Suppose you missed the bus, what would you do?* |
| but for, without | if it were not for / had not been for | *But for your help, I would have failed.* |

*In case* does not mean *if*. "Take an umbrella in case it rains" means take it now to be ready; "Take an umbrella if it rains" would mean take it only after the rain starts, which makes no sense.

### Could, might, should in the result clause

*Would* is not the only choice. Other modals change the sense slightly.

- *If you work hard, you can succeed.* (possibility or ability)
- *If you worked hard, you might succeed.* (a weaker chance)
- *If the lift were working, we could go up quickly.*
- *If you see him, you should tell him.*

Keep the if-clause pattern the same; only the modal in the result changes.

### Imperatives in the result

The result can be a command: *If you see Raj, tell him to call me.* *If you feel dizzy, sit down.* The if-clause stays in the present simple.

## Mixed conditionals {lvl=2 id=s-mixed label="Class 10"}

Sometimes the past of the condition and the time of the result differ. Then the two clauses come from different types.

- **Past cause, present result**: *If I had taken the medicine, I would be well now.* (If-clause: past perfect; result: would + base.)
- **Present cause, past result**: *If she were more careful, she would not have broken the vase.* (If-clause: past simple; result: would have + V3.)

The rule: build each clause on its own timeline. Ask, "When was the condition? When is the result?" Then use the matching form for each.

:::worked title="From two plain facts to a mixed conditional"
Facts: *He did not study medicine. He is not a doctor now.*

The condition is in the past (did not study), and the result is in the present (is not a doctor). So the if-clause takes the past perfect and the result takes would + base verb: *If he had studied medicine, he would be a doctor now.*

Check by reversing: the original facts are both negative; the imagined sentence is both positive. That is how a conditional always works: the unreal version flips the facts.
:::

## Wishes and "as if" {lvl=2 id=s-wish label="Class 10"}

A wish is a conditional in disguise. It pictures something unreal, so it uses the same backward step.

| Meaning | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Regret about the present | wish + past simple (were) | *I wish I were taller.* (I am not tall.) |
| Regret about the past | wish + past perfect | *I wish I had listened to my teacher.* |
| Wanting a change in the future | wish + would | *I wish it would stop raining.* |

*If only* is a stronger version: *If only I knew the answer!* *If only she had called!*

*As if* and *as though* compare with an unreal situation and also take the past forms: *He talks as if he knew everything.* (He does not.) *She looked as though she had seen a ghost.* When the comparison is actually true, the verb stays normal: *It looks as if it is going to rain* (a real possibility).

Similarly *it is time* is followed by the past: *It is time we left.* (not *we leave*).

:::qc
q: "I wish I ___ how to swim." (I cannot swim.)
options: ["knew", "know", "will know", "had known"]
answer: 0
right: Right. A wish about the present uses the past simple, so knew.
wrong: The wish is about the present, so use the past simple. The past perfect would be a regret about the past.
:::

## Conditionals in the board's questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

Conditionals appear in these ways.

1. **Gap filling or multiple choice**: choose the verb forms. Find the result clause: *will* gives the first, *would* the second, *would have* the third.
2. **Error correction**: typical errors are *will* after *if*, *would* after *if*, a missing *have* in *would have*, and *unless ... not*.
3. **Sentence transformation**:
   - *if* to *unless* (and back);
   - two plain sentences into a conditional;
   - active conditionals into inverted ones (a beyond-the-board skill);
   - direct speech into reported speech, where conditionals backshift.

### Rewriting with unless

To change *if ... not* into *unless*, remove the *not* and flip nothing else.

- *If you do not work hard, you will fail.* becomes *Unless you work hard, you will fail.*
- *Unless she apologises, I will not speak to her.* becomes *If she does not apologise, I will not speak to her.*

### Combining plain sentences

Look at the facts, then flip them.

- *He is not rich. He cannot buy a yacht.* becomes *If he were rich, he could buy a yacht.* (Second.)
- *She did not hurry. She missed the bus.* becomes *If she had hurried, she would not have missed the bus.* (Third.)
- *Hurry up, or you will miss the train.* becomes *If you do not hurry, you will miss the train* or *Unless you hurry, you will miss the train.* (First.)

:::worked title="Four errors in thirty seconds"
Correct each: (a) *If I will find your pen, I will give it to you.* (b) *If I would be you, I would speak to him.* (c) *If he had called, I would told him.* (d) *Unless you do not stop, I will complain.*

(a) *will find* to *find*: no will in the if-clause. (b) *would be* to *were*: the if-clause of a second conditional takes the past form. (c) *would told* to *would have told*: the third conditional needs would have + V3. (d) *do not stop* to *stop*: unless already means if not.
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "If it will rain, the match will be cancelled."
options: ["will rain should be rains", "will be cancelled should be would be cancelled", "the match should be a match"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The if-clause of a first conditional takes the present simple.
wrong: The main clause is fine. The error is in the if-clause, where English uses the present simple for a real future.
:::

## Beyond the board: inversion and finer shades {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### Dropping if: inversion

Formal writing can leave out *if* and move the helper verb before the subject.

| With if | Without if |
|---|---|
| *If I had known, I would have helped.* | *Had I known, I would have helped.* |
| *If I were you, I would wait.* | *Were I you, I would wait.* |
| *If you should need help, call me.* | *Should you need help, call me.* |

Only *had*, *were* and *should* can do this. Negatives keep *not* after the subject: *Had she not left, ...* The result is more formal and slightly literary.

### Will and would inside the if-clause

Normally there is no will or would after *if*. There are two exceptions.

- **Politeness or a request**: *If you will wait a moment, I will call the manager.* (Meaning: please wait.)
- **Willingness or refusal**: *If he will not listen, nothing can be done.* (If he refuses to listen.)

These are about willingness, not about future time.

### Were to

*If you were to ...* makes an imagined situation sound polite or remote: *If you were to join us, we would be delighted.* It is a gentle second conditional.

### But for and without

*But for* and *without* replace a whole if-clause with a noun: *But for the rain, we would have played.* = *If it had not been for the rain, we would have played.* Both need the matching main clause (would have) for the past.

### Conditions in writing

Conditionals do real work in formal letters and reports: *If you could send me the form by Friday, I would be grateful.* The second conditional sounds more courteous than *If you can send ... I will be grateful*, because the distance from reality softens the request. This effect is not a rule but a convention of style.

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A conditional sentence joins a condition (if-clause) and a result. Verb forms show how real the condition is. {lvl=1}
2. Zero: if + present, present (always true). First: if + present, will (likely future). {lvl=1}
3. Second: if + past, would + base (unreal or unlikely now). Third: if + past perfect, would have + V3 (unreal past). {lvl=1}
4. The past tense in the second conditional marks distance from reality, not past time. Use *were* for all persons in formal English. {lvl=1}
5. Never put *will* or *would* directly after *if* in the ordinary types. {lvl=2}
6. Unless = if ... not; the verb after unless is positive. {lvl=2}
7. In case, provided that, as long as, otherwise, but for and without each add a shade of condition; in case is not if. {lvl=2}
8. Mixed conditionals build each clause on its own timeline: past condition with present result, or the other way round. {lvl=2}
9. I wish, if only, as if and it is time take the backshifted form: past simple for the present, past perfect for the past. {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: inversion (Had I known, Were I you, Should you need), polite *if you will*, and *were to*. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "If it will rain": use the present simple after if for a real future: "If it rains". {lvl=1}
- "If I would have money": the if-clause of a second conditional takes the past, not would: "If I had money". {lvl=1}
- "If she studied, she would have passed": the two clauses must match; the third conditional needs "had studied". {lvl=1}
- Leaving out have in "would have": "would told" should be "would have told". {lvl=1}
- "If I was you" in exam answers: write "If I were you". {lvl=2}
- "Unless you do not ...": unless already means if not; write "Unless you hurry". {lvl=2}
- Treating "in case" as "if": "Take a coat in case it gets cold" means be prepared now. {lvl=2}
- Mixing up wishes: "I wish I have a car" should be "I wish I had a car". {lvl=2}
- Using the third conditional for a general truth: use the zero conditional. {lvl=2}
- Inversion with the wrong helper: only had, were and should work, and the sentence is formal. {lvl=3}
:::
