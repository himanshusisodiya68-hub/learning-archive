## Why the sentence decides the meaning {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

A dictionary lists what a word *can* mean. A sentence shows what it *does* mean right now. Take the word **light**. In "Pack light for the trip" it means not heavy. In "The room was full of light" it means brightness. In "Light the lamp" it means to set burning. The same five letters carry three different jobs.

Readers who stop at the first meaning they know stumble. Readers who ask "what fits here?" almost never do. That question is the whole skill.

There are two ways a text speaks to you:

| What the text does | Example | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **States** | "Ravi arrived late, and the bus had already left." | Read it as it stands |
| **Implies** | "Ravi walked home, kicking stones." | Infer: he missed the bus and is annoyed |

The second is **inference**: you join clues in the text to what you already know about the world, and reach a conclusion the writer never wrote down. Vocabulary in context is a special case of the same move: the clue is a sentence, the conclusion is a meaning.

## Five clues that explain a new word {lvl=1 id=s-clues label="Foundation"}

Writers rarely drop a hard word without help. The help usually comes in one of five shapes.

| Clue | What to look for | Example | Meaning of the new word |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | the writer explains it, often after "is", "means", commas or dashes | "A **nomad**, a person who has no fixed home, packs up with the seasons." | a wanderer |
| **Synonym** (restatement) | another word or phrase nearby that says the same thing | "The room was **gloomy**, dark and dull." | dark, depressing |
| **Antonym or contrast** | words like but, unlike, however, while, instead point to the opposite | "Unlike his **garrulous** brother, Sam spoke only when asked." | talkative |
| **Example** | specific cases after "such as", "like", "for instance" | "She loves **pastimes** such as chess, knitting and birdwatching." | hobbies |
| **Cause and effect** | the result or the reason tells you the word | "The soldier was so **parched** that he drank the whole jug in one go." | very thirsty |

:::note title="The signal words are free hints" tone=tip
Contrast words (but, yet, although, unlike, whereas) tell you the next idea is the opposite. Addition words (and, also, moreover) tell you it is similar. Cause words (because, so, therefore) tell you one thing led to the other. Read the little word first and the big word often explains itself.
:::

:::qc
q: "The crowd was **jubilant**, cheering and dancing in the streets. Jubilant means"
options: ["joyful", "angry", "silent", "frightened"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Cheering and dancing is a restatement, a clue that gives the feeling of happy celebration.
wrong: Look at what the crowd is doing after the word: cheering and dancing. Those actions tell you the mood.
:::

:::qc
q: "Unlike his **frugal** friend, Arun spent money on anything he saw. Frugal means"
options: ["careful with money", "very rich", "generous", "greedy"]
answer: 0
right: Right. "Unlike" signals the opposite of spending on anything, which is being careful with money.
wrong: The word "unlike" turns the idea around. If Arun spends on everything, his frugal friend does the opposite.
:::

## Word parts as a second check {lvl=1 id=s-parts label="Foundation"}

Many English words are built from pieces. If you know the pieces, you can often guess the whole, and use that guess to confirm what the sentence suggests. Three kinds of piece matter.

- **Prefix**: goes in front and changes the meaning. *un-* (not), *re-* (again), *mis-* (wrongly), *pre-* (before), *dis-* (not, apart).
- **Root**: the core meaning. *port* (carry), *spect* (look), *dict* (say), *bio* (life), *tele* (far).
- **Suffix**: goes at the end and often changes the kind of word. *-ful* (full of), *-less* (without), *-ness* (a state), *-able* (can be).

| Word | Pieces | Guessed meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **unhappy** | un- (not) + happy | not happy |
| **rewrite** | re- (again) + write | write again |
| **transport** | trans- (across) + port (carry) | carry across |
| **inspect** | in- (into) + spect (look) | look into closely |
| **fearless** | fear + -less (without) | without fear |
| **telescope** | tele (far) + scope (view) | an instrument to see far |

Word parts are a useful second opinion but a poor first one. *Interest* does not mean "between rest", and *disgusted* is not "apart from gust". Always let the sentence have the last word. [Unverified: the origin of individual roots above is quoted from general knowledge of English word building and is not checked against a dictionary.]

:::qc
q: "The speaker was **tireless**, working for twelve hours without a break. Using the suffix, tireless means"
options: ["without getting tired", "very tired", "tired again", "tired of work"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The suffix -less means without, and the sentence agrees: twelve hours and no break.
wrong: -less means without, so tireless is "without tiring". The twelve hours without a break confirm it.
:::

## What an inference is {lvl=1 id=s-infer label="Foundation"}

An inference is a conclusion that the text points to but does not state. It always has two ingredients.

**Clues from the text + what you know = inference.**

Read this: *"Meera put down her umbrella, hung her dripping coat on the hook and rubbed her cold hands together."* Nothing says that it is raining. But umbrella, dripping and cold hands point there, and you know that umbrellas and dripping coats go with rain.

Two checks keep an inference honest.

1. **Can I point to the words that made me think so?** If yes, it is an inference. If no, it is a guess.
2. **Would another careful reader reach the same conclusion?** If it takes a stretch of imagination, it is probably not supported.

| Statement about the Meera sentence | Verdict |
|---|---|
| It is raining or has just rained. | Supported: umbrella, dripping, cold |
| Meera is a teacher. | Not supported: no clue at all |
| Meera has just come indoors. | Supported: hung her coat, put down the umbrella |
| Meera is unhappy. | Not supported: cold hands do not show mood |

:::qc
q: "Rohan checked his watch for the fifth time, tapped his foot and sighed loudly. We can infer that Rohan is"
options: ["impatient", "sleepy", "proud", "lost"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Checking the time again and again, tapping and sighing are all signs of impatience.
wrong: Ask which words point to the answer. Checking the watch, tapping and sighing together show someone who wants the waiting to end.
:::

## How the board asks vocabulary questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

In the CBSE Class 10 Reading section, an unseen passage is followed by questions that test vocabulary and inference. They come in a few set shapes. [Unverified: the exact number of marks and the mix of question types changes by year; check the latest sample paper.]

| Question shape | Example wording | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning as used** | "Choose the option that best gives the meaning of 'stern' as used in the passage." | Test each option by substitution |
| **Find the word** | "Find a word in the passage that means 'to shrink gradually'." | Look in the passage, not your memory; match part of speech |
| **Synonym or antonym from the passage** | "Pick a word from the passage that is opposite in meaning to 'ancient'." | Match the part of speech and the tense or number |
| **Inference** | "What can be inferred from the sentence ...?" | Find the supporting words, pick the option they allow |
| **Reason or purpose** | "Why did the writer mention ...?" | Link the detail to the writer's main point |

A routine that works on all of them:

1. **Go back to the sentence.** Locate the word or line in the passage and read the sentence before and after it.
2. **Find the clue.** Use the five clue types, then the word parts.
3. **Predict before you look.** Say the meaning to yourself, then scan the options.
4. **Test by substitution.** Put each remaining option into the sentence. Reject any that change the tone or break the grammar.
5. **Check the part of speech.** A "word that means rushed" must be an adjective or a verb in the past; a noun will not fit.

:::note title="Match the form exactly" tone=warn
For "find a word in the passage that means ...", the word must be in the passage, in the form asked. If the question says "means *to grow smaller*" it wants a verb, so *dwindled* is acceptable if the passage uses it, but *small* is not. Copy the word exactly as it appears.
:::

:::qc
q: "The new rule was met with **stiff** resistance from teachers. Which meaning of stiff fits as used here?"
options: ["strong and unyielding", "hard to bend", "formal and cold", "thick and heavy"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Resistance cannot be physically bent. Stiff here means strong and firm, like stiff competition.
wrong: Resistance is not an object, so "hard to bend" cannot fit. Substitute each option and see which makes sense with resistance.
:::

## One word, many meanings {lvl=2 id=s-meanings label="Class 10"}

The commonest board trap is a familiar word used in an unfamiliar way. Learn to expect it.

| Word | Meaning 1 | Meaning 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **fair** | just, equal (*a fair judge*) | light in colour; also a market event (*the book fair*) |
| **bear** | to tolerate (*I cannot bear the noise*) | the animal |
| **spring** | a season | a coil; a leap; a source of water |
| **close** | to shut (*close the door*) | near (*a close friend*; said differently) |
| **bank** | a place for money | the side of a river |
| **run** | to move fast on foot | to manage (*she runs a shop*); to flow (*the river runs south*) |

The same applies to **idioms and phrasal verbs**: groups of words whose meaning is not the sum of the words.

- *The news broke the ice at the meeting.* (made people at ease, not cracked frost)
- *He was over the moon about the result.* (extremely happy)
- *The talks fell through.* (failed)
- *She came across an old photograph.* (found by chance)

If the plain meaning of the phrase makes no sense in the sentence, it is likely an idiom, so read for the figurative sense.

:::qc
q: "The shopkeeper does not **run** a tab for strangers." Run here means"
options: ["keep or carry", "move fast", "flow", "operate a machine"]
answer: 0
right: Right. To run a tab is to let someone pay later; the shopkeeper keeps an account open.
wrong: Nobody is moving fast or flowing here. Substitute the options; only keeping an open account makes sense.
:::

## Kinds of inference you will be asked {lvl=2 id=s-kinds label="Class 10"}

Board inference questions fall into a few kinds. Know them and you know what evidence to look for.

| Kind | What it asks | Where the evidence is |
|---|---|---|
| **Feeling or character** | How does she feel? What sort of person is he? | Actions, speech and small physical details |
| **Cause** | Why did this happen? | Words just before the event, or "because", "so", "after" |
| **Effect or prediction** | What is likely to follow? | The trend of the passage so far |
| **Writer's attitude (tone)** | Is the writer admiring, critical, amused? | Choice of adjectives and verbs, the opening and closing lines |
| **Meaning of a line** | What does the writer mean by "..."? | The context before and after; the main idea |
| **Purpose** | Why does the writer give this example? | The point the example supports |

Feelings are often shown, not named: a trembling hand, a quick glance at the clock, a forced smile. Learn the mapping from behaviour to feeling and half the questions answer themselves.

| Behaviour | Likely feeling |
|---|---|
| bit her nails, paced the room | anxious |
| smiled to himself, hummed all day | quietly pleased |
| turned away, did not reply | hurt or angry |
| double-checked every answer | careful, or nervous |
| laughed too loudly | covering unease [Inference] |

:::qc
q: "After the match, Kabir sat alone, staring at his boots and ignoring his friends' calls." We can infer that Kabir is"
options: ["upset about the result", "tired of football", "waiting for the bus", "angry with his boots"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Sitting alone, staring down and ignoring friends are common signs of disappointment after a loss.
wrong: Ask what the clues together suggest. Sitting alone and ignoring friends after a match points to the result, not the boots or the bus.
:::

## A passage worked from start to finish {lvl=2 id=s-worked label="Class 10"}

:::worked title="Questions on an original passage"
**Passage (written for this archive).** When the new bridge opened across the Sarju, the boatmen of Kotli did not cheer. Old Habib, who had ferried villagers for forty years, stood at the bank with his pole across his shoulder and watched the first bus rumble over the water. "Fast," he said at last, and went back to his boat. In the weeks that followed, his passengers dwindled from forty a day to four. He still came at dawn, still bailed out the boat and coiled his ropes with care, as though a crowd might yet appear. One evening a schoolgirl, late for the last bus, ran down the bank. Habib smiled for the first time in a month and picked up his pole.

**Q1. Find a word in the passage that means "grew gradually less".**
The sense is a decrease over time. Look for a past-tense verb: *dwindled* (passengers fell from forty to four).

**Q2. Why did the boatmen not cheer?**
No sentence states it, so infer. Clue: the bridge carries buses over the water, and Habib's passengers then fall from forty to four. A bridge takes away the boatmen's work. Answer: they feared losing their living.

**Q3. What does "Fast," said Habib at last, reveal about his feelings?**
One short word, said after a long pause. He admits the bus is quick, but there is no praise in it. Tone: grudging admiration mixed with sadness. Not enthusiasm, and not anger (he simply returns to his boat).

**Q4. "as though a crowd might yet appear". What does this phrase suggest?**
He keeps his routine as if nothing had changed. It suggests a stubborn hope, or an unwillingness to accept that his trade is ending.

**Q5. Why did Habib smile when the schoolgirl came?**
She needed to cross the water, which means the boat was useful again. The smile is the first in a month, so it signals relief and pride at being needed.
:::

Notice that every answer points back to a phrase in the passage. That is the test of a good inference.

:::qc
q: "In the passage, the word **dwindled** is closest in meaning to"
options: ["shrank", "vanished suddenly", "increased", "stayed steady"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Forty a day falling to four shows a gradual shrinking, not a sudden vanishing.
wrong: Forty fell to four, so the number grew smaller, but he still had four passengers. It did not vanish suddenly.
:::

## Words that carry a feeling {lvl=3 id=s-connote label="Beyond the board"}

Two words can name the same thing and still lean opposite ways. The plain meaning is the **denotation**; the feeling attached is the **connotation**.

| Neutral | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| thin | slim, slender | skinny, scrawny |
| determined | resolute, persistent | stubborn, obstinate |
| thrifty | economical, careful | stingy, miserly |
| old | experienced, mature | ancient, outdated |
| confident | self-assured | arrogant, cocky |
| talk | discuss, converse | gossip, babble |

A writer's choice among these is a clue to **tone**. If a report calls a protest "a gathering of concerned citizens", it leans one way; "a mob" leans the other. The facts may be the same.

Questions about attitude therefore ask you to notice:

- **Adjectives and verbs**: *strolled* or *sauntered* or *slunk*?
- **Contrast words**: "however", "yet" show where the writer turns.
- **Qualifiers**: *barely*, *merely*, *at least*, *a mere ten*, which shrink or stretch the claim.
- **Irony**: saying one thing to mean its opposite. "What a fine moment to lose the keys" is a complaint, not praise.

:::qc
q: "The minister's plan was described as 'ambitious' by supporters and 'reckless' by critics. The difference between the two words is mainly one of"
options: ["connotation", "spelling", "tense", "grammar"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Both describe a bold plan, but ambitious admires it and reckless blames it.
wrong: The two words describe the same thing, a bold plan. They differ in the feeling they carry, which is connotation.
:::

## Where inference ends {lvl=3 id=s-limits label="Beyond the board"}

A good reader knows where evidence stops. Three habits help.

**1. Separate three levels.**

| Level | Example for "The shop shut at noon, three hours before its usual time." |
|---|---|
| **Stated** | It shut at noon; usual time is three hours later |
| **Strongly implied** | Something unusual happened |
| **Speculation** | The owner was ill [Speculation: nothing in the sentence says why] |

Board answers need the middle row. The bottom row is the usual wrong option.

**2. Beware of extremes.** Options with *always*, *never*, *only*, *everyone*, *completely* are seldom supported. The passage usually says *some*, *often*, *may*.

**3. Watch for implied meaning in everyday speech.** "Some of the students passed" states only that some did, and most listeners infer that not all did. A careful reader asks whether the conclusion comes from the words or from habit.

**A last tool for hard words.** When an unseen passage has a word you really cannot place, do not freeze. Read the line twice, decide whether the word is praise or blame from the mood of the sentence, decide its part of speech, and pick the option that fits both. Even this half-knowledge removes two of four options.

:::qc
q: "The notice said, 'Entry only for members.' Which inference is best supported?"
options: ["Visitors who are not members are not allowed in", "All members must attend", "The place is always empty", "Members pay nothing"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The notice limits entry to members, so non-members are kept out.
wrong: The notice speaks only about who may enter. It does not say members must attend, how crowded the place is, or what they pay.
:::

4. An inference is clues in the text plus what you know; you must be able to point to the words that support it. {lvl=1}
5. Test any guess by substitution: put the meaning back into the sentence and reread. {lvl=2}

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Choosing the meaning you already know without testing it in the sentence: always substitute. {lvl=1}
- Ignoring signal words such as but, unlike, because: they hand you the type of clue. {lvl=1}
- Relying on word parts alone: "interest" is not "between rest". {lvl=1}
- Copying a word from the passage in the wrong form (noun instead of verb) in a find-the-word question. {lvl=2}
- Treating idioms word by word: "break the ice" is not about ice. {lvl=2}
- Choosing an option that is true in the real world but is not in the passage. {lvl=2}
- Picking the option with extremes (always, never, all) when the passage says some or often. {lvl=2}
- Reading a feeling into a sentence without a clue: ask which words made you think so. {lvl=2}
- Missing irony or tone, so you read a complaint as praise. {lvl=3}
- Confusing a strong implication with speculation: if nothing in the text supports it, drop it. {lvl=3}
:::
