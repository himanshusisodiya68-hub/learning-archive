## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

**Part one: at home.** Sulekha is the youngest of seven children of Ramlal, a prosperous farmer who is also the village revenue official (the numberdar). As a baby she fell from a cot on her head, and the family decided that something in her mind was damaged. Smallpox at two left her face and body deeply scarred. She did not speak until she was five, and then she stammered. The village children mocked her, so she spoke less and less. Her brothers were sent to the city to study, and her elder sisters were healthy, good-looking and easy to marry off. Bholi was the one her father worried about.

**Part two: at school.** When she was seven, a primary school for girls opened in the village. The Tehsildar, a senior government officer, urged Ramlal to set an example and send his daughters. Ramlal's wife was horrified, because girls who study might not find husbands, but she had a plan: send Bholi, who was unlikely to marry anyway, and let the teachers carry the burden. Bholi was terrified on the way. Once there, the other girls and the coloured pictures on the wall interested her, and the lady teacher spoke to her so gently that she tried to say her own name. Each effort was met with "well done". The teacher promised that if she kept coming she would learn to read and, in time, speak without stammering, and that people would listen to her with respect.

**Part three: the wedding.** Years later the village has grown into a small town with a cinema and a railway station. A well-off grocer, Bishamber Nath, proposes to marry Bholi. He is much older than she is, limps, has grown-up children from an earlier marriage, and asks for no dowry. Her parents accept, partly because they think she will otherwise stay unmarried. On the wedding day Bishamber lifts the veil, sees the pock-marks and demands five thousand rupees before he will go on. Ramlal begs, bows and finally pays. Bishamber smiles in triumph. But when the veil is lifted again, Bholi looks him in the eye with cold contempt, throws the garland into the fire, and tells her father to take back his money. She will not marry "such a mean, greedy and contemptible coward". The groom's party leaves. Ramlal asks who will marry her now, and Bholi answers calmly that she will look after her parents in their old age and teach in the school where she learnt so much. The teacher, watching from a corner, agrees, with the satisfaction of an artist whose work is complete.

:::qc
q: Why do Bholi's parents send her to school?
options: ["Because they believe education will help her stammer", "Because her father fears the Tehsildar, and her mother thinks she has little chance of marriage anyway", "Because her brothers asked them to"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. The reasons are unusual and slightly sad: neither parent values her schooling, but the school becomes the best thing that ever happens to her.
wrong: Look again at the conversation between Ramlal and his wife. Neither one speaks of learning. One fears an official, the other is thinking about marriage chances.

## Characters {lvl=2 id=s-chars label="Class 10"}

| Character | What we learn | Evidence in the story |
|---|---|---|
| Bholi (Sulekha) | Neglected, frightened and silent at first; then eager, hopeful and finally brave, clear-spoken and self-respecting | She trembles on the way to school; she tries to say her name for a kind teacher; she throws the garland into the fire and speaks without stammering |
| The teacher | Gentle, patient, perceptive and committed; sees a person where others see a fool | She speaks softly, uses Bholi's name, praises every attempt, sets small goals and shares the final satisfaction of an artist |
| Ramlal | Fearful of authority and gossip, more concerned about izzat than about his daughter; not cruel, but weak | He cannot refuse the Tehsildar; he places his turban at Bishamber's feet; he stands rooted in shame afterwards |
| Ramlal's wife | Harsh and practical, sees daughters as a marriage problem | She says Bholi can go to school because no one will marry her |
| Bishamber Nath | Greedy, calculating and cowardly; reduces marriage to a bargain | He demands five thousand rupees after seeing her face and smiles when it is paid |

Notice that nobody is purely a villain. Ramlal pleads, pays and grieves. The story's target is the attitude the whole village shares, that a girl's worth is measured by looks and dowry.

## Themes {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10"}

**Education changes a person.** The school gives Bholi more than reading. It gives her a place where someone listens to her, goals to reach, and the belief that fear can be overcome. By the end she is ready to earn her own living as a teacher, a future that does not depend on any husband.

**The power of kindness.** The teacher does not fix Bholi by force or by correction. She simply treats her as someone worth waiting for. Read the exchange on the first day: she asks, she waits, she praises. Bholi's first full, unbroken word spoken to someone other than herself is a reply to that kindness.

**Dowry and the treatment of girls.** Bholi's brothers are sent to the city to study, while for the girls the main question is marriage. Her mother's first reaction to school is to ask who will marry girls who study. Bishamber then turns the wedding into a market, and the family accepts that this is how things work. The story criticises all of this, and it does so by showing a girl who refuses to be a price tag.

**Courage and self-respect.** Bholi first agrees to the match to protect her father's honour. She rejects it when she sees that the man is mean and greedy and that her father's honour was being used to trap her. The change is in what she is willing to sacrifice honour for.

**Appearance versus worth.** Her face is scarred, but her eyes were saved, and in the climax it is her steady eyes that carry the scene. The story invites us to look at what a person says and does, not at the face.

## Why does the narrator call her Sulekha? {lvl=2 id=s-name label="Class 10"}

The story opens by telling us her real name, Sulekha, and then says everyone calls her Bholi. After that she is Bholi in every line, in the mouths of the family and of the narrator, until the second-to-last paragraph, where the narrator writes "And Sulekha said in a voice that was calm and steady".

Here is why that matters.

| Name | Who uses it | What it means about her |
|---|---|---|
| Bholi | The family, village and, by habit, the narrator | A label meaning "simpleton"; she is seen as slow and helpless |
| Sulekha | Her parents gave it at birth; the narrator returns to it at the end | Her own identity, a person with her own mind |

When she speaks in a calm and steady voice, the label no longer fits, so the narrator drops it. The change of name tells us that others now see her differently, or at least that we should. A name is the smallest piece of identity, so the story uses it to mark the biggest change. (This is one possible answer; the text does not explain the choice.)

## The author's craft {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Contrast.** The story is built from pairs. Bholi at the start and at the end. The nameless village at the start and the small town with a cinema at the end. The sad "dumb cow" and the girl who finally speaks. The patient teacher and the impatient family. Bishamber's smile of triumph and then his retreat. Contrast makes the change impossible to miss.

**Recurring image: the cow.** The old cow Lakshmi is sold, and Bholi fears that school means being sent away like her. In the classroom she sees a picture of a cow just like Lakshmi, which comforts her. At the wedding the old woman and others call her a "dumb cow", an animal sold or led away without a say. When Bholi calls herself that "dumb cow" and tells them it is now speaking, she takes the insult back and turns it round. The cow image follows her from fear, to comfort, to defiance.

**Symbols.** The marriage fire is normally a holy witness to a vow. Bholi uses it to destroy the garland, so the same ritual carries a vow of a different kind, to herself. The veil, put down and lifted twice, shows hidden identity: the first time it hides her shame; the second time she throws it away and looks out. The bells in the temple and the trees flowering in red on the first day are a burst of hope, a feeling, not an event.

**Irony.** Ramlal and his wife send Bholi to school to get rid of the problem, and the school makes her the one person in the family able to solve it. Bishamber gambles that the family will pay and wins five thousand rupees, and loses the bride. The family worries that no one will marry her, and Bholi's answer is that she does not need anyone to.

**The closing image.** The teacher, standing quietly in a corner, watches with "the light of a deep satisfaction" like that of an artist who has finished a masterpiece (final paragraph). The comparison honours teaching as a creative act. It also raises a quiet question: is Bholi the masterpiece, or the artist of her own life? A good answer notes that the teacher gave the tools, but the final stroke, the refusal, belongs to Bholi.

**Narrative voice.** A third-person narrator who mostly follows Bholi, and in the second half also shows us the minds of her parents. Dialogue does a lot of the work: the stammer is written out on the page ("Bh-Bh-Bho-Bho-"), which makes us feel each effort. On the wedding day the stammer is gone and the sentences become short, direct and powerful.

## Writing a character sketch {lvl=2 id=s-sketch label="Class 10"}

A character sketch is a short piece that shows what a person is like, using evidence. CBSE questions ask for it in 100 to 120 words, often as "describe Bholi" or "how does the teacher help her".

| Part | What to include | Example for Bholi |
|---|---|---|
| Opening claim | One sentence that sums the person up | Bholi is a neglected child who grows into a courageous young woman. |
| Appearance and background | Only what the story uses | Smallpox scarred her; she stammered; her family called her a simpleton. |
| Qualities with evidence | Two or three qualities, each backed by an action or a quotation | Hopeful (she asks silently "Really?"), determined (she tries again and again to say her name), brave (she burns the garland). |
| Change | How the person alters, and what causes it | The teacher's kindness and schooling turn fear into confidence. |
| Closing comment | What we learn from the character | She shows that education gives people the strength to defend their dignity. |

Keep the verbs in the present tense for what the story shows, and avoid copying long lines.

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not say Bholi was stupid. She is called a simpleton by others; the story shows her as intelligent once someone is kind to her. {lvl=1}
- Do not write that she was sent to school to learn. Her parents had other motives, and that irony is part of the point. {lvl=2}
- Do not call Ramlal a villain. He is weak and conventional; the story criticises his attitude without hating him. {lvl=2}
- Bholi rejects Bishamber because of his greed and cowardice, not just because he limps or is old. Quote her own words. {lvl=2}
- A question on "Why Sulekha?" needs both names, who uses each, and what the shift shows. Naming only one scores half. {lvl=2}
- Do not quote long passages. A short phrase plus your own comment earns more than a copied paragraph. {lvl=3}
- Give your own view on discussion questions about girls' rights, but back it with a reason or an example, not only a slogan. {lvl=3}
:::
