## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

**Part I: the wish.** Valli is an eight-year-old who lives in a village. She has no playmates her age on her street, so her favourite pastime is standing in the front doorway, watching the street. The thing she likes best is the bus that runs between her village and the nearest town, passing once each hour in each direction. The wish to ride it grows day by day. She is so jealous of friends who describe the town that she shouts "Proud! proud!" at them. Over many months she quietly gathers facts from neighbours and regular passengers: the town is six miles away, the fare is thirty paise one way, the trip takes forty-five minutes, and a passenger who stays seated and pays again can come straight back.

**Part II: the ride begins.** One spring afternoon she calls out for the bus to stop and pays with coins from her hand. She refuses the conductor's offer of help, and he starts teasing her as "madam". The few passengers smile. She is shy for a moment, then finds the bus new and lovely, with a clock above the windshield and shining bars. Her window is half hidden by a canvas blind, so she stands on the seat to see the canal, palm trees, hills and green fields. An elderly man tells her to sit down and she snaps that she is not a child. Later an elderly woman asks where she is going. Valli thinks her ugly and nosy, and tells her curtly that she can look after herself.

**Part III: the town.** The story steps back to show how hard Valli worked for this day. She saved stray coins, turned down sweets and toys, and even skipped the merry-go-round at the fair. She slipped out while her mother napped. The journey is full of delight. The bus seems to gobble up everything in its way, trees run towards it and stop, and a young cow races down the middle of the road with its tail up. Valli laughs until she cries. At the town she gasps at the shops and the crowd, but she does not get off. She pays her second fare and refuses a walk and a cold drink the conductor offers to buy.

**Part IV: the way back.** On the return trip she sees the same young cow lying dead by the roadside, hit by a fast vehicle. The lovely creature has become a horrible sight, and Valli goes quiet and stops looking outside. She reaches home at three-forty, says goodbye to the conductor and runs home. A chatty aunt asks where she has been without waiting for a reply. Valli hears her mother say that so much happens around us that we cannot know, and that even when we know something we may not fully understand it. Valli breathes "Oh, yes!" and gets teased for butting in. She smiles to herself, sure that no one will understand why.

## Valli's plan in numbers {lvl=1 id=s-plan label="Foundation"}

The story gives us a plan we can check like a school timetable. Notice how much careful work an eight-year-old has done.

| Detail | What the story tells us | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Distance and time | Six miles to town, forty-five minutes each way | She has worked out how long she will be gone |
| Fare | Thirty paise one way (a "fortune" to her) | A small sum for adults, a large one for a child who rarely sees money |
| Money saved | Sixty paise from stray coins | It exactly covers two fares: no sweets, no toys, no merry-go-round |
| Chosen bus | The one-o'clock bus | It fits her mother's afternoon nap |
| Mother's nap | From about one to four | She has time to ride and be back unnoticed |
| Return | Reaches the village at three-forty | She is home before the nap ends |

Read down the table and you can see a pattern: **she plans every possible leak in her scheme**. This makes her later reply to the conductor ("Oh, I'd be much too afraid") interesting. She is bold in action but sensible about danger.

## Setting and theme {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10"}

**Setting.** A village with a main road and a canal, and a town six miles away. The story does not name them; it was translated from Tamil, but exact place and year are not given in the book [Unverified beyond this]. The setting matters because a village child with one coin-sized allowance and a single bus an hour has a small world, so one trip is a big event.

**Theme 1: curiosity and the wish to be independent.** Valli does everything herself: she plans, saves and pays. The adults' jokes, which call her "madam" and treat her as small, show how often children are not taken seriously.

**Theme 2: growing up.** A single afternoon carries Valli from delight to a quiet grief. She enters the house as someone who knows slightly more than her mother and aunt expect.

**Theme 3: the limit of knowledge.** The mother's remark about how much happens without our knowledge, and how we can know a thing without fully understanding it, is the key. Valli knew in some sense that animals die. She did not understand it until a living, laughing creature became a lifeless body.

**Theme 4: the world's mixture of joy and sorrow.** In the same afternoon she meets wonder (the canal, the shops, the speeding train) and loss (the cow). The story does not choose between them.

## Reading the ending {lvl=2 id=s-ending label="Class 10"}

The ending is brief, and a board question (TT Q7) asks about it, so read it step by step.

1. Valli comes home and her aunt asks where she has been, but "not expecting a reply". Valli just smiles. She has kept her secret.
2. Her mother tells the aunt that there are so many things in our midst and in the wider world that we cannot know about everything, and even when we know, we may not understand completely.
3. Valli says "Oh, yes!" and then explains she was agreeing about "things happening without our knowledge".
4. The aunt calls her a "chit of a girl" who pokes her nose into grown-ups' talk. Valli smiles to herself, for the adults will never guess what she meant.

What does she mean? Two things happened without anyone at home knowing: **her own secret journey**, and **the cow's death**, which she now understands in a way she did not before. Her mother's sentence, spoken about something else, describes her day exactly.

## The author's craft {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**An eight-year-old's point of view.** The narration is in the third person, but it stays close to Valli and describes the world as she would. The bus is almost an animal: it seems about to "gobble up" a pedestrian or another vehicle but always passes on safely (Part III). Trees run towards the bus, then stand helpless for a moment before rushing away in the other direction. That is personification and a child's illusion of motion rather than a grown-up's description. Money is counted in paise and thirty paise feels like a fortune. The clock above the windshield and the "silver" overhead bars are noted with delight. Even her disgust at the old woman is a child's: she sees big earring holes, ugly earrings and betel juice, and decides the woman is unbearable.

**Humour in dialogue.** The conductor's mock-polite lines ("May we start now, madam?") produce a running joke. The laughter in the first half is warm: Valli snaps, the passengers smile, and in the end she laughs too. This humour is what makes the second half hurt.

**Parallel scenes and contrast.** The cow appears twice. In Part III it is a frisky, tail-in-the-air runaway that makes Valli laugh until she cries. In Part IV it is a body with a fixed stare and blood on the ground. The writer makes the reader feel the shift by repeating one image in two moods. The same road, the same bus and the same child give a very different afternoon.

| Moment | Mood | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| Part II teasing | Bright, comic | Mock-polite dialogue, irony of "madam" |
| Part III journey | Wonder | Personification, lively verbs, child's wide-eyed view |
| Part IV cow | Dark, quiet | Contrast with the earlier cow, plain concrete detail |
| Ending | Calm, thoughtful | Understatement, dialogue that means more than it says |

**Irony.** "Madam" is a joke, but a gentle one, and it is also slightly true. She behaves with a lady's dignity. Another irony: the mother's remark is made with no idea of its force, yet it fits Valli's secret day exactly (dramatic irony).

**Foreshadowing and restraint.** The first cow is lively and loved by the reader before it dies. The story never lectures about death. It simply lets Valli look away from the window.

## Speech, tone and manner words {lvl=2 id=s-speech label="Class 10"}

**Why the story is full of speech.** The NCERT Speaking task says that this story has many people talking. Dialogue lets us hear a character's temper without the narrator saying so. To use it well in your own writing, you need two skills: setting out speech correctly, and choosing words that show how something is said.

**Setting out speech.**
- Put the speaker's exact words inside quotation marks. Begin a new line each time the speaker changes.
- Put a comma (or ? or !) inside the closing quotation mark before words like *she said*: "I can take care of myself," Valli said.
- If the tag comes first, put a comma after it and begin the quote with a capital: The conductor said, "Hurry then!"

**Manner adverbs show tone.** A manner adverb tells *how* something is said or done. Often it ends in *-ly*. Writers use them in speech tags to give the reader the voice.

| Adverb | How the character speaks | Who and when |
|---|---|---|
| haughtily | in a proud, superior way | Valli to the elderly man |
| curtly | briefly and with displeasure | Valli to the elderly woman |
| irritably | in an annoyed, short-tempered way | Valli, when called a child again |
| commandingly | in the manner of someone giving an order | the raised hand that stops the bus |
| wistfully | with sad longing | Valli, as she watches passengers |
| firmly | with no room for argument | Valli refusing the drink |

**Try it.** Rewrite "I will not go," she said, using a manner adverb that suits a shy child, then one that suits an angry teenager. Both sentences should have different meanings of *how*, and the reader should feel the difference. Do not stack two adverbs and do not use "said" with an adverb that merely repeats it, such as *said quietly in a whisper*.

**Moving to reported speech.** If asked to report a line from the story, change the tense and pronouns and drop the quotation marks: Valli told the conductor that she was not a madam. See the lesson on reported speech.

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not call Valli rude or spoilt without balance. Her pride shows a need to be taken seriously, and she is also thrifty and brave. {lvl=2}
- Do not say the conductor is unkind. His teasing is warm, and he offers her a drink, a front seat and a safe warning. {lvl=2}
- The cow is not killed by the bus. The text says it was struck by some fast-moving vehicle. {lvl=2}
- Do not claim Valli did not know cows die. The point is that she did not understand it until she saw it. {lvl=3}
- Keep to the facts of the plan: six miles, thirty paise one way, forty-five minutes, sixty paise saved. Mixing them up loses easy marks. {lvl=1}
- In dialogue, put the comma inside the closing quotation mark and start a new line for each new speaker. {lvl=2}
- Do not copy long passages in an answer. A short phrase plus your own comment earns more. {lvl=3}
:::
