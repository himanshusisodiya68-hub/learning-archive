## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

Two boys see fresh muddy footprints of bare feet on the steps of a London house. A new footprint appears out of nowhere, and then more follow down the street, getting fainter until they disappear. The explanation is simple. A scientist named **Griffin** has found a way to make a human body transparent like glass. He has swallowed certain rare drugs and become invisible, though still solid.

Griffin is clever but lawless. His landlord disliked him and tried to throw him out, so in revenge he set the house on fire. To escape unseen he had to take off his clothes, and so he became a homeless wanderer with no clothes and no money. The mud gave him away only because it stuck to his feet.

It is mid-winter. To get warm he slips into a big London store and, once the doors are shut, helps himself to warm clothes, food and wine and sleeps on a pile of quilts. He oversleeps, is spotted by the arriving assistants, panics, and has to strip off the new clothes to escape. Shivering, he goes to the theatre district at Drury Lane and gets a disguise for his missing head: bandages, dark glasses, a false nose, whiskers and a hat. He attacks the shopkeeper from behind and robs him.

Then he takes a train to the village of Iping and books two rooms at the inn. He tells Mrs Hall, the landlord's wife, that he wants solitude after "an accident" to his face. She puts up with his temper because he has paid in advance. When his money runs out he claims a cheque is on its way. Soon there is a burglary at the clergyman's study, with no one in sight. Then Mrs Hall's furniture seems to come alive, and a chair drives her out of the room.

The villagers suspect witchcraft, and then suspect the stranger. When Mrs Hall confronts him, Griffin loses his temper and throws off his disguise, revealing a headless man. The constable, Mr Jaffers, tries to arrest him with a magistrate's warrant, but Griffin knocks him out and shakes himself free. The chapter ends with nobody knowing where to lay hands on him.

## Setting and theme {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10"}

**Setting.** The chapter moves from a crowded city to a quiet village. London is large enough for Griffin to hide in but unforgiving in winter. The Iping inn is small, gossip travels fast, and a stranger "set all tongues wagging". Moving the scene from city to village raises the comedy, because the villagers have no idea what they are dealing with.

**Theme.** The main theme is the misuse of knowledge. Griffin's discovery is real, yet he uses it for burglary, assault and revenge. The chapter never lectures; it lets the consequences speak. A second theme is the gap between how a thing seems and what it is: a "haunted" chair, a "scientist with a facial accident", a man who is really nothing but a voice and a set of clothes.

**Conflict.** Griffin is in conflict with society (landlord, shopkeepers, constable) and with nature (cold, hunger). He loses most rounds of both.

:::qc

answer: 1
right: Correct. Griffin's discovery is genuine, but his lawlessness turns it into a series of crimes and escapes, with no real benefit to him.

## The author's craft {lvl=2 id=s-craft label="Class 10"}

**Humour and understatement.** The narrator reports a night of burglary and a hat leaping into a woman's face in the same cheerful tone. "A stranger of such uncommon appearance set all tongues wagging" is mild language for a man in bandages and dark glasses. The constable is "quite surprised" to arrest a man without a head. This deadpan understatement is the main source of comedy.

**Suspense and withheld information.** The chapter opens with the footprints and delays the explanation by a few lines, so we want to know before we are told. In the inn the reader already knows the secret, while Mrs Hall and the clergyman do not. This is **dramatic irony**: we know more than the characters, and their puzzlement is funny. The empty room, the cold bed and the discarded bandages are clues we can read and they cannot.

**Pace and structure.** Short, brisk scenes are arranged as a chase: boys, store, assistants, theatre shop, train, inn. Each place solves the last problem and creates the next.

**Narrative voice.** The narrator is in the third person and sees into events from outside. He calls Griffin "lawless" and "callous", but does not preach. Direct speech arrives only when it matters: "Surrender!" and Mrs Hall's complaint about the chair.

## Answering the "Think about it" questions {lvl=2 id=s-answers label="Class 10"}

**"Griffin was rather a lawless person." Comment.** A lawless person ignores the rules that society lives by. Say so, then prove it. Griffin burnt his landlord's house for revenge, stole clothes, food and wine from a London store, attacked the Drury Lane shopkeeper from behind and robbed him, took the clergyman's housekeeping money, and knocked out a constable doing his duty. Add one comment: each crime is a reaction to the last problem, never a plan.

**How would you assess Griffin as a scientist?** Make a two-sided answer. As a *scientist* he is remarkable: after "experiment after experiment" he makes a body transparent. As a *thinker* he is shortsighted. He never thought about clothes, cold, food, a place to live or how to be seen when he wanted to be. A good scientist asks what follows from a discovery. Griffin did not. He also lacks the ethics of science, since he uses the result for crime.

## Grammar in use: "Brilliant scientist though he was" {lvl=2 id=s-grammar label="Class 10"}

**Why this word order.** The chapter says: "Brilliant scientist though he was, Griffin was rather a lawless person." It is another way of writing "Though he was a brilliant scientist, Griffin was rather a lawless person." Moving the descriptive phrase to the front puts the stress on it, so the contrast with "lawless" lands harder.

**The pattern.** Adjective or noun + *though* / *as* + subject + verb, then the main clause.

| Plain form | Fronted form |
|---|---|
| Though she was tired, she kept walking. | Tired though she was, she kept walking. |
| Although he was a clever boy, he failed. | Clever boy though he was, he failed. |
| Although it was cold, they swam. | Cold as it was, they swam. |

**Trap.** In the fronted form, do not add a second linking word. "Although tired though she was" is wrong. Use one contrast marker only.

## Beyond the board: is it possible? {lvl=3 id=s-science label="Beyond the board"}

The NCERT page asks what makes glass or water transparent. Here is a short, careful account; details marked [Unverified] are from memory and not from the chapter.

**Why glass is transparent.** Light is a wave. When it meets a material, the material can absorb it (turning it into heat), scatter it in all directions, or let it through. Ordinary glass does not absorb the energies of visible light, and its structure is smooth and even, so light passes in a nearly straight line. That is why we see through it. A thick pile of crushed glass looks white, because the many surfaces scatter light.

**Why we still see glass.** Light bends and partly reflects at a surface where the speed of light changes. Glass has a refractive index of about 1.5, and air has about 1.0 [Unverified, approximate]. That difference is why you notice a glass window's edge and why a glass of water shows a bent pencil. A thing is truly invisible only if it has the same refractive index as its surroundings and absorbs and scatters nothing.

**The human body is a hard case.** A human body is made of many materials with different refractive indices, such as skin, fat, muscle and blood, and it is full of boundaries. To make all of it transparent is far beyond what we know how to do.

**The classic objection.** To see, our eyes must catch light and turn it into signals, which means the retina must absorb light. A fully transparent eye would let all the light pass through and so would see nothing. In the chapter Griffin sees perfectly well, which is a sign the story is fiction. Science writers often point this out, but that does not spoil the fun.

**What the story does well.** It treats the idea seriously about everyday consequences: footprints, cold, hunger, the need for clothes, and the way people react to what they cannot explain.

:::qc
q: According to the explanation above, why can we usually still see a glass window?
options: ["Glass absorbs all light", "Light bends and reflects at its surface because its refractive index differs from air", "Glass is always painted"]
answer: 1
right: Correct. The difference in refractive index at the surface makes it show up, even though most light goes through.
wrong: Glass lets most light pass. Think about what happens at the place where air meets glass.
:::

## Beyond the board: would you want to be invisible? {lvl=3 id=s-ethics label="Beyond the board"}

A strong answer to the NCERT "Talk about it" question balances both sides.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| You could watch rare animals without disturbing them | Others could not see you, so you could be hurt by accident, in a crowd or on the road |
| You could escape danger, such as a fire or a flood | You still need warmth, clothes and food, as Griffin learns |
| Useful in defence or rescue work in fiction | No one would trust you, and temptation to misuse the power is high |
| Fun for pranks and surprises | Loneliness: no one can see your face or hear you react |

The deeper question is moral. If no one can see you, do you still behave well? Griffin's answer is no. The test of character is what you do when you cannot be caught.
