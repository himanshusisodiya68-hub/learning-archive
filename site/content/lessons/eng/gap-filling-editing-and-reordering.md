right: Right. "Fond" is paired with "of", and no other preposition fits. Questions like this test a fixed pairing you must know, not a rule you can work out.
wrong: Look at the word before the gap. "Fond" always takes "of": fond of music, fond of travel. Some gaps are decided by the neighbour on the left.

## Why the board asks these questions {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Writing a sentence from scratch lets you choose the words you are sure of. Real writing does not work like that. You often have a draft, written in a hurry, and you have to check it. A good writer therefore needs two skills: to **notice** that something is wrong and to **repair** it without breaking what is right.

The board turns these two skills into four question types.

| Question type | What you receive | What you do | Skill tested |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gap filling (cloze) | A passage with words removed | Supply the missing words | Choosing the form that fits both sides of the gap |
| Editing | A passage with errors | Find the error, write the correction | Noticing wrong forms |
| Omission | A passage with words dropped | Find where a word is missing, supply it | Noticing what is not there |
| Reordering | Jumbled words or sentences | Arrange them in a meaningful order | Sentence structure and the flow of a paragraph |

The exact number of items, the marks, and which types appear in a given year change from time to time. Check the latest sample paper from the board. [Unverified: current format and marks]

What does not change is the idea underneath. All four types test grammar **in context**. A single sentence on a grammar page can be right or wrong on its own, but in a passage, a word is right or wrong **because of the words around it**. That is why one habit beats every other trick: always read around the gap.

## What the examiner is testing {lvl=1 id=s-what label="Foundation"}

Behind every gap or faulty line is a small family of rules. Examiners draw on the same families year after year, because these are the places where students slip.

| Family | The test | Typical clue | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tense** | Does the verb agree with the time of the story? | A time word (yesterday, since, by the time) or the other verbs | *He went to Delhi last week.* not *has gone* |
| **Agreement** | Does the verb match its subject in number? | The true subject, which may be hidden behind a phrase | *The list of names **is** long.* |
| **Articles** | a, an, the or nothing? | First mention or known? Singular countable? Sound of the next word | *an hour*, *the Moon*, *a university* |
| **Prepositions** | Which small word links the two ideas? | The word before it (fixed pairs) or the time/place meaning | *depend on*, *at noon*, *in 1999* |
| **Pronouns** | Who does the pronoun stand for, and in which case? | The noun it refers to; its role (subject or object) | *between you and me*, not *you and I* |
| **Connectors** | Does the link show the right relationship? | Cause, contrast, addition or time between the two ideas | *He was tired, **so** he slept.* |
| **Word form** | Is the word the right part of speech? | The slot (noun, verb, adjective, adverb) | *a **beautiful** view*, not *beauty view* |

When you meet a gap, ask which family it belongs to. Then you know where to look. A gap after "the" is often a noun or an adjective. A gap before a verb is often about agreement or tense. A gap between two clauses is nearly always a connector.

## Gap filling: the strategy {lvl=1 id=s-gap label="Foundation"}

There are two kinds: **gaps with options** (multiple choice) and **open gaps**, where you write the word yourself, sometimes with the verb given in brackets.

### Step by step

1. **Read the whole passage once.** Ignore the gaps. Find out who is speaking, what the time frame is (past, present, future), and what the topic is.
2. **Go to gap 1.** Read the five or six words before it and the five or six after it. A gap is controlled by both sides.
3. **Name the family.** Tense? Preposition? Connector?
4. **Choose or write the word.**
5. **Re-read the sentence** with your answer in place.
6. **Return at the end** and read the whole completed passage. Gaps depend on each other. If gap 1 is past, gap 4 should not be present unless there is a reason.

### With options: eliminate before you choose

Do not read the options and pick the one that "feels right". Cross out the options that **break a hard rule**.

- A singular subject with a plural verb.
- A continuous form with a stative verb (*am knowing*).
- *Will* inside an *if* clause.
- A preposition that is simply not paired with the word before it.

Then pick from what remains. If two options survive, they usually differ by meaning, so use the sense of the sentence.

### Without options: write one word, and a plain word

Open gaps usually ask for **one word**. Many answers are small function words: *of, to, in, the, and, but, so, who, which, if, that*. The meaning of the sentence tells you which. Read it aloud and ask what is missing.

If the verb is given in brackets, you are choosing the **form**, not the word. Work out the tense and the subject, then add the helpers. See the lesson on tenses for the twelve forms.

:::worked title="A short open gap passage"
Fill each blank with one suitable word.

*Priya's grandmother lives (1) ___ a village near the river. Every summer Priya goes there (2) ___ her cousins. This year they (3) ___ planning to build a boat out (4) ___ old planks, (5) ___ they are not sure it will float.*

Read it through once: present time, a happy family story, and the last sentence expresses doubt.

(1) A place inside a village: **in**. (2) Going together with cousins: **with**. (3) The subject is "they", and "planning" is an *-ing* form, so the gap needs a form of *be*: **are**. (4) "Made out of" is a fixed phrase: **of**. (5) The idea of the second clause is contrasting (they plan it, but they are unsure): **but**, because the second clause contrasts with the first.

Check the whole passage: all verbs are present, nothing clashes.
:::

:::qc
q: "He ___ the match if he had practised more."
options: ["would win", "would have won", "will win", "won"]
answer: 1
right: Right. "Had practised" is a past perfect in the if clause, so the other clause takes "would have" plus the past participle. The hypothetical event never happened.
wrong: The if clause has "had practised" (past perfect). That signals a situation that did not happen, and the main clause pairs it with "would have won".
:::

## Error spotting and editing {lvl=2 id=s-edit label="Class 10"}

In editing, a passage is given with a mistake in some lines. In the board's format, each marked line holds **one** error, and you must write what is wrong and its correction. [Unverified: layout and number of marked lines vary by year]

### The rules of the game

- **One error per marked line.** If you find two, you have probably misjudged one. Re-read.
- **Change the minimum.** Replace, insert or remove a word. Do not rewrite the line.
- **The correction must make the line right.** Check it with the lines around it.
- **Spelling counts.** A correct word, misspelt, loses the mark.
- **The error is a word-level error.** You will not be asked to restructure a sentence.

### A method

1. **Read the whole passage first,** for the story and its time frame.
2. **Take one line at a time.** Read it slowly, one word at a time, not at your normal speed. Fast reading lets your brain fix the mistake without telling you.
3. **Check the verbs first.** Tense and agreement are the most common errors. Look at each verb. Is the subject real? Does the form match the time?
4. **Then check the small words:** articles, prepositions, pronouns, connectors.
5. **If you find nothing,** check the word forms (a noun where an adjective is needed) and the fixed phrases.
6. **Write the answer** in the form asked: the faulty word, then the correction.

### How each error family shows itself

| Family | What it looks like | What to check | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tense | *Yesterday she **goes** to the bank.* | The time word; the other verbs in the passage | goes → went |
| Agreement | *The price of the tickets **have** risen.* | Find the true subject (price, not tickets) | have → has |
| Agreement | *Each of the players **were** tired.* | *Each*, *every*, *either*, *neither*, *one of* take a singular verb | were → was |
| Article | *She is **a** honest girl.* | The sound of the next word, not its spelling | a → an |
| Article | *He plays **the** cricket.* | Games and subjects take no article | the → (delete) |
| Preposition | *I am looking forward **for** the holidays.* | Fixed pairs: look forward to, depend on, good at, afraid of | for → to |
| Pronoun | *Between you and **I**, it was easy.* | After a preposition the pronoun is in the object form | I → me |
| Pronoun | *Everyone must bring **their** own pen.* [many modern writers accept this; the board key often expects *his or her*] | Check the board's convention | [Unverified] |
| Connector | *He was ill, **although** he stayed at home.* | Does the link show cause, contrast, addition or time? | although → so |
| Word form | *She sang very **beautiful**.* | Adverbs modify verbs | beautiful → beautifully |

Notice what the table says about **where to look**: for agreement, find the real subject; for articles, listen to the next sound; for prepositions, look left; for connectors, ask what relationship the two clauses have.

:::note title="Sound, not spelling, decides a or an" tone=tip
Use **an** before a vowel **sound** and **a** before a consonant **sound**. *An hour* (the h is silent), *an honest man*, *a university* (it starts with a "yoo" sound), *a one-rupee coin* (it starts with a "w" sound).
:::

:::qc
q: Which sentence contains an error?
options: ["Neither of the boys has finished.", "The news are good.", "Each of the rooms is clean."]
answer: 1
right: Right. News looks plural but is a singular uncountable noun: "The news is good."
wrong: Look at each subject. "Neither" and "each" are singular and take has and is. "News" ends in -s but is singular, so "are" is the error.
:::

### The tricky part: a line that looks wrong but is not

A passage may contain a line that is right. Do not invent an error. Check the line against the family list. If every verb, article, preposition, pronoun and connector is right, leave it. Spotting an error in a correct line is the usual way to lose a mark.

:::worked title="Editing one line at a time"
Passage (lines 2 to 4 are marked; each marked line has one error):

1. *Every Sunday, my father takes me to a market near our house.*
2. *He buy fresh vegetables and I help him carry the bags.*
3. *Last Sunday, he has bought a sack of potatoes.*
4. *The shopkeeper gave me a orange as a gift.*

Line 1 is not marked and is correct, so leave it alone.
Line 2: *buy* should be *buys*; the subject is *he* in the present simple.
Line 3: *has bought* should be *bought*; "Last Sunday" is a finished time.
Line 4: *a orange* should be *an orange*; orange begins with a vowel sound.
:::

## Omission {lvl=2 id=s-omit label="Class 10"}

In an omission question, a word has been dropped. You find the **gap** and say what is missing. It is the editing task turned around: instead of a wrong word, you are looking for a missing one.

Missing words are nearly always small: *a, an, the, to, of, in, on, by, and, but, that, who, is, are, has*. Read the line slowly, one word at a time, and stop where the sentence stumbles.

| Faulty line | What is missing | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *The meeting is scheduled Monday.* | **for** (or *on*) | A time needs a preposition |
| *She is student of Class X.* | **a** | A singular countable noun needs a determiner |
| *I want go to the park.* | **to** | *Want* is followed by *to* + base verb |
| *He asked me where was going.* | **I** | Every clause needs its own subject |

The board usually asks for the missing word and the words before and after it. Write all three, in order, so the examiner can see exactly where the word goes.

## Reordering {lvl=2 id=s-reorder label="Class 10"}

Reordering has two forms. In one, **words and phrases** are jumbled and you build a sentence. In the other, whole **sentences** are jumbled and you build a paragraph. The thinking is different for each.

### Reordering words into a sentence

English has a fixed backbone: **subject, then verb, then object or complement**, with adverbs and phrases attached around it. Build it in this order.

1. Find the **subject** (usually a noun or a pronoun that begins with a capital or carries *the*).
2. Find the **verb**, with its helpers (*was*, *had*, *has been*).
3. Attach the **object or complement**.
4. Place the **phrases**: time, place, manner.
5. Read the result aloud. Does it say something sensible?

:::worked title="Words into a sentence"
Jumble: *had / the / we / rain / before / reached / started / home*

Subject: **we**. Verb: **had reached**. Object/complement: **home**. A clause of time: **before the rain started**.

Answer: *We had reached home before the rain started.*

Check: the earlier event (reaching) takes the past perfect, the later one (starting) the past simple. Consistent with the tense lesson.
:::

### Reordering sentences into a paragraph

A paragraph is held together by clues. Use them in this order.

| Clue | What to look for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening sentence** | It introduces the topic and does not refer back. It contains no *this*, *he*, *however* with nothing before it. | *Last year, an old farmer found a spring in the desert.* |
| **Pronouns** | A pronoun (*he, it, they, this*) must come after the noun it replaces. | The noun first, then *he* |
| **Connectors** | *However, therefore, then, also, finally* refer to what came before. | *However* needs something to contrast with |
| **Time order** | Events follow the clock. *First, then, later, by evening.* | Morning before evening |
| **General to specific** | The big idea comes before the details. | A claim, then its example |
| **Closing sentence** | A result, a conclusion, a lesson. | *That is how the village got its first well.* |

Work like this. Pick the opening sentence. Place the sentence that follows naturally. Then check each pair: does the second sentence make sense **only because** the first came before it? When you have the order, read the paragraph through once.

:::qc
q: Which sentence is most likely to open the paragraph?
options: ["However, few people believed him.", "Last year, an old farmer claimed to have found a spring in the desert.", "This proved him right in the end."]
answer: 1
right: Right. It introduces the farmer and the topic. The other two refer back with "however" and "this", so they need a sentence before them.
wrong: An opening sentence cannot lean on a previous one. "However" and "this" both look back, so they must come later.
:::

## A marking checklist {lvl=2 id=s-check label="Class 10"}

Use this checklist at the end of each question. It is also how the examiner thinks.

**Gap filling**
- Did I read the whole passage before filling?
- Does each answer fit the words **before and after** the gap?
- Does the verb match the subject **and** the time frame?
- Is each answer one word if one word was asked, and spelt correctly?
- Does the finished passage read smoothly from start to end?

**Editing**
- Did I find exactly one error per marked line?
- Did I check verbs, then small words, then word forms?
- Is my correction the minimum change, spelt correctly?
- Did I avoid "correcting" a line that was right?

**Omission**
- Did I give the missing word and the words before and after it?

**Reordering**
- Did I find the opening sentence first (it does not refer back)?
- Do pronouns and connectors come after what they refer to?
- Did I read the final order aloud?

## Handling the hard cases {lvl=3 id=s-hard label="Beyond the board"}

The rules above solve most items. These finer points handle the rest.

### More than one answer is possible

Open gaps sometimes allow several words. In *"He stayed at home ___ it was raining"*, you can write *because*, *since* or *as*. The key accepts all of them, normally. Choose the simplest. A different word is only wrong when it changes the meaning or breaks a rule.

### The word that fits one side only

A wrong answer usually fits one side of the gap. *"She is interested ___ painting"* looks like it wants *in* on the right (interested in), and so it does. A tempting wrong answer *"on"* fits the idea of "about painting" but not the fixed phrase on the left. Always test both sides.

### Hidden subjects and distance

In *"The collection of old coins and stamps ___ displayed in the hall"*, the verb is far from its subject. The subject is *collection* (singular), not *coins and stamps*. The distance between a subject and its verb is the examiner's favourite trap. Cross out the phrase in the middle, then read the subject and the verb together.

### Reading for cohesion

Good paragraphs hold together in three ways: **reference** (pronouns that point back), **links** (connectors that show the relationship) and **repetition or substitution** (a word reappears or is replaced by a near synonym). When two sentence orders both seem possible, count the cohesive ties for each. The order with more unbroken ties is usually the one the writer meant.

### Why the same mistakes keep appearing

Examiners reuse error families because the mistakes come from real habits. Many come from translating a pattern from another language (*I am having a doubt*, *discuss about the matter*, *return back*). Learn the English pattern as a whole phrase (*discuss the matter*; *return*; *I have a doubt*), and the error stops looking right.

### A final step: write for the reader

Even in the exam, editing is a small act of kindness to a reader. The corrected passage must say clearly what the writer meant, in the smallest number of changes. If your corrected version reads naturally, it is almost certainly right.

:::keys

3. Name the error family first: tense, agreement, articles, prepositions, pronouns, connectors or word form. This tells you where to look. {lvl=1}

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Filling a gap by looking only at the word on its left: always read the words on both sides. {lvl=1}
- Writing two words where one was asked: read the instruction before you answer. {lvl=1}
- Skipping the first read-through: tense and tone clues often come from other sentences. {lvl=1}
- "Correcting" a line that was right: if all verbs and small words are right, leave it. {lvl=2}
- Rewriting the whole line in editing: replace or insert only the faulty word. {lvl=2}
- Choosing the verb by the nearest noun: "The price of the tickets have risen" should be "has risen". {lvl=2}
- Choosing a or an by spelling: "a hour" should be "an hour", "an university" should be "a university". {lvl=2}
- Using "I" after a preposition: "between you and I" should be "between you and me". {lvl=2}
- Choosing a connector by habit instead of relationship: "He was ill, although he stayed at home" should use "so". {lvl=2}
- Putting a "However" or a "This" sentence first in a reordered paragraph: the opening sentence never looks back. {lvl=2}
- Leaving out the neighbouring words in an omission answer. {lvl=2}
- Not reading the finished passage as a whole: mismatched tenses or numbers show up only then. {lvl=2}
- Thinking only one answer is ever possible: where several fit, pick the simplest and most natural. {lvl=3}
:::
