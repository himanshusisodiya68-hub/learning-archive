## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

Horace Danby is about fifty, unmarried and well thought of. He makes locks, has two helpers and lives with a housekeeper who fusses over his health. The only thing that troubles him is hay fever in summer. His secret is that he loves rare, expensive books, and the only way he can afford them is to rob one safe every year. He has done this since he served his first and only sentence, fifteen years earlier, in a prison library.

This July, after studying the house at Shotover Grange for two weeks, he decides the time is right. The family is in London and the two servants have gone to the cinema. Using the kitchen key left on a hook, and wearing gloves, he enters and calms a small dog by using its name. The safe, hidden behind a poor painting in the drawing room, holds jewels worth about fifteen thousand pounds, of which he hopes to get at least five thousand. But the flowers on the table set off his hay fever, and as he sneezes a young woman in red appears and gently names his complaint.

She says she lives there. She could call the police, but after a little talk she decides to help him if he helps her. She explains that she forgot the safe's number and wants her jewels for a party that night. He is only too glad. He opens the safe, hands her the jewels and goes off happy. Two days later he is looking for his next safe when the police arrive. His fingerprints are all over the drawing room, and the real wife of the owner, a grey-haired, sharp-tongued woman of sixty, says she knows nothing about his story. He is sent back to prison, where he is now the assistant librarian and furious at the thought of honour among thieves.

:::qc
q: Why does Horace Danby rob a safe once a year?
options: ["He is poor and cannot pay his housekeeper", "He wants money to buy rare, expensive books", "He enjoys the danger of being almost caught"]
answer: 1
right: Correct. He plans each theft carefully to get enough for twelve months of book-buying, which he does secretly through an agent.
wrong: Look at the second paragraph again. His business does well enough; the money is for the books he loves.
:::

| Character | What we learn | Evidence in the story |
|---|---|---|

Think of the lady and Horace as mirror images. Both are thieves, both sneak into rich houses, and both are polite. The difference is that she studies *him*, while he only studies the house.

## How the lady fools him {lvl=2 id=s-trick label="Class 10"}

This is the heart of the first "Think about it" question. The deception works because every detail fits the picture of a lady of the house, and Horace's own wishes fill the gaps.

| What she does or says | Why it looks genuine | What it really does |
|---|---|---|
| Speaks from the doorway as if she has the right to be there | She is not hiding or rushing | She puts him on the defensive |
| Names his hay fever and recommends a cure | It feels kind and personal | It makes him feel known and lowers his guard |
| The dog rubs against her, and she says anyone would think she had been away a month | Pets welcome family | It works as proof of belonging, without any real proof |
| Tidies the ornaments by the fireplace | Owners straighten their own things | A small act of ownership |
| Says she came back just in time, then talks of her husband and her jewels | A specific, plausible story | It gives him a reason to help |
| Admits she was about to ring the police, then bargains | She seems fair and human | Hope of escape makes him eager to agree |
| Says she will mend the safe herself, and her husband will not be home for a month | It solves his worry about breaking the safe | It removes any reason to doubt |

**Why he does not suspect.** First, he is frightened of prison, and a person who is frightened wants the exit to be real. Second, she is young, pretty and amused, not hostile, and he cannot imagine a thief behaving so calmly. Third, he studied the house, its wiring, paths and garden, but never the family, so he cannot compare her with anyone. Fourth, he is flattered that she treats him as a skilled worker. Fifth, he takes off his gloves to give her his lighter, which shows that his attention is on pleasing her, not on protecting himself.

## Where Horace goes wrong {lvl=2 id=s-faults label="Class 10"}

Horace is a "meticulous planner", yet he fails. The reasons come in layers.

1. **He researches the house but not the people.** He watched the building for two weeks but did not find out who lived there or what they looked like. A magazine article gave him the plan of the rooms, and he did not think to check anything else.
2. **He assumes the house is empty.** He saw the servants leave and took for granted that nobody else would turn up.
3. **He lets fear and hope decide.** The moment she hints at mercy he agrees to anything, without asking a single question.
4. **He removes his gloves.** Until then he had been careful about fingerprints. The first break in discipline is also the one that convicts him.
5. **He breaks his own promise.** He swears he will never rob again, then plans another job on the third morning. The man who is ready to cheat the law is not as shocked as he should be when someone cheats him.
6. **He ignores his weak spot.** His hay fever, set off by the flowers, tells her he is there, and it also distracts him from watching her.

:::qc
q: Which single mistake directly leaves evidence for the police?
options: ["Cutting the burglar alarm wire", "Using the dog's name", "Taking off his gloves", "Buying books through an agent"]
answer: 2
right: Yes. Once the gloves are off, his fingerprints are on the lighter and around the safe. That is what the police use.
wrong: Think about what the police actually find. It is not the wire or the dog but marks on the room.
:::

## Themes: trust, honesty and means {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10"}

**Trust.** The title is a question: who can be trusted, and on what evidence? Horace trusts a stranger with no proof. The lady trusts that Horace's fear will make him compliant. The only person who deals fairly is the narrator, who tells us in the first page that Horace is "not completely honest".

**Honest and respectable are not the same.** The narrator calls Horace "good and respectable" but "not completely honest". Respectable is about how others see you; honest is about what you do when nobody is watching. Horace can be respectable and dishonest at once, as many people are.

**Why he is not a typical thief.** He steals once a year, takes only what he needs, steals from the very rich, has no violent streak, hates prison and talks politely. A typical thief takes for greed or habit; he takes for a passion. That does not make it lawful, but it makes him unusual and a little sympathetic.

**Do ends justify means?** Horace says he has "a very good reason" for stealing. Our book asks whether a good intention makes a wrong act right. The story's reply is a shrug and a smile: his reason is a taste for books, not a need for food, and the cost falls on other people. A purpose can explain an act, but it does not excuse it.

## The author's craft {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Twist ending.** Up to the last paragraphs the reader thinks Horace is a clever burglar who has been lucky. Only when the real wife denies everything do we reread the lady's earlier lines and realise she was a thief. A good twist depends on fair clues, and there are several.

**Foreshadowing.** The kind young woman says "I have always liked the wrong kind of people", which sounds like flirting but warns us. Her first line is also about his sneeze, a reminder that she has been watching him for a while. Horace's early remark that people are foolish when they own valuable things comes back on him.

**Irony, in three layers.**
1. *Situational:* the man whose trade is locks and safes is tricked into opening one.
2. *Dramatic:* readers who look back see what Horace could not.
3. *Verbal:* the narrator says he thinks of "the charming, clever young lady who was in the same profession as he was". He praises the very person who ruined him, and still gets angry at the idea of honour among thieves.

**Characterisation by small detail.** The writer rarely says what a character is like. We learn Horace is cautious from the gloves and the cut alarm wire; we learn he is kind-hearted from calling the dog by its name; we learn he is vain from his thought about foolish owners.

**Narrative voice.** A calm third-person narrator mostly follows Horace's thoughts, so we share his confidence. The tone is light and gently mocking, never cruel. This closeness is why the ending surprises us as it surprises him.

**The title.** *A Question of Trust* sounds serious, but the story answers it comically: you should not trust anyone who benefits from your trust, least of all a fellow cheat.

:::qc
q: The phrase "who was in the same profession as he was" is an example of
options: ["a simile", "ironic understatement", "a metaphor for the safe", "a rhetorical question"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. The narrator quietly calls a thief's profession a profession and pretends there is a kind of professional pride, which is dry, ironic humour at Horace's expense.
wrong: The sentence does not compare two things or ask a question. It says something mild and polite about something serious, which is understatement used for irony.
:::

## Writing about the chapter {lvl=2 id=s-write label="Class 10"}

| Question type | What the examiner expects | Plan |
|---|---|---|
| "Did you suspect...?" (opinion) | A clear yes or no with a reason from the text | Say when you doubted (for example, when she says she lives there but offers no proof), say why, then comment |
| "How does X deceive Y?" (analysis) | Three or four clues, each explained | Name the clue, say what it suggested, say what it really did |
| "Why is the description apt?" (character) | A quote from the opening, then evidence | Define both sides: *good and respectable* and *not completely honest*, then give two actions |
| "Where did he go wrong?" (cause) | A list of mistakes with reasons | Rank them: not studying the family, taking off the gloves, trusting without proof |
| "Do you think...?" (discussion) | Both sides, then your view | State a side, give a reason, concede one point to the other side, conclude |

**A model structure for any answer of 80 to 100 words:** a topic sentence that answers the question; two pieces of evidence with a short quote or paraphrase; a closing comment that returns to the question. Keep to your own words and put any quotation (under fifteen words) in inverted commas.

## Arguing both sides {lvl=3 id=s-argue label="Beyond the board"}

The "Talk about it" prompts have no right answer, but good answers weigh evidence. Here is a way to organise the argument.

| Question | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Was Horace unfairly punished? | He was tricked and carried the whole blame; the real thief walked away; the wife denies the story | He did break into the house and was caught opening a safe; he had stolen every year; he served time before and knew the risk |
| Do intentions justify actions? | Some actions, such as lying to save a life, seem excusable when the motive is truly serious | The motive can be self-serving; Horace's reason is a taste for books, and the people robbed did not agree |
| Is dishonesty ever excusable? | Small, harmless untruths (a polite white lie) are often accepted | A line is crossed when someone is harmed, or when the excuse is repeated every year |

Weigh two things: who is harmed, and whether the person would accept the same action done to them. Horace rejects honour among thieves only after he is a victim.

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not call the lady the "owner's wife" in an answer. She only pretends to be, and the real wife appears at the end. {lvl=1}
- Do not say Horace is a violent or hardened criminal. The text shows a polite, careful man who is afraid of prison. {lvl=2}
- Do not retell the whole plot for a "why" question. Give the reason, one clue, and one comment. {lvl=2}
- Irony needs two sides: what was expected and what happened. Naming only one earns half marks. {lvl=2}
- Do not say "honour among thieves" is proved true. The ending shows the opposite. {lvl=2}
- In opinion questions, always give a reason from the text. A bare "I think he was punished fairly" earns little. {lvl=2}
- Do not quote long passages; a short phrase with your own comment is better. {lvl=3}
:::
