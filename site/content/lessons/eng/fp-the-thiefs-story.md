## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

The narrator is a fifteen-year-old thief who calls himself Hari Singh, a name he changes every month to stay ahead of the police. He meets Anil, a tall, lean young man of about twenty-five, at a wrestling match. Anil looks easy-going and kind, so the boy flatters him and asks for work. Anil cannot pay, but he agrees to feed him if he can cook. The first meal is so bad that Anil gives it to a stray dog, yet he lets the boy stay and promises to teach him to cook, write his name and, soon, whole sentences.

Hari Singh settles in. He makes the tea, goes to the bazaar, and skims about a rupee a day off the shopping, which Anil probably guesses but ignores. Anil is a writer whose money comes in fits and starts; he borrows one week, lends the next. One evening he brings home a small bundle of notes from a publisher and tucks it under the mattress. The boy notes that Anil has given him a key and trusts him fully. That very trust makes the robbery oddly difficult, but he decides it is time for "real work".

That night he takes the money, 600 rupees in fifties, and runs to the station. He hesitates and the Lucknow Express leaves without him. Alone, cold and wet from the rain, he sits under the clock tower at midnight and thinks. He knows exactly how Anil will look when he finds out: sad, not for the money but for the lost trust. He also realises he has thrown away the chance to learn to read and write. He returns and slips the notes back under the mattress while Anil sleeps.

In the morning Anil has already made the tea. He holds out a fifty-rupee note, saying he made money yesterday and the boy will now be paid regularly. The note is still wet from the rain. Then he adds that today they will begin writing sentences. The thief understands that Anil knows, and that he will never say so. Hari Singh smiles, and for once the smile comes without effort.

:::qc
q: Why does Hari Singh decide to go back to Anil's room?
options: ["He is caught by a policeman at the station", "He realises he would lose his chance to learn to read and write, and to become a respected man", "Anil sends a friend to ask for the money"]
answer: 1
right: Correct. The thief admits he had forgotten the lessons in the excitement of the theft. He reasons that being clever and respected is a bigger prize than a few hundred rupees.
wrong: Nobody catches or calls him. Look at what he thinks about under the clock tower: it is about whole sentences and what they might bring him.
:::

## Characters {lvl=2 id=s-chars label="Class 10"}

| Character | What we learn | Evidence in the story |
|---|---|---|
| Hari Singh (the narrator) | Cunning, ambitious, lonely, a liar by habit, but also capable of conscience and gratitude | He takes a new name every month; he lies about cooking; he overcharges on shopping; yet he cannot enjoy the stolen money and returns it |
| Anil | Generous, trusting, easy-going, wise, quietly perceptive | He feeds and houses a stranger; gives him a key; laughs at the burnt dinner; gives a wet note and says nothing about the theft |
| The wrestlers and the stray dog | Minor background figures | The wrestlers set the scene of the meeting; the dog eats the first meal the boy cooks |

**Hari Singh in more detail.** He is proud of his work and talks about it like a trade (he calls himself "experienced and fairly successful"). He studies faces, and he judges people coolly: a greedy man can afford to be robbed, he says, but a careless one is harder to rob. Yet he is not heartless. He feels grateful for the lessons, hesitates at the station, and is nervous about returning the notes.

**Anil in more detail.** His trust is not stupidity. The thief suspects that Anil knows he cheats on the shopping and does not mind. When he discovers the theft, he answers it with the one thing that can change the boy: a regular wage and the promise of a lesson. He treats Hari Singh as a person who can learn, not as a criminal.

## Themes {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10"}

**Trust and betrayal.** Trust is the story's engine. Anil gives it freely and the boy sees it as a weakness to exploit. By the end it is the boy's strongest reason to change, because he does not want to see the sadness he has predicted on Anil's face.

**Education as a way out.** At the start Hari Singh values writing as a tool: once he can write "like an educated man", there will be no limit to what he can achieve (he means cheat). After the theft his view shifts. He sees that whole sentences could bring him more than a few hundred rupees and that being "a really big man, a clever and respected man" is a different prize from stealing. Learning is no longer a trick; it is a way to become somebody.

| Stage | How he thinks about education | Sign in the text |
|---|---|---|
| Early | A useful tool for a thief | "No limit to what I could achieve" |
| After the theft | A way to be respected, worth more than the money | Whole sentences "could one day bring me more" |
| Ending | An invitation he accepts | He smiles when Anil says they will start writing sentences |

**Forgiveness without a lecture.** Anil never says "I know". His wet note and his words about sentences give the boy a way back with his pride intact. Compare what most employers would do: call the police, throw him out, or lecture him. Anil's way works because the boy has already judged himself.

**Poverty and circumstance.** The boy has no family and no friends ("friends were more trouble than help"). The story does not excuse theft, but it asks what circumstances make a fifteen-year-old a thief.

## Why the thief returns, and why Anil does not call the police {lvl=2 id=s-why label="Class 10"}

These two questions come straight from the chapter's "Think about it" section, so board answers should be structured around them.

**Why he returns.**
1. *The train he misses.* A chance hesitation gives him time to think.
2. *Loneliness.* He has no friends and nowhere to go; the only person he really knows is the man he robbed.
3. *The lessons.* He remembers that Anil is teaching him to write sentences. That is a prize no thief can steal.
4. *Conscience.* He imagines Anil's sadness at the lost trust, and he does not want to cause it.

**Why Anil stays silent.**
1. *He understands the boy.* He treated him as a person who could change, so he will not treat him as a criminal now.
2. *Trust is his method.* Handing him a key was never a mistake; it was an offer.
3. *The police would end the lessons and probably the boy's chance.* Silence gives both of them a fresh start.
4. *Being a writer.* Anil makes his living by noticing people, so he reads the boy better than the boy reads him [Inference].

**Do most people act like Anil?** Most employers would likely have called the police or dismissed the boy. Anil is different because he values the person over the property, and because his trust is a considered choice, not carelessness.

## Writing a character sketch {lvl=2 id=s-sketch label="Class 10"}

A board question may ask you to describe a character in about 120 words. Use this layout.

| Part | What to write | Example for Hari Singh |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Who he is and his role | Hari Singh is the fifteen-year-old thief who narrates the story. |
| Trait + evidence | Two or three traits, each tied to the text | Cunning: he changes his name every month. Ambitious: he wants to write like an educated man. |
| Change | How he is different at the end | He returns the money and begins to learn. |
| Closing | What he shows or teaches us | Even a thief may reform when someone believes in him. |

**Checklist.** Use the present tense. Give a trait word, then proof. Do not retell the plot. Do not copy long phrases; a short quoted phrase with your own comment is enough.

## The writer's craft {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**First-person narration.** The thief tells us what he was thinking, which makes us uneasy partners in the crime. We feel his nervousness at the door and his satisfaction when the notes are back. Because it is "I", we never see inside Anil. We read his heart only through what he does, so his silence at the end is all the more powerful.

**A narrator who half-understands himself.** Hari Singh claims to be a hard-boiled professional, yet he says he hesitated "for some reason I can't explain". The reader sees what the narrator cannot: his conscience has woken. This gap between what he says and what the reader understands is a form of **irony**.

**Irony.** Several kinds appear.
1. *Situational:* the thief steals from the one man who would have given him a little spending money the next morning.
2. *Verbal:* he calls writing "a queer way to make a living", not realising he is about to want a writer's skill for himself.
3. *Reversal:* the thief becomes the one who is forgiven, and the victim is the one who gives a gift.

**Symbols and motifs.**

| Detail | What it may stand for |
|---|---|
| Key to the door | Anil's trust, freely given |
| The clock tower at midnight | A moment of judgement, time to think |
| The rain and the wet note | The night's secret shared silently between them |
| The "most appealing" smile | The thief's old trick, becoming a real smile at the end |

**Understatement and the closing scene.** Nothing dramatic happens in the last paragraph. Anil makes tea, hands over a note, mentions sentences. The writer uses plain, quiet language, and that plainness carries the emotion. It also leaves the reader to put the whole moral together.

**The smile motif.** The smile appears at the start as a tool for getting what he wants. At the end it "came by itself, without any effort". The same gesture now means gratitude, and the circle shows the change in one detail.

:::qc
q: The thief tells us he hesitated at the station "for some reason I can't explain". What does this phrase show about him?
options: ["He is a weak person who cannot make up his mind", "His conscience is working before he is willing to admit it", "He was afraid of the police"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The narrator acts as if the pause is a mystery, but the reader sees the early stirring of conscience that leads him back.
wrong: He has the money, the platform and the chance to jump on the train. The story hints at something inside him that is not fear. What might it be?
:::

## Talking about the story {lvl=3 id=s-talk label="Beyond the board"}

The chapter ends with discussion prompts. Good discussion answers have three parts: a clear opinion, a piece of evidence from the text, and one real-life connection.

| Prompt | A way into it |
|---|---|
| Are Anil and Hari Singh found only in fiction? | Fiction exaggerates, but kind employers and street children both exist. Name a general pattern rather than a real person. |
| Is it significant that Anil is a struggling writer? | He knows what it is to be uncertain of income, so money does not control him; and writers study people. |
| What turns a fifteen-year-old into a thief? | Having no family, no schooling and no safety net. Say that circumstances explain, but do not excuse, his acts. |
| Where is it set, and what language do they speak? | The names (Jumna, Lucknow, maidan, rupees) point to North India [Inference]. The characters probably spoke Hindi or Hindustani, and the author gives us an English version. |

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not call Anil foolish. The text shows his trust as a choice, and the story rewards it. {lvl=1}
- Do not say the thief returns the money because he was afraid of being caught. The text points to the lessons, loneliness and conscience. {lvl=2}
- For "Why does Anil not hand him to the police?", give reasons from the text, not just "he was kind". {lvl=2}
- Do not retell the whole plot in a character sketch. Trait, evidence, comment. {lvl=2}
- Remember that the story is told by the thief, so statements about Anil's thoughts are the narrator's guess, not fact. {lvl=3}
- Quote short phrases only, and always add your own comment. {lvl=3}
:::
