## Words are built from parts {lvl=1 id=s-parts label="Foundation"}

Take the word **unbreakable**. Cover the ends and you are left with *break*, a word you already know. Add *-able* and you have *breakable*, something that can be broken. Add *un-* at the front and the meaning flips: *unbreakable*, not able to be broken.

Those three pieces have names.

- The **root** (or base word) carries the core meaning. Here it is *break*.
- A **prefix** goes in front and changes the meaning. Here it is *un-*, meaning *not*.
- A **suffix** goes at the end and usually changes the part of speech. Here it is *-able*, which turns a verb into an adjective.

The general name for a prefix or a suffix is an **affix**. A root that can stand alone, like *break* or *happy*, is also called a base word. Some roots only live inside longer words: *spect* (look) never appears alone, but it is in *inspect*, *spectator* and *prospect*.

Not every word has all three parts. *Happy* has none, *happiness* has a suffix, *unhappy* a prefix, and *unhappiness* has both.

| Word | Prefix | Root | Suffix | Meaning from the parts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| unhappiness | un- (not) | happy | -ness (state of) | the state of not being happy |
| rewrite | re- (again) | write | none | write again |
| careless | none | care | -less (without) | without care |
| disagreement | dis- (not) | agree | -ment (result) | the state of not agreeing |
| replayable | re- (again) | play | -able (can be) | can be played again |

:::qc
q: In the word "disappearance", which part is the root?
options: ["appear", "dis", "ance", "disappear"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Dis- is the prefix, -ance is the suffix, and appear is the base word in the middle.
wrong: Strip the front and back. Dis- is a prefix and -ance is a suffix. What is left in the middle is the root, appear.
:::

## Prefixes change the meaning {lvl=1 id=s-prefixes label="Foundation"}

A prefix does not change the word's job: *happy* and *unhappy* are both adjectives. It changes what the word **means**, most often to its opposite, to a number, to a place, or to a time.

| Job | Prefix | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Not, opposite | un- | not | unkind, unfair, unlock |
| | in- / im- / il- / ir- | not | incorrect, impossible, illegal, irregular |
| | dis- | not, apart | dislike, disappear, discover |
| | non- | not | nonsense, non-stop |
| | mis- | wrongly, badly | misspell, misunderstand |
| | anti- | against | antibiotic, anticlockwise |
| Again, back | re- | again, back | rewrite, return, rebuild |
| Time or place | pre- | before | prepaid, preview |
| | post- | after | postpone, post-war |
| | inter- | between | international, interval |
| | trans- | across | transport, transfer |
| | sub- | under | submarine, subway |
| | super- | above, beyond | supermarket, superstar |
| | ex- | out, former | export, ex-captain |
| Number | uni-, mono- | one | uniform, monologue |
| | bi-, di- | two | bicycle, bilingual |
| | tri- | three | triangle, tricolour |
| | quad- | four | quadrilateral, quadruple |
| | multi-, poly- | many | multinational, polygon |
| | semi- | half | semicircle, semi-final |
| Other | co- / con- / com- | together, with | cooperate, connect, companion |
| | auto- | self | autograph, automatic |
| | bene- / mal- | good / bad | benefit, malfunction |

### Which spelling of in-?

The prefix **in-** meaning *not* changes to match the first letter of the root, so the word is easier to say.

| Before | Use | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| b, m, p | im- | imbalance, immature, impossible |
| l | il- | illegal, illiterate |
| r | ir- | irregular, irresponsible |
| any other letter | in- | incorrect, invisible |

Say *inpossible* quickly and your lips want to close for the *p*, so the *n* turns into an *m*. That is the whole rule.

### The opposite is not always un-

Not every word takes every prefix. We say *unhappy*, *impossible*, *disagree*, *irregular* and *non-stop*, but never *unpossible* or *dishappy*. There is no rule that picks the prefix, so learn each opposite together with its word.

:::note title="Two meanings of in-" tone=warn
In *incorrect* and *inactive*, in- means **not**. In *inhale*, *income* and *inflow*, it means **in** or **into**. That is why *flammable* and *inflammable* mean the same thing (inflammable comes from setting something *in* flames, not from a negative). Safety signs now say *flammable* to avoid the confusion. Likewise *invaluable* means **very** valuable, not valueless.
:::

:::qc
q: Which is the correct opposite of "regular"?
options: ["irregular", "unregular", "inregular", "disregular"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Before the letter r, the prefix in- becomes ir-: irregular.
wrong: The prefix in- changes to match the first letter of the root. Before r it becomes ir-.
:::

## Suffixes change the part of speech {lvl=1 id=s-suffixes label="Foundation"}

A suffix works on the job the word does in a sentence. *Kind* describes a person (adjective). *Kindness* names a quality (noun). *Kindly* describes how someone acts (adverb). Same root, three jobs.

**Suffixes that make nouns**

| Suffix | Means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -ness | state or quality | kindness, darkness |
| -ity | state or quality | purity, curiosity |
| -tion / -sion | act or result | invention, decision |
| -ment | act or result | enjoyment, payment |
| -ance / -ence | state or action | importance, difference |
| -ship | state, skill | friendship, leadership |
| -hood | state, group | childhood, neighbourhood |
| -dom | state, realm | freedom, kingdom |
| -ism | belief, practice | tourism, heroism |
| -er / -or / -ist / -ian | the person who | teacher, actor, scientist, librarian |

**Suffixes that make adjectives**

| Suffix | Means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -ful | full of | hopeful, careful |
| -less | without | hopeless, careless |
| -able / -ible | can be | readable, visible |
| -ous | full of | dangerous, famous |
| -al | to do with | musical, national |
| -ive | tending to | active, creative |
| -ic | to do with | heroic, historic |
| -y | having | rainy, thirsty |
| -ish | like, rather | childish, reddish |

**Suffixes that make verbs and adverbs**

| Suffix | Makes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -ify | verb, "make" | simplify, purify |
| -ise / -ize | verb, "make or become" | modernise, memorise |
| -en | verb, "make or become" | widen, strengthen |
| -ate | verb, "cause" | activate, motivate |
| -ly | adverb | slowly, happily |
| -ward(s) | adverb of direction | homeward, backwards |
| -wise | adverb of manner or direction | clockwise, otherwise |

A single root can move through all the jobs: **act, action, active, activate, actively, activity**. This family is a word-formation chain, and the board's "correct form of the word" tasks (see the board section) ask you to pick the right link for the sentence.

:::note title="Not every -ly word is an adverb" tone=tip
Many words ending in -ly are adjectives: *friendly*, *lovely*, *daily*, *lonely*. The test is the job, not the ending: *a friendly dog* (adjective), *she spoke kindly* (adverb).
:::

:::qc
q: Which suffix turns the adjective "simple" into a verb meaning "make simple"?
options: ["-ify", "-ness", "-ful", "-ly"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Simple becomes simplify, a verb that means to make something simple.
wrong: You need a verb-making suffix. -ness gives a noun, -ful an adjective and -ly an adverb. -ify means "make".
:::

## Spelling when parts join {lvl=1 id=s-spelling label="Foundation"}

Adding a part often changes the spelling at the joint. Four rules cover most cases.

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| Drop a silent **e** before a suffix that starts with a vowel; keep it before one that starts with a consonant | hope → hoping, but hopeful; love → lovable, but lovely |
| A word ending in a consonant + **y** changes the y to **i** | happy → happiness, happily; beauty → beautiful. After a vowel the y stays: play → playful |
| A short word with one vowel and one final consonant doubles the consonant before a vowel suffix | run → running; stop → stopped; big → bigger |
| The suffix **-ful** has just one l | careful, thankful, not carefull |

Exceptions are real. After a soft c or g the e stays: *notice → noticeable*, *courage → courageous*. If a prefix ends in the same letter that starts the root, keep both: *un + necessary = unnecessary*, *dis + satisfied = dissatisfied*, *mis + spell = misspell*. Adding a prefix never changes the spelling of the root.

:::qc
q: Which spelling is correct?
options: ["happiness", "happyness", "happines", "happynes"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The y after a consonant changes to i, and the suffix -ness keeps both of its s's: happiness.
wrong: Happy ends in a consonant + y, so y changes to i. -ness is added whole, with two s's.
:::

## Greek and Latin roots {lvl=2 id=s-roots label="Class 10 board"}

Prefixes and suffixes are the cheap part. The roots are where the mass of English vocabulary lives. A large share of formal and scientific English comes from Latin and Greek roots, so a short list of roots opens hundreds of words.

## Taking an unknown word apart {lvl=2 id=s-decode label="Class 10 board"}

When a passage contains a word you do not know, do not panic. Cut and test.

1. **Read the sentence**, and guess whether the word is a noun, verb, adjective or adverb. The suffix helps.
2. **Peel off the suffix** and then the prefix. What is left is the root.
3. **Give each part a meaning** from the tables above.
4. **Join the meanings** into a plain guess.
5. **Test it** in the sentence. If it makes sense, keep it. If it does not, the word has drifted from its root, so trust the context.

:::worked title="Four unknown words in a minute"
**inaudible.** *in-* (not) + *aud* (hear) + *-ible* (can be). So: not able to be heard. *Her reply was inaudible at the back of the hall.* It is an adjective.

**retrospective.** *retro-* (back) + *spect* (look) + *-ive* (tending to). So: looking back. A *retrospective* exhibition looks back over an artist's work.

**circumnavigate.** *circum-* (around) + *navig* (sail, from the Latin for ship) + *-ate* (verb). So: to sail around. *Magellan's expedition was the first to circumnavigate the globe* [Unverified: the usual attribution; the famous leader died before the voyage ended].

**intractable.** *in-* (not) + *tract* (pull, handle) + *-able* (can be). So: not able to be pulled or handled, hence stubborn or hard to manage. *An intractable problem* resists every attempt to solve it.
:::

:::qc
q: "Unprecedented" appears in a news report about a record flood. Which meaning fits the parts un + pre + ced (go) + ent?
options: ["not having gone before, never happening before", "happening again", "going backwards", "easy to manage"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Un- (not) + pre- (before) + cede (go) gives "not gone before", an event with no earlier example.
wrong: Put the parts together in order: not + before + go. An event that has not gone before has no earlier example.
:::

## Word families and look-alike pairs {lvl=2 id=s-families label="Class 10 board"}

A root can change its job by changing its suffix. Knowing the whole family is what a board question on word forms tests.

| Noun | Verb | Adjective | Adverb |
|---|---|---|---|
| decision | decide | decisive | decisively |
| beauty | beautify | beautiful | beautifully |
| courage | encourage | courageous | courageously |
| confusion | confuse | confusing, confused | confusedly |
| difference | differ | different | differently |
| success | succeed | successful | successfully |
| strength | strengthen | strong | strongly |
| possibility | none | possible | possibly |

### Look-alike words with different meanings

Two suffixes on the same root can produce words with different meanings, and the board loves these pairs.

| Pair | Meaning |
|---|---|
| child**ish** / child**like** | childish is a fault (silly, immature); childlike is a compliment (innocent, trusting) |
| econom**ic** / econom**ical** | economic is about the economy; economical means saving money or resources |
| histor**ic** / histor**ical** | historic is important in history; historical is about the past |
| sensible / sensitive | sensible shows good sense; sensitive is easily affected or hurt |
| respect**ful** / respect**able** / respect**ive** | showing respect / worthy of respect / belonging to each one separately (their respective rooms) |
| care**ful** / care**less** | taking care / not taking care |

### Words that only look as if they have a prefix or suffix

Do not cut blindly. *Uncle* is not un + cle, *unit* is not un + it, *disc* is not dis + c, *reason* is not re + ason, *hammer* is not hamm + er, and *mother* is not moth + er. If the left-over piece is not a root or a word, the word has no affix.

### Where this appears in the board paper

The Class 10 English paper does not usually say "name the prefix". The skill appears inside other tasks [Unverified: check the latest sample paper for the exact current format]:

- **Reading vocabulary**: "find a word in the passage which means..." or "the opposite of...". Use roots and affixes to confirm the word.
- **Gap-filling and editing**: the verb, noun or adjective is given in the wrong form and you correct it (*careful for carefull, happily for happyly, unforgettable for unforgetable*).
- **Choosing between look-alikes**: childish or childlike, economic or economical.

right: Right. After "The" and before "of", you need a noun, and the noun from decide is decision.
wrong: Look at the slot. After "The" and before "of" the sentence wants a noun, so choose the noun suffix -ion.
:::

## Sound changes at the joint {lvl=3 id=s-assim label="Beyond the board"}

The prefix in- (not) became im-, il- and ir- because of ease of speech. Linguists call this **assimilation**: the last sound of the prefix becomes like the first sound of the root. Latin prefixes did this wherever they met a root, and the English words we borrowed still carry the pattern.

| Prefix + root | Result | Why |
|---|---|---|
| in + possible | impossible | n becomes m before p |
| in + legal | illegal | n becomes l before l |
| in + regular | irregular | n becomes r before r |
| ad + tract | attract | d becomes t before t |
| con + lect | collect | n becomes l before l |
| sub + port | support | b becomes p before p |
| ob + press | oppress | b becomes p before p |
| ex + fect | effect | x becomes f before f |

This is why the same prefix looks different in *connect*, *collect*, *correct* and *compose* (all from *con-* or *com-* with a shift). It is also why the roots you learn have two or three forms (*ject/jet*, *duc/duct*, *fact/fect*): English took the same Latin root in more than one shape.

:::qc
q: Which prefix + root pair produces "suppress"?
options: ["sub + press", "super + press", "su + press", "sup + ress"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Sub- (under) assimilates before p, becoming sup-, so sub + press = suppress (to press down).
wrong: Suppress means to press something under. The prefix is sub-, and its b becomes p before the p of press.
:::

## The root is not the whole meaning {lvl=3 id=s-drift label="Beyond the board"}

Etymology (the history of a word) is a source of clues, not a rule for today's meaning. Words drift.

- **Decimate** comes from the Latin for tenth. It once described a Roman punishment in which one soldier in ten was killed [Unverified: the usual account]. Today it usually means to destroy a large part of something.
- **Awful** once meant full of awe, in a respectful sense. Now it means very bad.
- **Companion** comes from Latin *com* (with) + *panis* (bread): a person you share bread with [Unverified: standard etymology, not checked here].

The mistake of saying a word "really means" what its root meant is sometimes called the etymological fallacy. Use roots to make a first guess; use the sentence and a dictionary to confirm it.

### Prefixes can be ambiguous

Some prefixes have two jobs. *Un-* with an adjective means *not* (unhappy), but *un-* with a verb means *reverse the action* (untie, undo). So **unlockable** can mean *able to be unlocked* or *not able to be locked*. Likewise *re-* can mean *again* (re-cover a sofa) or have lost that sense (recover from illness). Context settles it, and writers use a hyphen to keep the meaning clear.

:::qc
q: "The earthquake decimated the village." Why is it risky to say decimate "really means" kill one in ten?
options: ["Its meaning has drifted; today it usually means to destroy a large part", "The word has no connection to ten", "It is only used for soldiers", "Latin never used the word"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The root records the origin, but the word now has a wider meaning. Always check the sentence.
wrong: The origin from the Latin for ten is real. The point is that meaning moves with use, so the root alone does not settle what the word means today.
:::

## Inflection, derivation and coining new words {lvl=3 id=s-derive label="Beyond the board"}

Linguists separate two kinds of ending.

| | Inflection | Derivation |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | changes the form for grammar, same word class | builds a new word, often another class |
| Examples | walk, walks, walked, walking; cat, cats; big, bigger | walk to walker; kind to kindness; kind to unkind |
| Count in English | very few (-s, -ed, -ing, -er, -est) | many (hundreds) |
| Dictionary treatment | same entry | own entry |

In this lesson, the prefixes and suffixes we call "word building" are derivation. Verb endings like -ed and -ing were covered under tenses and non-finite verbs.

### New words are coined every day

English makes new words by stacking the old parts, so a new word is usually readable on first sight. Biodiversity (bio + diversity), cyber-security (cyber, from a Greek word for steering [Unverified], + security), telemedicine, microfinance and prepaid are all coined from familiar bricks. Some are *hybrids*, with parts from different languages: *television* joins Greek *tele* with Latin *vision*, and *automobile* joins Greek *auto* with Latin *mobile*. Purists have complained about hybrids, but they are everywhere.

### A glance across Indian languages

English, Sanskrit and Greek belong to the same ancient family of languages, the Indo-European family [Unverified: the standard account in comparative linguistics; check a reliable history of language]. That is why some Sanskrit and Hindi parts echo Greek and Latin ones. The negative prefix *a-/an-* in Sanskrit (*anant*, endless) matches the Greek *a-/an-* in *anarchy* (no ruler) and *anonymous* (no name). Numbers also line up: Sanskrit *dvi* and *tri* with Latin *duo* and *tres* [Unverified: widely cited cognates]. If you speak Hindi or Sanskrit, you already know some of these parts.

:::qc
q: Which pair shows inflection rather than derivation?
options: ["walk, walked", "kind, kindness", "teach, teacher", "happy, unhappy"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Walked is the same verb in a past form; the other pairs build a new word with a new meaning or class.
wrong: Inflection keeps the same word and only changes its grammar form. Kindness, teacher and unhappy are new words.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A built word has a root (core meaning), optionally a prefix at the front and a suffix at the end. {lvl=1}
2. A prefix changes the meaning of a word (not, again, before, against, number); it does not change the spelling of the root. {lvl=1}
3. A suffix usually changes the part of speech: -ness, -ment, -tion make nouns; -ful, -less, -ous, -able make adjectives; -ify, -ise, -en make verbs; -ly makes adverbs. {lvl=1}
4. The prefix in- (not) becomes im- before b, m, p, il- before l and ir- before r. {lvl=1}
5. Spelling changes at the joint: drop the silent e, change y to i after a consonant, double the final consonant of short words. {lvl=1}
6. Learn the common roots: dict (say), scrib/script (write), port (carry), spect (look), duc (lead), bio (life), graph (write), tele (far), phon (sound), meter (measure). {lvl=2}
7. To decode a new word: find the suffix, the prefix and the root, join their meanings, then test the guess in the sentence. {lvl=2}
8. A word family changes its job with its suffix; choose the noun, verb, adjective or adverb that the slot in the sentence needs. {lvl=2}
9. Look-alike pairs differ in meaning: childish and childlike, economic and economical, historic and historical. {lvl=2}
10. A root is a clue, not a definition: words drift (decimate, awful), and prefixes can be ambiguous (unlockable). {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Writing "unpossible" or "inregular": the prefix must match the word (impossible, irregular). {lvl=1}
- Adding the same prefix to every word to get an opposite: some words need dis-, some un-, some in-. Learn them in pairs. {lvl=1}
- Writing "carefull" or "unforgetable": -ful has one l, and forget doubles its t before a vowel suffix (unforgettable). {lvl=1}
- Forgetting y to i: "happyness" should be "happiness". {lvl=1}
- Treating every -ly word as an adverb: friendly, lovely and daily are adjectives. {lvl=1}
- Cutting words that only look built: uncle, unit, reason, hammer and disc have no affix to remove. {lvl=2}
- Choosing a noun where an adjective is needed in a "correct form" question: check the slot (after the, before a noun, after is). {lvl=2}
- Mixing childish with childlike, economic with economical, historic with historical: the endings change the meaning. {lvl=2}
- Taking a root's old meaning as the word's present meaning: use the sentence to confirm. {lvl=3}
- Reading in- as always meaning "not": inflammable and invaluable do not have a negative meaning. {lvl=3}
:::
