## What a phrasal verb is {lvl=1 id=s-what label="Foundation"}

Say "He stood up" and every word means what it says: he was sitting, now he is standing. Say "He gave up" and something strange happens. Nobody has gone up and nobody has given anything. The two words together mean "he stopped trying".

That is the whole idea. A **phrasal verb** is a verb plus a small word, called a **particle**, that together carry a meaning of their own. The particle is usually one of: up, down, in, out, on, off, over, away, back, through, along, by.

Why does English use them at all? Because English has two layers of words. One layer is short, old, everyday words (put off, find out, go on). The other is longer, formal words that came in through French and Latin (postpone, discover, continue). Speakers use the short layer all day and reach for the formal layer in writing.

| Everyday phrasal verb | Formal one-word twin |
|---|---|
| put off | postpone |
| call off | cancel |
| find out | discover |
| carry on | continue |
| put up with | tolerate |
| look into | investigate |
| give in | surrender |
| set up | establish |

:::note title="Phrasal verb or ordinary verb + preposition?" tone=tip
Compare: *She ran down the street* (ran, then down the street: the words keep their own meaning, so this is just a verb with a direction) and *She ran down the idea* (criticised it: a phrasal verb). Test: if you can replace the pair with one new verb that has a meaning you could not guess from the parts, it is a phrasal verb.
:::

## Three jobs a particle can do {lvl=1 id=s-jobs label="Foundation"}

Not every particle works the same way. There are three common patterns.

| Pattern | What the particle does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Literal** | Shows direction; the meaning is just verb + direction | sit down, come in, go out, walk away |
| **Completing or intensifying** | Says the action is finished or done fully | eat up, drink up, fill up, tear up |
| **Idiomatic** | A new meaning you must simply know | give up, break down, call off, put up with |

The idiomatic group is where learners go wrong, so most of this lesson is about it. Note that one phrasal verb can sit in two groups: *She put the book down* (literal) and *The police put down the riot* (idiomatic: ended it by force).

:::qc
q: In which sentence is "give up" used idiomatically?
options: ["He gave up smoking last year.", "He gave the book up to the shelf.", "He gave a gift up the stairs."]
answer: 0
right: Right. Gave up smoking means stopped smoking. In the other two the words keep their ordinary directional meaning, and they are not natural English.
wrong: Idiomatic means the pair has a new meaning. Only "gave up smoking" shows that: it means stopped, not 'handed something up'.
:::

## Taking an object: which phrasal verbs need one? {lvl=2 id=s-object label="Class 10"}

Some phrasal verbs take no object at all (**intransitive**): *The plane took off. The bus broke down. He turned up.* Others need one (**transitive**): *She called off the match.*

Among the transitive ones, there is a rule that decides where the object goes. It is the single most tested point in this lesson.

### Separable: the object can split the phrasal verb

With a **separable** phrasal verb, a noun object may go after the particle or in between.

- *Turn off the light.* = *Turn the light off.*
- *Put on your coat.* = *Put your coat on.*
- *Call off the match.* = *Call the match off.*

If the object is a **pronoun** (it, him, her, them, us, me), it must go in the middle.

- Right: *Turn it off. Call them off. Put it on.*
- Wrong: *Turn off it. Call off them. Put on it.*

Why? A pronoun is short and refers to something already known, so English prefers to put it early and let the new information (the particle) come last. This is a habit of the language, not something you can reason out, so remember the rule.

### Inseparable: the object always follows

With an **inseparable** phrasal verb, nothing can come between the verb and the particle. A pronoun also goes at the end.

- *She looks after her grandmother.* → *She looks after her.* (not *looks her after*)
- *I came across an old photo.* → *I came across it.*
- *The police are looking into the case.* → *They are looking into it.*

Typical inseparable ones: look after, look for, look into, come across, run into, get over, go through, take after.

:::note title="How to tell which is which" tone=tip
There is no rule that guesses it from the meaning. Try the pronoun test: say the sentence with *it*. If *turn it off* sounds right, it is separable. If *look after it* sounds right and *look it after* sounds wrong, it is inseparable. A dictionary labels them too (separable verbs are shown with a gap, like *turn sth off*).
:::

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["Please switch it off.", "Please switch off it.", "Please off switch it."]
answer: 0
right: Right. Switch off is separable, and a pronoun object goes between the verb and the particle.
wrong: With a separable phrasal verb, a pronoun comes between the verb and the particle: switch it off.
:::

:::qc
q: Replace the noun with a pronoun: "The children are looking after the puppy."
options: ["The children are looking after it.", "The children are looking it after.", "The children are after looking it."]
answer: 0
right: Right. Look after is inseparable, so the pronoun follows the whole phrasal verb.
wrong: Look after cannot be split, so the pronoun goes at the end: looking after it.
:::

## Three-word phrasal verbs {lvl=2 id=s-three label="Class 10"}

Some phrasal verbs add a preposition after the particle. They are always inseparable, and you cannot drop the last word.

| Phrasal verb | Meaning | Sentence |
|---|---|---|
| put up with | tolerate | I cannot put up with this heat. |
| look forward to | await with pleasure | She looks forward to meeting you. |
| run out of | have none left | The taxi ran out of petrol. |
| get on with | have a good relationship with; continue | Do you get on with your neighbours? |
| look down on | think you are better than | Never look down on anyone. |
| look up to | respect | Children look up to their teachers. |
| come up with | think of (an idea) | Rohan came up with a clever plan. |
| keep up with | stay level with | It is hard to keep up with her. |
| get away with | avoid punishment | He will not get away with this. |
| catch up with | reach someone who is ahead | I ran to catch up with the others. |

### The -ing trap

In *look forward to*, *get used to*, *object to* and a few others, the word **to** is a preposition, not part of a to-infinitive. So what follows must be a noun or a verb with **-ing**.

- Wrong: *I look forward to meet you.* Right: *I look forward to meeting you.*
- Wrong: *She is used to wake up early.* Right: *She is used to waking up early.*

After *give up*, *carry on* and *keep on*, an -ing form also follows: *He gave up smoking. She carried on reading.*

## Phrasal verbs in tenses, questions and the passive {lvl=2 id=s-grammar label="Class 10"}

Only the verb changes, the particle stays put.

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| present | They call off the match when it rains. |
| past | They called off the match. |
| perfect | They have called off the match. |
| continuous | They are calling off the match. |
| question | Did they call off the match? / Who called it off? |
| passive | The match was called off (by them). |

The passive works for transitive phrasal verbs, and the whole phrase moves with the verb: *The baby was looked after by her aunt. The plan was put off.* In a question or negative with do/does/did, the main verb returns to the base form: *Did he give up?* not *Did he gave up?*

:::qc
q: Choose the correct past tense: "Yesterday he ___ his old friend in the market."
options: ["ran into", "run into", "runs into", "has ran into"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Run into means meet by chance. Its past is ran into; only the verb changes for tense.
wrong: Yesterday needs the past simple. The verb run becomes ran, and the particle into stays.
:::

## Mix-ups the board loves {lvl=2 id=s-mix label="Class 10"}

Many wrong answers come from verbs that share a first word. Learn them in groups.

| Group | Meanings |
|---|---|
| look after / look for / look into / look up | care for / search for / investigate / check in a book |
| break down / break out / break in / break up | stop working / start suddenly / enter by force / end (a meeting, a relationship) |
| turn up / turn down / turn on / turn off | arrive / refuse or lower / start / stop |
| run out / run out of / run into | come to an end / use up the supply / meet by chance |
| put off / put out / put on / put up | postpone / extinguish / wear / put on a wall; give a place to stay |
| bring up / bring out / bring about | raise (a child) or mention (a topic) / publish / cause |

Note: *run out* and *run out of* look alike but differ. *Our water has run out* (no object: the supply ended). *We have run out of water* (we are the people with none left, so of is needed).

:::note title="Which particle? Go to the idea" tone=tip
Before you choose, ask what the sentence needs. Searching: look for. Taking care: look after. Delaying: put off. Stopping a fire: put out. Cancelling: call off. Mixing these up is more common than not knowing the verb at all.
:::

## How the board tests phrasal verbs {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

The CBSE Class 10 paper has no separate section called phrasal verbs, but they turn up in several places. The exact pattern changes from year to year, so treat the list below as a guide [Unverified: check the latest sample paper].

1. **Vocabulary in a reading passage.** A question asks, "Pick the word or phrase from the passage that means..." The answer is often a phrasal verb. Look in the sentence near the clue and match the meaning, not the shape.
2. **Gap-filling and multiple choice.** Choose the right particle: *The fire broke ___ at midnight* (out). Eliminate the options that would change the meaning.
3. **Editing and error correction.** One line may have a wrong particle (*ran into of*), a missing last word (*ran out petrol*), a misplaced pronoun (*turn off it*) or a wrong form after to (*look forward to meet*).
4. **Writing tasks.** In a formal letter or report, choose the formal verb; in a story or dialogue, a phrasal verb makes the voice sound natural.

:::worked title="Four lines, four phrasal verb errors"
Correct each line: (a) *We look forward to see you.* (b) *The teacher told him to switch off it.* (c) *The bus ran out petrol.* (d) *She puts up him for years.*

(a) look forward to **seeing**: to is a preposition, so -ing follows. (b) switch **it off**: separable, pronoun in the middle. (c) ran out **of** petrol: the three-word phrasal verb needs of. (d) She has **put up with** him for years: the missing with, and since *for years* shows duration, the present perfect fits better than the present simple.
:::

:::qc
q: Fill the gap: "A fire broke ___ in the old market last night."
options: ["out", "down", "up", "in"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Break out means start suddenly, used for a fire, a war or a disease.
wrong: Fire, war and disease break out. Break down means stop working, break up means end, and break in means enter by force.
:::

## Formal or casual? Choosing the register {lvl=2 id=s-register label="Class 10"}

In letters, applications and reports, formal readers expect the single-word verbs. In dialogue and stories, phrasal verbs sound natural.

| Casual (talk, story) | Formal (letter, report) |
|---|---|
| We had to put off the trip. | We had to postpone the trip. |
| They called off the match. | They cancelled the match. |
| The prices have gone up. | The prices have risen. |
| We looked into the complaint. | We investigated the complaint. |
| She turned down the offer. | She declined the offer. |
| He gave in after a long fight. | He surrendered after a long fight. |

Do not overdo it. In a formal piece, a few common phrasal verbs (such as *carry out*, *set up*, *point out*) are perfectly acceptable. The rule is: if there is an obviously more formal word and the reader is a stranger, use it.

## One verb, many meanings {lvl=3 id=s-many label="Beyond the board"}

Skilled writers use phrasal verbs that have several meanings, and let the sentence decide. Compare:

| Phrasal verb | Meaning 1 | Meaning 2 | Meaning 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| take off | remove (a coat) | leave the ground | become popular (Her business took off) |
| take in | understand (He could not take it all in) | deceive (I was taken in by the offer) | make smaller (take in a dress) |
| make out | see with difficulty (I could barely make out the sign) | understand (I cannot make him out) | pretend (He made out that he was ill) |
| turn out | prove to be (It turned out to be true) | produce (The factory turns out toys) | attend (Thousands turned out to vote) |
| come off | succeed (The plan came off) | separate (The handle came off) | |
| go off | explode or ring (The bomb / alarm went off) | go bad (The milk has gone off) | leave (He went off in a huff) |

When you meet a phrasal verb you do not know, do three things: read the whole sentence, ask what role the particle seems to play (see the next section), and test your guess by putting a one-word meaning in its place.

## Particles have personalities {lvl=3 id=s-particles label="Beyond the board"}

The small words are not random. They have tendencies that help you guess new phrasal verbs. These are patterns, not laws, and exceptions are common.

| Particle | Common idea | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **up** | completion, increase, approach | eat up, speed up, come up to, wash up |
| **down** | decrease, stopping, writing | slow down, shut down, calm down, write down |
| **out** | outward, to the end, disappearance | hand out, work out, die out, wipe out |
| **off** | separation, start, ending | set off, cut off, break off, call off |
| **on** | continuation, attachment | carry on, hold on, switch on, go on |
| **away** | removal, going far | put away, run away, fade away |
| **over** | review, change, finish | think over, hand over, get over |
| **through** | from start to end | read through, go through, get through |

So *slow down*, *calm down*, *cool down* all share "decreasing", and *eat up*, *drink up*, *use up* all share "finishing completely". A learner who sees *tidy up* for the first time can guess "make completely tidy".

## Nouns, adjectives and the passive made from phrasal verbs {lvl=3 id=s-nouns label="Beyond the board"}

English readily turns a phrasal verb into a noun or adjective. The noun is usually one word (or hyphenated) and stresses the first part.

| Phrasal verb (two words) | Noun / adjective (one word or hyphenated) |
|---|---|
| break down | breakdown |
| break out | outbreak |
| set back | setback |
| take off | takeoff |
| check in | check-in |
| make up | make-up |
| drop out | dropout |
| wash up | washing-up |

*The car broke down* (verb, two words) but *The breakdown held us up for hours* (noun, one word). Mixing up the spelling is a typical writing slip.

## Phrasal verbs in good writing {lvl=3 id=s-style label="Beyond the board"}

A strong writer does not choose one style only; they switch to suit the moment.

- **Narration and dialogue.** *"Hold on," she said, and the bus pulled up.* Phrasal verbs give speed and voice. A story full of *commenced*, *terminated* and *proceeded* would sound stiff.
- **Report or essay.** Mix: use a few phrasal verbs for rhythm, but use formal verbs for the key actions: *The committee carried out a survey and concluded that...*
- **Idiom meets phrasal verb.** Many idioms are built on phrasal verbs (*cut down on*, *bring down the house*, *get cold feet*). If a phrase has a verb and a particle, it may be both an idiom and a phrasal verb. In that case use the meaning from your idioms notes.
- **Beware stacked phrasal verbs.** *He turned up late and carried on as if nothing had broken down* is fine, but a paragraph with nine in a row is hard to follow. Vary the verbs.

:::qc
q: Which sentence uses the noun, not the verb?
options: ["The outbreak of flu closed the school.", "Flu broke out in the school.", "The flu may break out again."]
answer: 0
right: Right. Outbreak (one word) is the noun. Broke out and break out (two words) are the verb.
wrong: A noun is one word (outbreak), a verb is two (break out). Only the first sentence has the one-word noun.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle that together have a meaning of their own, like give up (stop trying) or put off (postpone). {lvl=1}
2. Many phrasal verbs have a formal one-word twin (call off / cancel, put up with / tolerate). Use the casual form in speech and stories, the formal one in letters and reports. {lvl=1}
3. Particles may be literal (sit down), completing (eat up) or idiomatic (give up). {lvl=1}
4. Some phrasal verbs take no object (the plane took off); others take one. {lvl=2}
5. Separable: the object can split the verb (turn the light off), and a pronoun must go in the middle (turn it off). {lvl=2}
6. Inseparable: the object always follows (look after her, come across it). {lvl=2}
7. Three-word phrasal verbs (put up with, look forward to, run out of) cannot lose their last word. {lvl=2}
8. After look forward to, get used to and object to, use a noun or -ing: look forward to meeting. {lvl=2}
9. Only the verb changes for tense and in the passive; the particle stays: gave up, has given up, was called off. {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: one phrasal verb can have several meanings, particles show tendencies (up = completion, down = decrease), and many become nouns (breakdown, outbreak). {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Learning a phrasal verb as two separate words: learn it as one unit with one meaning. {lvl=1}
- Choosing the wrong particle in a group (look after vs look for vs look into). {lvl=2}
- Putting a pronoun after the particle of a separable verb: "turn off it" should be "turn it off". {lvl=2}
- Splitting an inseparable verb: "look it after" should be "look after it". {lvl=2}
- Dropping the last word of a three-word phrasal verb: "run out petrol", "put up him". {lvl=2}
- "Look forward to meet", "get used to wake": use -ing after the preposition to. {lvl=2}
- Confusing run out (supply ends) with run out of (we have none). {lvl=2}
- Using the noun spelling for the verb or the verb spelling for the noun (breakdown / break down). {lvl=3}
- Writing a formal letter full of casual phrasal verbs; prefer the formal one-word twin. {lvl=2}
- Guessing a meaning from the particle alone: read the whole sentence and test your guess. {lvl=3}
:::
