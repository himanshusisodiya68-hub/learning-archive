## His First Flight: the story {lvl=1 id=s-plot1 label="Foundation"}

A young seagull is alone on a ledge on the side of a cliff. His two brothers and his sister flew away the day before. He is bigger than his little sister, but he could not do it. Each time he ran to the brink and tried to flap, the sea looked so far below that he felt sure his wings would not hold him. Hurt and frightened, he went back to his hole. His parents scolded him loudly and even threatened to let him starve, but he stayed where he was.

Now twenty-four hours have passed. He has watched his parents teach the others to skim the waves and dive for fish. He has seen his brother catch a herring. The family stands on a plateau on the other side, laughing at his cowardice. The sun beats on his ledge, and he has not eaten since the night before. He closes one eye, pretends to sleep and hopes they will forget him.

His mother picks up a piece of fish and eats before him. He cries to her for food. She answers with a mocking scream, then picks up the fish, flies across and stops in mid-air, close enough to tempt him but just out of reach. Mad with hunger, he dives. For a moment he falls and is terrified, then his wings spread and the wind holds him. He flaps once and rises. His family gathers round him, shrieking with joy. He flies out over the sea, mistakes the water for a green floor, lands on it, sinks a little, and finds he floats. Tired and hungry, he is fed by his family. The last line says it all: he had made his first flight.

:::qc
q: Why does the mother seagull stop in mid-air with the fish instead of bringing it to the ledge?
options: ["She is tired and has to rest", "She wants him to leave the ledge to get it", "She is afraid of the cliff"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. She has understood that nothing else will make him jump. By keeping the food just out of reach she turns his hunger into the push he needs.
wrong: Think about what a patient parent would do after a whole day of scolding that did not work. She tries something different with the one thing he cannot ignore, his hunger.
:::

## The Black Aeroplane: the story {lvl=1 id=s-plot2 label="Foundation"}

The narrator is a pilot, flying his old Dakota aeroplane from France to England in the early morning hours. The sky is clear, the moon is up, and he is thinking about his holiday and a big English breakfast. He talks to Paris Control by radio, switches to his second and last fuel tank, and turns where he is told to turn.

About a hundred and fifty kilometres out of Paris he sees a wall of huge storm clouds. He cannot fly over them, and he does not have enough fuel to go around. He knows he ought to go back, but he wants to get home. He decides to take the risk and flies straight in. Inside, everything is black. The plane is thrown about, and then the compass spins, the other instruments die, and the radio is silent. He is lost.

Then he sees another aeroplane flying beside him. It has no lights, but he can see the pilot's face. The pilot raises a hand, waves and turns north, so the narrator follows, gratefully, like an obedient child. After half an hour the fuel is nearly gone. Then the stranger begins to go down, and the narrator breaks out of the cloud and sees the double line of lights on a runway. He lands safely. When he looks for the black aeroplane, the sky is empty. At the control centre a woman tells him that no other aeroplane was flying that night, and the radar showed only his. The story ends with the pilot's own questions: who helped him?

## Fear, hunger and the push {lvl=2 id=s-theme1 label="Class 10 board"}

**Why he is afraid.** The text says the sea was "miles down". That is the seagull's exaggeration: fear makes the drop look huger than it is. He has never flown, so he has nothing to compare it with. His fear is not about the sea itself but about the unknown, which is why every young creature, and every human baby taking a first step, feels the same.

**Why the parents are harsh.** Seagulls cannot live without flying, since fish are caught from the air. Their parents cannot feed him on the ledge forever. They scold, threaten and later cheer, because they know he must fly or starve. So the harshness is a form of love, and the mother's last trick is the clearest sign of it.

**The phrase to learn.** The text says the sight of food "maddened him". It means hunger had grown so strong that he lost his careful fear. It is that loss of fear, not a decision, that makes him lunge. This is the point the question asks you to state: what compelled him was hunger, helped by his mother's clever teasing.

**What the story says.** Fear can be bigger than the danger, and sometimes the only cure is to act. Once the seagull has jumped, he is astonished at how natural it feels ("he was no longer afraid"). The story gently suggests that first attempts are harder to start than to do.

:::qc
q: In "The sight of the food maddened him", what does "maddened" suggest?
options: ["He became angry with his mother", "His hunger grew so strong that it overpowered his fear", "He went mad and could not think at all"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The word is used for a wild, uncontrolled feeling. Here it is hunger so strong that it beats his fear and makes him dive at the fish.
wrong: The word is about a feeling too strong to control, and the feeling is hunger. It is not anger at his mother and not real madness.
:::

## Risk, instruments and the mystery {lvl=2 id=s-theme2 label="Class 10 board"}

**The risk.** The pilot has three choices: fly over the storm (impossible), fly round it (not enough fuel), or turn back to Paris (safe, but he would be late). He chooses to fly straight in, the most dangerous option, mainly because he wants to be home for breakfast. The risk is crashing in a storm with no way to see or steer. His reason is small, which is part of the irony: such a risk for such a prize.

**The storm.** The clouds look like black mountains. Inside, everything is dark, the plane jumps and twists, the compass turns round and round, and every instrument and the radio go dead. Lost without instruments, a pilot has nothing to trust except his eyes and his nerve, and his eyes see only black.

**The relief.** "I landed and was not sorry to walk away from the old Dakota." He is not sorry because he has just escaped death: he has been in a storm, without instruments, and with almost no fuel. The plane that looked like a friend is also the machine that nearly became his coffin. He wants to leave it and thank his rescuer.

**The woman's strange look.** She looks at him strangely because there was no other aeroplane that night. The radar showed only his plane, so his story of another pilot could not be true as far as she could tell.

**Who helped him?** The story gives no answer. A model answer can argue any of these, with reasons:
- *A guardian spirit* (a ghost or an angel): it appeared in a storm where nothing else could fly, had no lights, vanished on arrival, and was not on radar.
- *A hallucination*: a tired, frightened mind might have created a guide.
- *A real plane not on radar*: possible but weak, since a lit runway needs a controller who sees traffic.
Whatever you choose, give a reason from the text.

:::qc
q: Which fact from the story best supports the idea that the black aeroplane was not an ordinary plane?
options: ["It flew north", "It had no lights and did not show on the radar", "The pilot waved his hand"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. An ordinary plane in a storm would have lights and appear on radar. Both the lack of lights and the controller's words point to something unusual.
wrong: Look for a detail that an ordinary aeroplane could not explain. Flying north and waving are normal; the missing lights and the empty radar are not.
:::

## Comparing the two stories {lvl=2 id=s-compare label="Class 10 board"}

| Point | His First Flight | The Black Aeroplane |
|---|---|---|
| Who is stuck | A young seagull | A lost pilot |
| The fear | Falling into the sea | Crashing in a storm |
| Where it happens | A ledge above the sea in daylight | The night sky over France, inside a storm |
| What the hero does wrong | Gives in to fear | Takes a foolish risk |
| What gets him through | Hunger and his mother's trick | A mysterious plane that guides him |
| How the help arrives | Planned by the mother | Unplanned and unexplained |
| How it ends | Joy and a clear solution | Safe landing and an open question |
| Narrator | Third person, close to the seagull | First person, the pilot himself |

The big link is that both heroes are rescued by something outside themselves, and both rescues arrive when each can do nothing more. The big difference is that the seagull's story explains itself, while the pilot's does not.

## How the stories are told {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Close third person (story I).** The narrator stays inside the seagull's head: we feel the heat of the ledge, the empty stomach, the terror of the first drop. Short, quick sentences ("He was no longer afraid. He just felt a bit dizzy.") carry the speed of the dive. Fear, hunger, relief and joy follow each other in order.

**Suspense and first person (story II).** Telling the story as "I" lets the reader sit in the cockpit. Early, the narrator is relaxed and even a little boastful (the English breakfast). When the instruments fail the sentences get shorter and the repetition of the compass "round and round" imitates its spinning. Withholding the answer to the last question is what makes the story linger.

**Imagery.** Storm clouds are "black mountains": solid, huge, impossible to pass. The seagull's first flight is described through touch: the wind on his breast feathers, then under his stomach, then on his wings. In each case the image shows the thing instead of naming the feeling.

**Irony and the ending.** The seagull mistakes the sea for a "green flooring" and sinks, only to find that it holds him, so his first landing is a comic surprise. The pilot is comforted by the one thing that, as the controller tells him, does not exist. Both endings turn on a small gap between what the hero thought and what is true.

**Where the writers want you to end.** The first story closes with a short, plain line and a feeling of completion. The second closes on questions. The same chapter therefore shows the reader two different kinds of ending, one tied and one open.

## Language: black and fly {lvl=2 id=s-lang label="Class 10 board"}

**Why one word has many meanings.** People take a word for one thing and use it again for something that looks, feels or works like it. Black first meant the colour of coal. From the colour came *no light* (a black night), then *dirty* (black hands), *gloomy* (a black future), and even *wicked* (a black crime) and *hidden or illegal* (black market). Context tells you which one is meant.

| Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| a black look | an angry or disapproving look |
| black comedy | humour about grim or dark subjects |
| sell in black | sell illegally, usually at a high price and without tax |
| beaten black and blue | covered in bruises |

**Fly** can mean to travel through air, but also to display (fly a flag), to move quickly (time flies), to be successful (fly high), to escape (fly the coop) and to become suddenly angry (fly into a rage). The literal meaning in the chapter belongs to wings; the other meanings borrow the speed, height or sudden movement.

:::qc
q: In "Inside the clouds, everything was suddenly black", what does black mean?
options: ["very dirty", "with no light", "very wicked"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Inside a storm cloud at night there is no light at all, so black means dark, without light.
wrong: Think about where the pilot is. Nothing is dirty or evil in the sentence; the clouds simply cut off all light.
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not say the seagull flew because his parents forced him. They shouted for a day without result; it was hunger and the mother's trick that worked. {lvl=1}
- Do not say the black aeroplane was definitely a ghost, or definitely imagined. The story leaves it open; argue a view with reasons. {lvl=2}
- Do not call the pilot stupid or the mother cruel without evidence; say the pilot took a foolish risk and the mother used a clever method. {lvl=2}
- 'Maddened' does not mean angry here; it means a feeling (hunger) too strong to resist. {lvl=2}
- Do not retell the whole plot for a character or theme question. Give a claim, a detail and a comment. {lvl=2}
- Do not copy long lines from the story. A short phrase with your own comment scores better. {lvl=3}
:::
