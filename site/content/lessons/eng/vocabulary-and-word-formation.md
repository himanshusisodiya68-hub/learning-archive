## One root, a family of forms {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Why does English keep four versions of the same idea? Because a sentence needs different kinds of word to hold together. It needs a **thing** (noun), an **action** (verb), a word that **describes a thing** (adjective) and a word that **describes an action** (adverb). One root can supply all four.

| Root idea | Noun | Verb | Adjective | Adverb |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| decide | decision | decide | decisive | decisively |
| create | creation, creator, creativity | create | creative | creatively |
| succeed | success | succeed | successful | successfully |
| beauty | beauty | beautify | beautiful | beautifully |
| strong | strength | strengthen | strong | strongly |
| danger | danger | endanger | dangerous | dangerously |
| differ | difference | differ | different | differently |
| simple | simplicity | simplify | simple | simply |

Read across a row and the **word family** appears. A word family is the set of words that share one root and differ in job. Knowing the family means one word learned is four words owned.

Not every family has all four members. Some roots have only a noun and an adjective (*heaven, heavenly*), and some forms are rare. The dictionary lists what really exists; do not invent forms such as "successfuly" or "differention".

:::qc
q: Which word is the adjective of the root "succeed"?
options: ["successful", "success", "successfully", "succession"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Successful describes a noun (a successful plan). Success is the noun, successfully the adverb.
wrong: An adjective describes a thing. "A ___ plan" needs successful. Success is the noun and successfully the adverb.
:::

## Read the slot, then choose {lvl=1 id=s-slot label="Foundation"}

Picture a bus seat. It does not matter how nice the passenger is; the seat is either for a person or for a bag. A gap in a sentence is a seat, and the words around it say who may sit there.

| Clue near the gap | The gap needs | Example (answer in bold) |
|---|---|---|
| the, a, an, my, his, some, many, or an adjective before the gap | a noun | Her honest **answer** surprised us. |
| of, in, for, by, with before the gap | a noun (or an -ing form) | We were proud of her **achievement**. |
| to, will, can, must, does, did before the gap | a verb (base form) | The coach will **strengthen** the team. |
| is, was, seems, looks, feels, becomes before the gap | an adjective | The ground felt **slippery**. |
| the gap sits right before a noun | an adjective | A **dangerous** road ran through the forest. |
| the gap describes a verb, an adjective or a whole sentence | an adverb (often -ly) | She spoke **confidently**. |

Two more clues. A plural word such as *many* or *several* before the gap points to a plural noun (*many successes*). And a gap after a linking verb (*is, seems, feels*) wants an adjective, even if the word sounds like it describes an action: *She felt happy*, not *happily*.

:::note title="The five-second test" tone=tip
Cover the bracket word. Read the sentence with a dummy word in the gap (*thing, do, nice, nicely*) and see which dummy sounds right. "The captain's quick *thing*" works, so a noun is needed. Then look at the bracket word and build the noun.
:::

:::qc
q: She answered all the questions ___. (confident)
options: ["confident", "confidence", "confidently", "confidences"]
answer: 2
right: Right. The gap describes how she answered, an action, so an adverb is needed: confidently.
wrong: Ask what the gap describes. It describes the verb "answered", so it must be an adverb. The adverb of confident is confidently.
:::

## Endings that give the job away {lvl=1 id=s-endings label="Foundation"}

You can often tell a word's job from its tail. This is the working summary of the suffixes you met in Roots, prefixes and suffixes, rearranged by job.

| Job | Common endings | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | -tion, -sion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -ance, -ence, -ship, -hood, -ism, -er, -or | action, decision, movement, kindness, activity, importance, friendship |
| Verb | -ise / -ize, -ify, -en, -ate | organise, simplify, widen, activate |
| Adjective | -ful, -less, -ous, -ive, -able, -ible, -al, -ic, -ish, -y, -ent, -ant | helpful, careless, famous, active, comfortable, national, heroic, childish, windy, different |
| Adverb | -ly | quickly, carefully, happily |

These are strong hints, not laws. *Friendly* ends in -ly but is an adjective; *lovely*, *lonely* and *elderly* are too. And some adverbs do not end in -ly (*fast, hard, well, late*): *She worked hard* is correct, while *hardly* means "almost not". Check the job by the slot, and use the ending as a second opinion.

:::qc
q: Which word is NOT an adverb?
options: ["suddenly", "friendly", "gently", "quickly"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Friendly is an adjective (a friendly dog), even though it ends in -ly.
wrong: Suddenly, gently and quickly all describe actions. Friendly describes a thing (a friendly smile), so it is an adjective.
:::

## Start a word bank today {lvl=1 id=s-bank-start label="Foundation"}

You will forget most new words within a week unless you give them somewhere to live. A **word bank** is that place: a notebook page or a phone note where each word gets an entry in your own words.

Start small. Three new words a day is enough, and each entry should hold:

| Word | Word class | Family | Meaning in my words | Partner words | A sentence I wrote |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| reluctant | adjective | reluctance (n), reluctantly (adv) | not wanting to do something | reluctant to agree | Mira was reluctant to sing in front of the whole school. |

Two rules matter more than the format. First, write the **sentence yourself**; copying the dictionary's example teaches you the dictionary's sentence, not the word. Second, collect words from things you read for fun or study (a story, a news article, a prose chapter), not from word lists, because a word met in a sentence arrives with its partners and mood attached.

The advanced version of this routine, with review timing, is in the Beyond the board section.

## Gap filling with a root in brackets {lvl=2 id=s-forms label="Class 10 board"}

The board paper tests this skill in gap-filling and editing tasks. Where a root is given in brackets, the answer is nearly always a different member of its family. [Unverified] The exact marks, number of blanks and layout of the current paper change from year to year, so check the latest sample paper from the board.

Use four steps.

1. **Slot.** Read the words just before and after the gap.
2. **Class.** Decide noun, verb, adjective or adverb.
3. **Family member.** Build that form, then check meaning: does the sentence need a negative (*unfair*), a plural (*participants*) or a past form (*impressed*)?
4. **Spelling.** Apply the changes in the table below.

:::worked title="Worked example: five gaps in one passage"
Fill each gap with the correct form of the word in brackets.

Last summer our school organised a (1) ___ (compete) in painting. The (2) ___ (participate) were asked to show their (3) ___ (imagine) by drawing their town as it might look in fifty years. The judges were (4) ___ (impress) by the colourful, (5) ___ (create) entries.

| Gap | Clue | Class | Answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "a" before the gap, nothing singular to describe it | noun, singular | competition |
| 2 | "The" before, "were asked" after, so more than one person | noun, plural, a person | participants |
| 3 | "their" before | noun | imagination |
| 4 | "were" before, and the judges felt it | adjective or participle of feeling (-ed) | impressed |
| 5 | comes right before a noun, "entries" | adjective | creative |
:::

Spelling changes are where marks leak away, even when the class is right.

| Change | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Drop the final -e | before a suffix that starts with a vowel | create, creation, creative; admire, admirable (keeps e after c or g: notice, noticeable; courage, courageous) |
| Keep the final -e | before a suffix that starts with a consonant | hope, hopeful; manage, management; care, careless |
| Exceptions to the e-rule | learn them | argue, argument; true, truly; whole, wholly; nine, ninth |
| y becomes i | consonant + y, before most suffixes | happy, happiness, happily; beauty, beautiful; carry, carried |
| y stays | before -ing, and in some words | carrying; shy, shyness; dry, dryness |
| Double the last letter | one short vowel + one consonant, stressed last syllable | begin, beginner; run, runner; admit, admitted (but open, opener: stress is on the first syllable) |
| Surprising roots | the root itself bends | decide, decision; divide, division; admit, admission; long, length; strong, strength; wide, width; deep, depth; high, height; please, pleasure; die, death; grow, growth; know, knowledge; choose, choice |
| Noun and verb spelt apart | British spelling | advice (noun), advise (verb); practice (noun), practise (verb); licence (noun), license (verb) |
| Adverb from -le, -ic, -ue | special endings | possible, possibly; gentle, gently; public, publicly (not publically); true, truly |

:::qc
q: The ___ of the new bridge took three years. (construct)
options: ["construct", "construction", "constructive", "constructively"]
answer: 1
right: Right. "The ___ of" is a noun slot: construction.
wrong: "The" before and "of" after mean a noun is needed. The noun of construct is construction.
:::

## Look-alike forms with different meanings {lvl=2 id=s-lookalikes label="Class 10 board"}

Sometimes the same root offers two adjectives or two nouns, and both look right. They are not the same word, so the meaning has to choose.

| Pair | First means | Second means |
|---|---|---|
| economic / economical | to do with the economy: economic growth | saving money or resources: an economical car |
| historic / historical | important enough to be remembered: a historic victory | belonging to the past, or to history as a subject: a historical novel |
| sensible / sensitive | showing good sense: a sensible plan | easily affected or hurt: sensitive skin, a sensitive remark |
| childish / childlike | silly in a way that is not suitable for an adult (negative) | innocent and fresh like a child (positive) |
| continual / continuous | happening again and again, with breaks: continual interruptions | without any break: continuous rain |
| respectful / respectable / respective | showing respect | decent in the eyes of society | belonging to each one separately: they went to their respective homes |
| imaginary / imaginative | not real: an imaginary friend | full of ideas: an imaginative writer |
| successful / successive | achieving a goal | coming one after another: three successive wins |
| considerate / considerable | thoughtful about others | fairly large: a considerable sum |
| industrial / industrious | of industry: an industrial town | hard-working: an industrious student |

A second family of pairs is -ing and -ed. The **-ing** form describes the thing that causes the feeling; the **-ed** form describes the person who has it.

| Thing (-ing) | Person (-ed) |
|---|---|
| The film was **interesting**. | We were **interested** in the film. |
| The lecture was **boring**. | The students were **bored**. |
| The result was **surprising**. | I was **surprised**. |
| The climb was **tiring**. | The hikers were **tired**. |

:::qc
q: The football team won three ___ matches.
options: ["successful", "successive", "success", "succeeded"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Successive means one after another. Successful would only say that the matches were won.
wrong: "Three ___ matches" means three in a row, one after another. That is successive, not successful.
:::

## Negatives: which prefix? {lvl=2 id=s-negatives label="Class 10 board"}

To say the opposite you add a prefix, but several prefixes do the job, and the choice looks random. Part of it is spelling and part is habit.

| Prefix | When | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| un- | the commonest for adjectives and participles; also reverses verbs | unhappy, unkind, unfair, unable, untie |
| in- | many words of Latin origin | incomplete, inactive, incorrect, invisible |
| im- | in- before b, m, p | impossible, immature, impatient, imbalance |
| il- | in- before l | illegal, illogical, illiterate |
| ir- | in- before r | irregular, irrelevant, irresponsible |
| dis- | opposite of, or removal | dislike, disagree, disappear, dishonest, disobey |
| mis- | wrongly, badly | misunderstand, misuse, misspell, mislead |
| non- | simply not | nonsense, nonstop, non-fiction |

The spelling part (im-, il-, ir-) has a rule: the n of in- changes to match the next letter. The un- or in- part has no rule. We say *impossible* but *unhappy*; nobody says *unpossible*. Learn the pairs as you meet them, and put them in your word bank.

Two traps:

- **Inflammable** means "can catch fire", the same as *flammable*. The in- here is an old intensifier, not a negative. Safety labels use *flammable* to avoid the confusion.
- **Invaluable** means "extremely valuable", not "worthless". The word for worthless is *valueless*.

:::qc
q: Which word is spelt correctly?
options: ["inregular", "unregular", "irregular", "imregular"]
answer: 2
right: Right. In- becomes ir- before r: irregular.
wrong: The in- prefix changes its n to match the next letter. Before r it becomes ir-.
:::

## Editing: the wrong form that looks right {lvl=2 id=s-edit label="Class 10 board"}

In an editing task a passage has errors you must find and correct. Word-form errors are the sneakiest, because the wrong word is a real word that simply sits in the wrong slot. Scan with the slot test: for every noun slot check it holds a noun, and so on.

:::worked title="Worked example: four word-form errors"
The new metro line has made travel to work far more convenience for thousands of people. Passengers no longer depend on crowded buses. The trains run frequent, and the service is very reliably. Officials say the line was built in record time because the engineers worked careful.

| Incorrect | Correct | Why |
|---|---|---|
| convenience | convenient | "more ___ " compares a quality, so an adjective is needed |
| frequent | frequently | describes how the trains run, an action |
| reliably | reliable | "is very ___" after a linking verb: adjective |
| careful | carefully | describes how the engineers worked |
:::

How to write the answer: give the incorrect word and its correction, exactly as in the table (or as the question's layout asks). Mark nothing that is already right; a wrong "correction" can cost marks. [Unverified] The marking scheme for editing differs between sample papers, so follow the instructions printed on the question.

Common patterns in this task: adjective for adverb (*worked careful*), adverb for adjective (*seems quietly*), noun for adjective (*a success plan*), verb form for noun (*a long decide*), and the -ing/-ed mix (*I was boring*, which says that you are dull).

:::qc
q: Find the error: 'Rina looked angrily when she saw the broken window.'
options: ["angrily should be angry", "looked should be looks", "broken should be break", "saw should be seen"]
answer: 0
right: Right. After the linking verb "looked" (meaning appeared), an adjective is needed: angry.
wrong: Here "looked" means "appeared", a linking verb, and a linking verb takes an adjective. Change angrily to angry.
:::

## Vague words, vivid words {lvl=2 id=s-vivid label="Class 10 board"}

Which sentence lets you see something: "The food was good" or "The curry was rich and fragrant, and the rotis were still warm"? The first uses a word that fits everything and so says nothing. A **vague** word could describe a thousand different situations. A **vivid** word has been chosen for this one.

Writing tasks on the paper reward exact words: a letter, a paragraph, a short story. Three quick fixes cover most of it.

1. **Replace the weak verb.** Get, make, do, put, go and say are the most overworked words in English. Ask what exactly happened.
2. **Replace very + adjective** with one stronger adjective.
3. **Replace the vague noun** (thing, stuff, problem) with the exact one.

| Vague | Vivid (depends on the situation) |
|---|---|
| She got a prize. | She won / earned / received a prize. |
| He walked into the room. | He strode / shuffled / tiptoed into the room. |
| He said it. | He whispered / announced / muttered / replied. |
| She was very tired. | She was exhausted. |
| It was a very big crowd. | It was a vast crowd, thousands strong. |
| There are many problems. | There are three problems: no water, no light, no road. |
| It was a nice day. | It was a crisp, sunny morning. |

Notice the last two. Vivid is often not a fancier word but a more **specific** one: a number, a name, a detail.

:::note title="Vivid does not mean fancy" tone=warn
Do not swap *use* for *utilise* just to sound grown-up. A rare word chosen from a thesaurus often misses by a shade, and in an exam an incorrectly used grand word loses more marks than a plain correct one earns. Use the exact word you can use correctly.
:::

**Register** is the other half of right word: how formal the situation is. A letter to the principal asks for formal words; a message to a friend does not.

| Informal | Formal |
|---|---|
| ask for | request |
| buy | purchase |
| get | receive / obtain |
| kids | children |
| need | require |
| show | demonstrate |
| help | assist |

:::qc
q: Which version is the more vivid and specific?
options: ["The room was very messy.", "The room was in a state.", "Books, clothes and half-eaten toast lay across the floor of the room.", "The room was not tidy at all."]
answer: 2
right: Right. It shows the mess through exact nouns instead of calling the room messy.
wrong: The first, second and fourth only label the room. The third lets you see what is on the floor, and that detail is what makes writing vivid.
:::

## The right partner as well {lvl=2 id=s-partners label="Class 10 board"}

Even the right word can be marked wrong if it sits beside the wrong partner. English speakers say *heavy rain* and *strong tea*, never the reverse. These natural pairs are **collocations**, taught in full in the Collocations lesson. For writing, remember three habits.

| Wrong | Right | Habit |
|---|---|---|
| do a mistake | make a mistake | make for results and creations, do for tasks |
| make homework | do homework | do the homework, the dishes, the work |
| say a lie | tell a lie | tell a lie, tell a story, say a word |
| strong rain | heavy rain | rain is heavy, tea is strong |
| take a photo of | take a photograph | learn the verb that usually goes with the noun |

When you add a word to your word bank, write one partner next to it. A word with its partner is far easier to use than a word on its own.

:::qc
q: She ___ a serious mistake in the calculation.
options: ["did", "made", "took", "said"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Make a mistake is the fixed partnership.
wrong: English says make a mistake, not do a mistake. Learn it as one unit.
:::

## Same spelling, different job {lvl=3 id=s-shift label="Beyond the board"}

What kind of word is *water*? In "Water the plants", it is a verb; in "Drink some water", a noun. English lets many words change class without any change of spelling, which is called **conversion**. The job decides the class, not the word.

- Noun to verb: to email, to text, to head a committee, to chair a meeting, to google.
- Verb to noun: a walk, a swim, a must-see, a go.
- Adjective to noun: the poor, the young, the rich.

Often a change of **stress** (the loudest syllable) separates a noun from its verb twin, with the stress on the first syllable for the noun and the second for the verb. This is a strong tendency, not a law.

| Word | Noun (stress first) | Verb (stress second) |
|---|---|---|
| record | REcord: a school record | reCORD: to record a song |
| present | PREsent: a birthday present | preSENT: to present a prize |
| object | OBject: an unknown object | obJECT: to object to a plan |
| increase | INcrease: an increase in price | inCREASE: to increase speed |
| import | IMport: imports and exports | imPORT: to import goods |
| desert | DEsert: a dry land | deSERT: to leave someone |

Why does this matter for writing? Because a dictionary entry lists each class separately, and a new-looking use of an old word (*to friend someone*, *a big ask*) is often just conversion at work. When you meet a strange form, ask what job the word is doing before you reach for a meaning.

:::qc
q: In which sentence does "present" have the stress on its second syllable?
options: ["She gave me a present.", "The principal will present the prizes.", "Her present address is in Pune.", "The present is a gift."]
answer: 1
right: Right. In the verb (to present) the stress falls on the second syllable: preSENT.
wrong: When "present" is a noun or an adjective (a present, the present address) the stress is on the first syllable. Only the verb is stressed on the second.
:::

## Shades of feeling: connotation {lvl=3 id=s-shade label="Beyond the board"}

Two words can mean nearly the same and still make the reader feel very different things. The dictionary meaning is the **denotation**; the feeling around it is the **connotation**. Choose carelessly and you may insult someone you meant to praise.

| What it describes | Pleasant | Neutral | Unpleasant |
|---|---|---|---|
| thin | slim, slender | thin | skinny, scrawny |
| careful with money | thrifty, economical | careful | stingy, miserly |
| wanting to know | curious, inquisitive | interested | nosy |
| sure of oneself | confident, self-assured | sure | arrogant, cocky |
| not changing one's mind | determined, resolute | firm | stubborn, obstinate |
| inexpensive | affordable, reasonable | inexpensive | cheap |

Also mind pairs that learners confuse: *disinterested* traditionally means "fair, with nothing to gain" while *uninterested* means "not interested". [Unverified] Many modern speakers use disinterested for both, so in an exam use it only in the first sense.

Writers use connotation deliberately. A news report may call a protest "a gathering", "a demonstration" or "a mob", and each choice nudges you. Reading closely for this is useful in unseen passages, where you are sometimes asked about the writer's attitude.

:::qc
q: You want to praise a classmate who never gives up an argument she believes in. Which word is the safest?
options: ["stubborn", "obstinate", "determined", "pig-headed"]
answer: 2
right: Right. Determined is positive. The other three suggest a refusal to listen.
wrong: Stubborn, obstinate and pig-headed carry a negative feeling. Determined praises the same trait.
:::

## Where new words come from {lvl=3 id=s-newwords label="Beyond the board"}

New words do not appear from nowhere. Almost all of them are made with a few tools, and spotting the tool often tells you the meaning.

| Process | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Compounding | two words joined | notebook, rainfall, blackboard |
| Blending | pieces of two words merged | brunch (breakfast + lunch), smog (smoke + fog), motel (motor + hotel) |
| Clipping | a long word cut short | exam (examination), flu (influenza), phone (telephone) |
| Acronym | initials read as a word | laser, radar, ISRO |
| Initialism | initials read letter by letter | UPI, ATM, SMS |
| Back-formation | a shorter word made by removing what looks like an ending | edit (from editor), burgle (from burglar) |
| Borrowing | taken from another language | jungle, bungalow, shampoo, pyjamas, loot (from Indian languages); algebra (Arabic) |

English has taken in many words from Indian languages, such as *jungle, bungalow, shampoo, pyjamas, loot, cot* and *verandah*. [Unverified] The exact language each word came from, and the route it took into English, is disputed for some of them, so check a historical dictionary before using one in a debate.

For you as a reader this is a decoding trick. Meeting an unfamiliar word like *edit* or *brunch*, break it up. For you as a writer, it explains why such coinages feel natural. For the board, it is background: it is not usually asked directly. It is a good conversation starter though.

:::qc
q: "Smog" was made from "smoke" and "fog". What is this process called?
options: ["clipping", "compounding", "blending", "back-formation"]
answer: 2
right: Right. Blending merges parts of two words into a new one.
wrong: Smog uses only parts of smoke and fog. When parts of words (not whole words) are merged, it is a blend.
:::

## Growing a word bank that lasts {lvl=3 id=s-bank label="Beyond the board"}

Collecting words is easy; keeping them is the skill. Memory research has long shown two findings that matter here [Unverified in detail; widely reported]: you remember more when you **test yourself** than when you re-read, and more when you **space** your reviews than when you cram. A word-bank routine can use both.

1. **Capture in context.** Copy the sentence where you met the word, not just the word.
2. **Build the family.** Note the noun, verb, adjective and adverb that really exist, from a learner's dictionary. Many entries list the derived forms at the end.
3. **Write your own sentence** within 24 hours and use the word once in speech or writing.
4. **Review by recall.** Cover the meaning, say it, then check. Review on the next day, after about a week and after about a month. Drop words you now use freely.
5. **Sort now and then** by family, by theme (courage, weather, money) or by the book chapter they came from.

A word is truly yours only when it climbs this ladder:

| Level | What you can do | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Recognise it when reading | You are not stopped by it |
| 2 | Recall its meaning | You can explain it without looking |
| 3 | Use it in a sentence | Your sentence sounds natural |
| 4 | Use the right form in any slot | You can fill any gap with its family without help |

Aim for level 4 on a small number of words rather than level 1 on hundreds. Words from the prose chapters you study are a good source because you already know the context.

:::qc
q: Which word-bank habit helps memory the most?
options: ["Copying a long list of new words from a thesaurus", "Writing your own sentence with the word and reviewing it after a day, a week and a month", "Reading the dictionary meaning once", "Highlighting the word in the text"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Your own sentence, plus spaced recall, makes you use the word, which is what builds memory.
wrong: Lists, one reading and highlighting are all passive. Producing your own sentence and revisiting it at intervals is active and spaced.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. One root has a word family: a noun, a verb, an adjective and an adverb (decision, decide, decisive, decisively). {lvl=1}
2. In any gap, read the slot first. Words before and after it tell you the word class needed. {lvl=1}
3. Endings hint at the job (-tion noun, -ise verb, -ful adjective, -ly adverb), but friendly is an adjective and fast an adverb. Check the slot. {lvl=1}
4. A word bank holds meaning in your words, family, a partner and your own sentence; three words a day is enough. {lvl=1}
5. Four steps for a word-form gap: slot, class, family member (negative, plural, tense?), spelling. {lvl=2}
6. Spelling changes: drop or keep the e, y becomes i, and some roots bend (decide, decision; strong, strength; advice, advise). {lvl=2}
7. Look-alikes differ in meaning: economic or economical, historic or historical, successful or successive, interested or interesting. {lvl=2}
8. Negative prefixes: im- before b, m, p; il- before l; ir- before r; un-, in- and dis- must be learned in pairs. {lvl=2}
9. In editing, a real word in the wrong slot is the usual error. Scan each slot and give the incorrect word and its correction. {lvl=2}
10. Vivid means exact: strong verb, specific noun, one strong adjective instead of very + adjective. Fancy words are no substitute. {lvl=2}
11. Words keep partners (make a mistake, heavy rain), and formal writing needs formal words (request, not ask for). {lvl=2}
12. Conversion lets one spelling serve two classes, and stress can mark the difference (REcord and reCORD). {lvl=3}
13. Connotation: choose among near-synonyms for feeling (determined, stubborn). {lvl=3}
14. Word bank method: capture in context, build the family, write a sentence, review by recall on day 1, week 1, month 1. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Copying the bracket word unchanged: the answer is nearly always another member of the family. {lvl=1}
- Inventing forms that do not exist (successfuly, differention): check a dictionary. {lvl=1}
- Trusting -ly blindly: friendly, lovely and lonely are adjectives; fast, hard and late can be adverbs. {lvl=1}
- Copying a dictionary example into the word bank instead of writing your own sentence. {lvl=1}
- Adjective for adverb, or the reverse (worked careful, seems quietly). After a linking verb use an adjective. {lvl=2}
- Forgetting meaning after finding the class: the answer may need un-, a plural or a past form. {lvl=2}
- Spelling slips: argument (no e), truly, publicly, advice/advise, practice/practise, licence/license. {lvl=2}
- Confusing the pairs: economic/economical, historic/historical, successful/successive, continual/continuous. {lvl=2}
- Mixing -ing and -ed: "I was boring" says that you are boring; "I was bored" says that you feel bored. {lvl=2}
- Treating inflammable as "cannot burn": it means can burn, like flammable. {lvl=2}
- In editing, changing a word that was already correct. {lvl=2}
- Choosing rare words from a thesaurus that you cannot use correctly: prefer the exact word you know. {lvl=2}
- Using a word of one register in the wrong place (kids in a letter to the principal). {lvl=3}
- Ignoring connotation: stubborn and determined are not interchangeable. {lvl=3}
:::
