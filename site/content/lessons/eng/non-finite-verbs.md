## Why verbs need a second life {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

A sentence needs a verb that tells what the subject does and when: *Meera writes. Meera wrote. Meera will write.* But often we want to talk **about** an action without telling a whole story with its own subject and time. We want to say that writing is difficult, or that a girl is holding a pen for writing, or that someone sat down in order to write. In each case the action is not the main event. It is only a piece used to build something else.

English solves this by letting a verb change its form and take on the job of another word class.

- As a **noun**: *Writing is difficult.* *To write well takes practice.*
- As an **adjective**: *a sleeping child*, *a broken cup*.
- As an **adverb**: *She sat down to write.*

These forms keep the meaning of the verb but lose its tense and its link to a subject. They are **non-finite verbs**. The word *finite* means limited: a finite verb is limited by tense, number and person. A non-finite verb is not limited in these ways, so it can be slotted into a sentence wherever a noun, adjective or adverb is needed. This saves words. Compare *She sat down so that she could write* with the shorter *She sat down to write.*

## Finite and non-finite verbs {lvl=1 id=s-finite label="Foundation"}

A **finite verb** does three things: it agrees with its subject, it shows tense, and it can stand as the main verb of a clause.

| | Finite | Non-finite |
|---|---|---|
| Has its own subject | Yes: *Rahul plays.* | No: *to play*, *playing*, *played* |
| Shows tense | Yes: *plays, played, will play* | No: *to play* is the same for any time |
| Agrees with the subject | Yes: *he plays, they play* | No |
| Can be the main verb alone | Yes | No (it needs a finite verb next to it) |

Test it: in *Rahul wants to play cricket*, the verb **wants** is finite (it takes -s for Rahul and tells the time), while **to play** is non-finite (it would stay *to play* if Rahul were replaced by *they* or if the sentence were in the past: *Rahul wanted to play cricket*).

There are three non-finite forms:

1. the **infinitive**: *to play*, or the bare *play*;
2. the **gerund**: *playing* used as a noun;
3. the **participle**: *playing* or *played*, used as an adjective.

:::qc
q: In "The injured player wants to rest", which word is a finite verb?
options: ["wants", "injured", "to rest", "player"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Wants agrees with the subject player and shows the present tense. Injured is a participle used as an adjective and to rest is an infinitive.
wrong: A finite verb has a subject, shows tense and agrees with the subject. Only wants does all this. Injured is a participle and to rest is an infinitive.
:::

## The infinitive {lvl=1 id=s-inf label="Foundation"}

The infinitive is the base form of the verb, usually with **to**: *to read, to travel, to be*. It names the action in its plainest form, with no subject, no time and no number. It can work in three ways.

**As a noun** (subject or object): *To err is human.* *She wants to learn the violin.* *His aim is to win.*

**As an adjective** (it follows the noun and says what the noun is for): *I need a book to read.* *This is the best way to learn.* *She has no time to waste.*

**As an adverb** (usually showing purpose or reason): *He went to the market to buy vegetables.* *We were happy to see you.*

### Verbs followed by to + base verb

Many common verbs are followed by the full infinitive: *want, hope, decide, plan, promise, refuse, agree, learn, manage, fail, offer, afford, need, expect, seem, appear*.

- *They decided to leave early.*
- *He refused to apologise.*
- *We cannot afford to miss the bus.*

### The bare infinitive

Sometimes the verb appears **without to**. This is called the bare infinitive. Use it:

- After **modal verbs**: *can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must*. *She can swim.* *You must listen.*
- After **let**, **make** and (optionally) **help**: *Let me go.* *The teacher made him repeat the answer.* *Help me (to) carry this.*
- After **had better**, **would rather**, **need not**, **dare not**: *You had better hurry.* *I would rather stay.*
- After **see, hear, watch, notice, feel** with an object, when the whole action was seen from start to finish: *I saw him cross the road.* *We heard her sing.*

When the verb with **make** is made passive, the *to* comes back: *The teacher made him repeat it* but *He was made to repeat it.*

### Too ... to and enough to

These patterns use the infinitive to show a result.

- *The tea is too hot to drink.* (so hot that it cannot be drunk)
- *She is old enough to vote.*
- *He ran fast enough to catch the bus.*

### Forms of the infinitive

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| Simple | *to write* |
| Continuous | *to be writing*: *She seems to be writing a letter.* |
| Perfect | *to have written*: *He seems to have written the report.* (earlier than now) |
| Passive | *to be written*: *The letter needs to be written today.* |

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["She made me to laugh.", "She made me laugh.", "She made me laughing."]
answer: 1
right: Right. After make with an object we use the bare infinitive, so made me laugh is correct.
wrong: Make takes the bare infinitive (the base verb without to) in the active voice. "She made me laugh" is the form.
:::

## The gerund {lvl=1 id=s-ger label="Foundation"}

The gerund is the **-ing form of a verb used as a noun**. It names an action as a thing: *Swimming is good exercise.* *I love reading.* *Her hobby is painting.* Because it is a noun, it can be a subject, an object, a complement, or follow a preposition.

- Subject: *Cooking relaxes my mother.*
- Object of a verb: *They enjoy singing.*
- Object of a preposition: *He is fond of gardening.*
- Complement: *My favourite pastime is knitting.*

### After prepositions

A preposition must be followed by a noun, pronoun or gerund, never by an infinitive. So:

- *She is interested in learning Spanish.* (not *interested to learn*)
- *He left without saying a word.*
- *Thank you for helping me.*
- *Instead of complaining, do something.*

A few fixed phrases end in the preposition **to**, so the verb after them is a gerund: *look forward to, be used to, get used to, object to, be committed to, in addition to*. *I look forward to seeing you. He is used to waking early.* Test: if you can put a noun after it (*look forward to the holidays*), then to is a preposition and the gerund is correct.

### Verbs followed by the gerund

| Group | Verbs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Liking and disliking | enjoy, mind, dislike, can't stand | *I don't mind waiting.* |
| Stopping and avoiding | finish, stop, quit, avoid, give up, postpone, delay | *She finished painting the wall.* |
| Saying and thinking | suggest, admit, deny, consider, imagine, recommend | *He denied taking the pen.* |
| Continuing | keep, practise, go on | *Keep trying.* |

Also useful: *It is no use crying. It is worth visiting. There is no point arguing. I can't help laughing.*

:::note title="A memory aid" tone=tip
Verbs that look **forward** to a goal or plan (*want, hope, decide, plan, promise*) take **to + verb**: the action is still ahead. Verbs about an action that is **happening or already done** (*enjoy, finish, avoid, mind, deny, practise*) take **-ing**. It does not cover every verb, but it gives you a good first guess.
:::

:::qc
q: "He avoided ___ to the teacher."
options: ["to talk", "talk", "talking", "to talking"]
answer: 2
right: Right. Avoid is always followed by a gerund, so avoided talking.
wrong: Avoid takes the -ing form. The sentence should read "He avoided talking to the teacher."
:::

## The participle {lvl=1 id=s-part label="Foundation"}

A participle is a verb form that works as an **adjective**. It describes a noun or pronoun. There are three kinds.

| Kind | Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present participle | V-ing | active, happening now or during another action | *a smiling girl*, *the barking dog* |
| Past participle | V3 (or -ed) | finished, or passive in meaning | *a broken window*, *the fallen leaves* |
| Perfect participle | having + V3 | earlier than the main action | *Having finished, she left.* |

### Participles as adjectives

*The crying baby* (the baby is crying; active). *The lost wallet* (the wallet was lost; passive). A participle can also come after the noun: *The boy standing at the door is my cousin.* *The letter written yesterday has arrived.*

### Participial phrases

A participle can bring other words with it, forming a short phrase that joins two sentences into one.

- *Seeing the policeman, the thief ran away.* (= When he saw the policeman, the thief ran away.)
- *Tired after the long walk, we went to bed early.* (= Because we were tired ...)
- *Having locked the door, Ravi left for office.* (= After he had locked the door, ...)

The phrase describes a noun, and the usual place for that noun is **right after the comma**. This is where the trap lies (see below).

### Gerund or participle?

Both end in -ing, so ask what job the word does.

- **Noun** job: gerund. *Reading is fun.* *a swimming pool* (a pool for swimming).
- **Adjective** job: participle. *a reading girl* (a girl who is reading). *a swimming boy* (a boy who is swimming).

A quick test: if the -ing word can be replaced by "for ..." (*a sleeping bag* = a bag for sleeping), it is a gerund. If it can be replaced by "who is ..." (*a sleeping baby* = a baby who is sleeping), it is a participle.

## The dangling participle {lvl=2 id=s-dangle label="Class 10"}

A participial phrase must refer to the **subject of the main clause**: the word right after the comma. If it does not, the phrase "dangles", and the sentence says something silly.

- Wrong: *Walking home, the rain began.* (Was the rain walking home?)
- Right: *Walking home, we felt the rain begin.* Or: *As we were walking home, the rain began.*
- Wrong: *Having finished the exam, the bell rang.* Right: *Having finished the exam, the students heard the bell ring.*
- Wrong: *Opening the box, a snake was seen.* Right: *Opening the box, he saw a snake.*

To fix a dangling phrase, ask **who** did the action in the phrase, and make that person the subject of the main clause. Or turn the phrase into a full clause with its own subject.

:::qc
q: Which sentence has a dangling participle?
options: ["Reading in the garden, Asha heard a bird.", "Reading in the garden, a bird was heard.", "Asha, reading in the garden, heard a bird."]
answer: 1
right: Right. A bird was not reading. The doer of reading is Asha, who must be the subject of the main clause.
wrong: Check who is reading in each sentence. Only in the second does the subject of the main clause (a bird) fail to match the person doing the reading.
:::

## Choosing between the infinitive and the gerund {lvl=2 id=s-choose label="Class 10"}

Some verbs take **either** form, but with a change in meaning. Learn these for the board.

| Verb | + infinitive | + gerund |
|---|---|---|
| **stop** | *He stopped to smoke.* (stopped in order to smoke) | *He stopped smoking.* (gave it up) |
| **remember** | *Remember to lock the door.* (do not forget to do it) | *I remember locking the door.* (I recall that I did it) |
| **forget** | *I forgot to post the letter.* (it was not posted) | *I forgot posting the letter.* (I posted it but cannot recall it) |
| **try** | *Try to be on time.* (make an effort) | *Try adding salt.* (experiment) |
| **regret** | *I regret to inform you ...* (sorry to say) | *I regret saying that.* (sorry I said it) |

Some verbs, such as *begin, start, continue, like, love, hate, prefer*, take both with little change: *It began to rain. It began raining.* With *like*, the gerund usually means enjoyment (*I like swimming*), and the infinitive a choice or habit (*I like to swim early in the morning*). Treat this last point as a general tendency, not a firm rule [Unverified].

### Seeing the whole action or part of it

After verbs of perception, the bare infinitive shows the **complete** action; the -ing form shows the action **in progress**.

- *I saw him cross the road.* (I watched from start to finish.)
- *I saw him crossing the road.* (He was in the middle of it.)

### Possessive before a gerund

A gerund is a noun, so a possessive often goes before it: *I do not like your talking so loudly.* In everyday speech, the object form is also common (*I do not like you talking so loudly*). Either is accepted in most exam keys, but the possessive is the formal choice [Unverified].

## Non-finite verbs in the board's questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

The board paper tests these forms in four ways.

1. **Gap filling with the verb in brackets.** *She enjoys (swim) in the lake.* Look at the word before the gap: enjoy needs -ing, so *swimming*.
2. **Error correction.** Each line has one error, often a gerund after a preposition (*good at to cook*), a wrong form after a modal (*can to swim*) or a dangling participle.
3. **Combining sentences.** You are asked to join two sentences into one, using a participle, an infinitive or a gerund.
4. **Transformation.** Change a clause into a phrase or the reverse: *He was so tired that he could not walk* becomes *He was too tired to walk.*

A reliable routine:

- Is there a **preposition** before the gap? Use the gerund.
- Is there a **modal**, **let**, **make** or **had better**? Use the bare infinitive.
- Does the main verb belong to the *want/hope/decide* group? Use to + verb.
- Does the main verb belong to the *enjoy/finish/avoid* group? Use -ing.
- Does a phrase begin the sentence with a participle? Check that the doer is the subject after the comma.

:::worked title="Combining sentences in three ways"
Join: *The boy saw the dog. He climbed the wall.*

With a **participle**: *Seeing the dog, the boy climbed the wall.* The doer of seeing and of climbing is the same person.

With an **infinitive**: *The boy climbed the wall to escape the dog.* This changes the meaning slightly (it gives a purpose), which is fine if the original sentence suggests it. Keep the meaning unchanged in exam answers.

With a **gerund**: *On seeing the dog, the boy climbed the wall.* After the preposition on, the gerund *seeing* is needed.

For two actions in order, use the perfect participle: *The boy had finished his homework. He went out to play.* becomes *Having finished his homework, the boy went out to play.*
:::

:::worked title="Spotting errors in a short passage"
Find and correct the error in each line.

*(a) Ashok is fond of to play chess.* The preposition of takes a gerund: fond of playing.
*(b) He can to solve the puzzle quickly.* A modal takes the bare infinitive: can solve.
*(c) Having seen the snake, the stick was dropped.* Dangling: the stick did not see the snake. Correct to: Having seen the snake, he dropped the stick.
*(d) She suggested to go for a walk.* Suggest takes a gerund: suggested going for a walk.
*(e) I look forward to see you soon.* To is a preposition here: look forward to seeing you.
:::

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["She is used to get up early.", "She is used to getting up early.", "She is used to got up early."]
answer: 1
right: Right. In "be used to", the word to is a preposition, so a gerund follows: used to getting up.
wrong: In "be used to" (meaning accustomed to), to is a preposition, so the verb takes -ing: getting up.
:::

## Beyond the board: finer points {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### Absolute constructions

Sometimes a participle phrase has its **own subject**, and it is deliberately not linked to the subject of the main clause. This is correct, unlike a dangling participle, because the phrase has its own noun.

- *The weather being fine, we set out.* (The weather being fine is a complete modifier with its own subject.)
- *Weather permitting, the match will be played on Sunday.*
- *All the tickets having been sold, the show was cancelled.*

The test is that the phrase has an explicit subject of its own. A dangling participle has none.

### Infinitive of result

An infinitive can show an outcome, sometimes an unexpected one: *He grew up to be a doctor.* *She woke to find the house empty.* *He hurried to the station only to find the train gone.* The last pattern (*only to*) signals disappointment.

### The infinitive as a plan or duty: be + to

*The Prime Minister is to visit Japan next month.* (an arranged plan) *You are to report at nine.* (an order) Compare with *will* or *must*: this form belongs to official, reported language.

### The split infinitive

Putting a word between *to* and the verb is a split infinitive: *to boldly go*. Older handbooks forbade it. Many modern style guides allow it when it reads better [Unverified]. Board answers are marked for correctness, so keep the infinitive together when you can: *to go boldly*.

### Choosing for style

Participle phrases make writing compact, but too many in a row blur who is doing what. Gerund subjects (*Reading widely improves your vocabulary*) sound more formal than *it* sentences (*It improves your vocabulary to read widely*). Reading closely for these choices will help in comprehension and writing tasks, even when the board does not ask for the names.

:::qc
q: "The match ___ cancelled because of rain, the players went home." Which fits best?
options: ["having been", "been", "to be", "be"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The cancellation came before the players went home and the phrase has its own subject, so the perfect participle having been fits (an absolute construction).
wrong: The cancellation was earlier than the going home, so you need the perfect participle: having been cancelled.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A finite verb has a subject, shows tense and agrees with the subject. A non-finite verb (infinitive, gerund, participle) does none of these, and works as a noun, adjective or adverb. {lvl=1}
2. The infinitive is to + base verb (or the bare base verb). It can be a noun (To err is human), an adjective (a book to read) or an adverb of purpose (He came to help). {lvl=1}
3. Use the bare infinitive after modals, let, make, help, had better, would rather and, with an object, after see/hear/watch. {lvl=1}
4. The gerund is the -ing form used as a noun. It follows prepositions and verbs like enjoy, finish, avoid, mind, deny, suggest, practise. {lvl=1}
5. The participle is a verb form used as an adjective: present (smiling), past (broken) and perfect (having finished). {lvl=1}
6. In "look forward to", "be used to" and "object to", the word to is a preposition, so the verb after it takes -ing. {lvl=2}
7. A participle phrase must refer to the subject of the main clause; otherwise it dangles. {lvl=2}
8. Stop, remember, forget, try and regret change meaning with the infinitive and the gerund. {lvl=2}
9. Too ... to and enough to are infinitive patterns for result. {lvl=2}
10. Absolute constructions (The weather being fine, ...) have their own subject and are correct; infinitives of result (only to find) and be + to belong to formal writing. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "She can to swim", "He must to go": a modal takes the bare infinitive, no to. {lvl=1}
- "He made me to cry": use the bare infinitive after make in the active voice (made me cry). {lvl=1}
- "I enjoy to read", "He avoided to meet her": enjoy and avoid take the gerund. {lvl=1}
- "I want going", "She hopes passing": want and hope take to + verb. {lvl=1}
- "Good at to draw", "interested to learn", "without to say": after a preposition use -ing. {lvl=2}
- "I look forward to meet you", "He is used to wake early": to is a preposition in these phrases; use the gerund. {lvl=2}
- Dangling participles ("Walking home, the rain began"): the doer must be the subject of the main clause. {lvl=2}
- "He suggested to go": suggest takes a gerund (suggested going) or a that-clause. {lvl=2}
- Mixing up stop to do and stop doing, or remember to do and remember doing: the meanings differ. {lvl=2}
- Using the passive without to after make: "He was made to wait", not "He was made wait". {lvl=2}
- Treating every -ing word as a gerund: ask whether it is a noun (gerund) or an adjective (participle). {lvl=3}
:::
