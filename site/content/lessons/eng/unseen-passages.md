## Why the exam gives you a stranger {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Every textbook chapter you have studied was read in class, explained and revised. An **unseen passage** takes all that away on purpose. It is a short text you meet for the first time, with questions underneath. Nobody can memorise it in advance, so the paper tests something deeper: whether you can understand a stranger's writing by yourself.

That is the same skill you use every day. You read a notice, a message from a teacher, a recipe, a news report. In each case a writer has put something on a page, and you have to take out what is there, no more and no less.

Two ideas hold the whole lesson together.

1. **The answer is on the page.** Whatever is asked, the passage contains the evidence. Your own opinion, general knowledge and guesses do not earn marks.
2. **Reading has speeds.** You do not read a timetable the way you read a story. Good readers choose the speed to suit the purpose.

## How a passage is built {lvl=1 id=s-build label="Foundation"}

A passage is made of paragraphs, and a paragraph is made of sentences with different jobs. Learning the jobs lets you see the structure quickly.

| Part | What it does | Where you find it |
|---|---|---|
| **Title** | Names the topic, sometimes the angle | Above the passage (in an unseen passage there may be none) |
| **Topic sentence** | States the main point of the paragraph | Usually the first sentence; sometimes the last |
| **Support** | Reasons, examples, numbers, quotations that back the topic sentence | The middle of the paragraph |
| **Linking words** | Show how one idea relates to another (but, for example, as a result) | Start of sentences, or inside them |
| **Conclusion** | Pulls the argument together or says what follows | The last paragraph, often the last sentence |

Take this short paragraph.

> Monsoon rains are good for farmers, but they are not equally good for everyone. In low-lying towns, a single night of heavy rain can flood the streets. Shops close, buses stop, and families move their belongings to upper floors.

The topic sentence is the first one: rain is not equally good for everyone. The next two sentences are support, giving one example (flooded towns) with its effects. If a question asks "What is the paragraph mainly about?", the topic sentence usually gives you the answer, and the support tells you which options are too narrow.

:::qc
q: In the monsoon paragraph above, which sentence is the topic sentence?
options: ["Monsoon rains are good for farmers, but they are not equally good for everyone.", "In low-lying towns, a single night of heavy rain can flood the streets.", "Shops close, buses stop, and families move their belongings to upper floors."]
answer: 0
right: Right. It states the main point; the other two sentences give an example and its effects.
wrong: The second and third sentences describe flooding in particular, which is an example. The first sentence gives the general point that they support.
:::

## Skimming and scanning {lvl=1 id=s-speeds label="Foundation"}

You need two different kinds of fast reading, and they do different jobs.

| | Skimming | Scanning |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Get the gist: what is this about? | Find one fact: a name, date, number or phrase |
| **What you read** | Title, first line of each paragraph, last line, bold words | Only the part around the key word you are hunting |
| **Speed** | Fast, with the eyes moving down the page | Very fast, ignoring everything not matching the target |
| **When** | At the start, once | For each question, after the skim |

A good way to scan is to take the key word from the question (a name, a number, a rare word) and look for it in the passage, remembering that the passage may use a different word with the same meaning. A question that says "How many members chose the study space?" will be answered by a number near the words "study space" or "quiet study".

:::qc
q: A question asks, "At what time does the market become crowded?" Which reading speed is best for finding the answer?
options: ["Scanning for a time near the words about crowds", "Reading the whole passage again slowly", "Skimming only the title"]
answer: 0
right: Right. It is a single detail, so scan for the time word and the idea of crowding.
wrong: One specific fact needs scanning, not a slow reread or a title-only skim. Hunt for the time expression near the crowd.
:::

## Main idea and detail {lvl=1 id=s-main label="Foundation"}

A question such as "What is the passage mainly about?" or "Choose the best title" asks for the **main idea**, the one point that all the paragraphs serve. A detail question asks about one fact inside a paragraph. Mixing them up costs marks.

A reliable test for a main idea: it must be **wide enough** to cover the whole passage, and **narrow enough** to say something. Suppose a passage describes a Sunday market from sunrise to afternoon, and explains that it works on memory and trust.

| Option | Why it fails or fits |
|---|---|
| "Vegetables are sold in markets" | Too wide and too plain; it says nothing about this passage |
| "Mr Joseph sells books for ten rupees" | Too narrow; it is one detail |
| "A Sunday market comes alive for a few hours and runs on trust" | Covers the passage and says something specific |

When in doubt, check each paragraph: does the option agree with all of them? The one that does is the main idea.

:::qc
q: A passage spends three paragraphs on how a village library in a school corridor began, grew and was loved. Which is the best main idea?
options: ["Libraries have books", "The story of a village library that began small and grew", "The first book on the shelf was a dictionary"]
answer: 1
right: Right. It covers all three paragraphs without being vague.
wrong: One option is too general to say anything, and another is a single detail. The middle option covers the beginning, the growth and the affection.
:::

## The CBSE reading section {lvl=2 id=s-cbse label="Class 10"}

The Class 10 paper opens with a Reading section of two passages. Here is the shape as it has been in recent years; the exact marks, passage lengths and number of questions can change, so check the current year's sample paper and marking scheme [Unverified: exact split].

| Passage | What it is | Typical questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Case-based factual** | A situation or report with facts, often with a table, notice, chart or short data summary | MCQs, short answers, True / False / Not given, finding a figure, reading a table |
| **Discursive** | An argument or discussion of an idea, with views and reasons | MCQs on main idea and meaning, short answers on reasons and the writer's view, vocabulary in context |

Together the two passages carry about a quarter of the paper's marks (20 of 80) [Unverified]. That is a lot for a section where every answer is already printed on the page, which is why it pays to practise the method until it is automatic.

The questions come in a small number of families.

| Question family | What it asks | How to attack it |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct (literal)** | A fact stated in one place | Scan for the key word, read that sentence, answer |
| **Main idea / title** | The point of a paragraph or the whole | Topic sentences; wide enough, narrow enough |
| **Reference** | What does "it", "this", "they" stand for? | Look back to the nearest noun that fits in the same place |
| **Vocabulary** | A word or phrase in context | Read the sentence around it; next lesson covers this in depth |
| **Inference** | What the passage suggests without saying | Evidence in the passage; next lesson covers this in depth |
| **True / False / Not given** | Does the passage support a statement? | Compare with the exact sentence; if absent, Not given |

## A reliable method: locate, read, verify {lvl=2 id=s-method label="Class 10"}

For every question, follow three steps, even when the answer seems obvious.

1. **Locate.** Pick the key words in the question and find where the passage talks about them. For a main-idea question, use the topic sentences instead.
2. **Read around it.** Read the sentence before and after as well. Many wrong answers come from reading half a sentence, or from missing a word like "not", "only" or "although".
3. **Verify.** Check your answer against the page. Can you point to the words that prove it? If you cannot, the answer is a guess.

A few small habits protect marks.

- **Work in the order of the questions.** They usually follow the order of the passage, so you move through it once.
- **Treat "according to the passage" as a command.** It means: forget what you know; report what the passage says.
- **Watch the signpost words.** *However* and *but* flip the direction; *for example* introduces support; *therefore* introduces a result. The sentence after a *but* often carries the writer's real view.
- **Keep your pencil on one passage at a time.** Do not carry the idea of passage one into passage two.

## A discursive passage, worked {lvl=2 id=s-discursive label="Class 10"}

A discursive passage argues. Its skeleton is nearly always the same: a **claim** (what the writer wants you to believe), **reasons** and examples, a **counterpoint** (the other side, often introduced by *however*, *of course*, *yet*), and a **conclusion**. Reading for this skeleton shows you which sentences are central and which are decoration.

Here is a worked example, written for this archive.

:::worked title="The Gift of Boredom"
> Ask any parent what they dread hearing in the summer holidays and the answer is likely to be the same two words: "I'm bored." We hurry to cure it. We hand over a phone, switch on the television, or enrol the child in one more class. Boredom, we assume, is a problem to be solved as quickly as possible.
>
> Yet some psychologists suggest that we may be treating the wrong patient. When a child has nothing to do, the mind does not simply stop. It begins to wander, to invent, to ask itself what it would like to do. Many writers and scientists recall that their best ideas came not in the middle of busy work but during long, empty afternoons, on train journeys, or while waiting for someone. A child who has been left alone for an hour with a cardboard box and no instructions often returns with a ship, a shop or a robot. The boredom was the doorway; the creativity came afterwards.
>
> Of course, not all boredom is useful. A child who is bored because the lessons are too easy, or too hard, needs a teacher's attention, not a lecture on patience. Nor should anyone suggest that children be left alone all day. Companionship, books and games matter enormously. The point is narrower: that a schedule packed from morning until night leaves no space in which a child can learn to entertain herself.
>
> There is also the matter of what screens do. A phone is an excellent cure for boredom, which is exactly the trouble. It fills every gap so completely that the child never discovers what she would have done with the gap. Over time, the ability to sit with an empty hour, which adults often call "being at peace", may grow weaker from lack of use, like a muscle.
>
> The next time a child complains of having nothing to do, then, it may be wiser to resist the urge to rescue. A short, uncomfortable silence is not a failure of parenting. It may be the beginning of something the child will make entirely her own.

**Skeleton.** Claim: boredom can be valuable and should not be rushed away. Reasons: a free mind invents (the cardboard box), and screens fill every gap. Counterpoint: not all boredom is useful, and children should not be left alone all day (*Of course*, *Nor*). Conclusion: resist the urge to rescue.

**Q1. What is the passage mainly about?** (a) How to keep children busy in holidays; (b) The possible benefit of letting children be bored; (c) The dangers of phones; (d) Why parents dislike holidays. Answer: (b). It covers the claim, the reasons and the conclusion. Option (c) is one reason, so it is too narrow; (a) is the opposite of the argument.

**Q2. What does the writer mean by "treating the wrong patient"?** Parents treat boredom as an illness to be cured, but the writer suggests the boredom is not the real problem. [Evidence: the sentences before it, where boredom is "a problem to be solved".]

**Q3. According to the writer, why is a phone "exactly the trouble"?** Because it fills every empty moment so completely that the child never finds out what she would have done with it. [Evidence: paragraph four, sentence two.]

**Q4. Which of these does the writer NOT say?** (a) Some boredom needs a teacher's help; (b) Children should be left alone all day; (c) Companionship matters; (d) Famous people found ideas in empty hours. Answer: (b). The writer says the opposite in paragraph three ("Nor should anyone suggest..."). This is a classic NOT question: three options are in the passage and one is not.

**Q5. Find a word in paragraph 2 that means "to create something new".** *Invent*. The question names the paragraph and the meaning, and the answer must be a word taken from the passage, not one of your own.
:::

Notice how every answer points to a sentence, and how Q4 is built: three options come from the passage and one does not.

:::qc
q: In the passage above, the writer begins paragraph three with "Of course, not all boredom is useful." What is the job of this sentence?
options: ["It gives the counterpoint, admitting a limit to the claim", "It repeats the claim in other words", "It changes the topic to the lessons in school"]
answer: 0
right: Right. "Of course" signals a concession: the writer admits a limit before returning to the main point. That makes the argument fairer.
wrong: The sentence does not repeat the claim, and it does not leave the topic. "Of course" is a classic sign of a counterpoint, where the writer fairly admits a limit.
:::

## A case-based factual passage, worked {lvl=2 id=s-factual label="Class 10"}

A case-based factual passage gives you facts about a real or realistic situation: a library survey, a school notice, a city scheme. Often there is a table. The skill is to read it **accurately**, because nothing in it is opinion; every question has one correct figure or statement.

Before you read the numbers, read the **title, headings and units**. A table of "Members (number)" and "Share (%)" says two different things about the same people.

:::worked title="Green Park Public Library: a member survey"
> Green Park Public Library, which serves a neighbourhood of about 4,000 households, ran a month-long survey in March to understand how its members use it. Staff handed a short form to every member who visited, and 620 forms were returned. The library is open from 9 a.m. to 7 p.m. and is closed on Mondays.
>
> The survey asked each member which service they used most. The results are in the table.

| Service used most | Members | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Borrowing books | 279 | 45% |
| Reading newspapers and magazines on site | 124 | 20% |
| Children's story hour | 93 | 15% |
| Computer and internet corner | 62 | 10% |
| Quiet study space | 62 | 10% |
| **Total** | **620** | **100%** |

> The librarian, Ms Iyer, also asked what would make members visit more often. 38 per cent asked for longer weekday opening hours, and 27 per cent wanted more new titles. Only 6 per cent complained about the annual membership fee of Rs 120. Based on the findings, the library committee has decided to extend weekday closing time to 8 p.m. from 1 April, to buy 150 new titles, and to keep the fee unchanged.

**Q1. Which service did the largest number of members use most?** Borrowing books (279 members). Scan the table's "Members" column for the biggest number.

**Q2. How many members chose the computer corner or the study space?** 62 + 62 = 124. The same figure appears for newspapers (124), which is why you must read the row labels, not just spot a familiar number.

**Q3. "A majority of members use the library mainly for borrowing books." True, False or Not given?** False. 45 per cent is the largest group but it is less than half. A *majority* means more than half, so the table does not support the statement.

**Q4. Why has the committee extended closing time?** Because the biggest single request (38 per cent) was for longer weekday opening hours.

**Q5. Does the passage say what time the library opens on Sundays?** Not given. It says only "9 a.m. to 7 p.m." and "closed on Mondays"; it does not describe Sundays separately.
:::

Two traps appear again and again in factual passages: reading the wrong **row** of a table, and mixing up **counts and percentages** (279 members is not 279 per cent). A third trap is the difference between *largest group* and *majority*.

:::qc
q: In the library survey, which statement is supported by the table?
options: ["Fewer members chose newspapers than the story hour", "More than half the members use the study space", "The computer corner and study space were chosen by equal numbers"]
answer: 2
right: Right. Both rows show 62 members.
wrong: Compare the rows one by one. Newspapers (124) beats the story hour (93), so the first option is false, and the study space has only 10 per cent, nowhere near half. Both the computer corner and the study space show 62.
:::

## Writing the answer the paper wants {lvl=2 id=s-answer label="Class 10"}

Even when you have found the right sentence, the answer can lose marks by its shape. Match the answer to the question.

| Question type | What to write | Common loss of marks |
|---|---|---|
| **MCQ** | One option, chosen by eliminating the wrong ones | Choosing the option that "sounds right" but goes beyond the passage |
| **Short answer (one or two lines)** | A complete, direct answer in your own words, starting with the point | Copying three lines when one clause is needed; leaving the question half-answered |
| **True / False / Not given** | One of the three, with the sentence as evidence if asked | Using what you know instead of what the passage says |
| **Find a word / phrase** | A word or phrase taken from the passage | Offering your own synonym; wrong form or spelling |
| **Reference** | The noun or idea the pronoun replaces | Picking a noun that is nearby but does not fit |

### Eliminating MCQ options

For an MCQ, treat the options as suspects. Cross out any that:

- are **not in the passage** at all;
- say the **opposite** of the passage;
- use **extreme words** (always, never, all, only) when the passage said "often" or "some";
- are **true in real life** but not stated in the passage;
- are **too narrow** (one detail) or **too wide** (the whole subject).

What is left is the answer. If two remain, go back to the passage and test each against the actual sentence.

### Short answers: own words, same meaning

In a short answer, give the idea in your own words but keep the meaning exactly. Do not lift a whole sentence from the passage if a clause will do, and do not add your own opinion.

| Question | Weak answer | Better answer |
|---|---|---|
| "Why does the writer say a phone is 'exactly the trouble'?" | "A phone is an excellent cure for boredom, which is exactly the trouble. It fills every gap so completely that the child never discovers what she would have done with the gap." | "Because it fills every empty moment, so the child never learns what she might have done with it." |
| "Why do most riders pay nothing?" (a bike scheme) | "Because it is free." | "Because the first 30 minutes are free and the average ride is shorter than that." |

The better answers are shorter, direct and fully correct. They begin with the "because".

:::qc
q: A short-answer question asks: "Why did the library committee extend the weekday closing time?" Which answer is best?
options: ["Because the largest number of members asked for longer weekday hours", "Because libraries should be open longer", "Because 4,000 households live nearby"]
answer: 0
right: Right. It uses the evidence from the passage in a single direct sentence.
wrong: One option states an opinion that the passage does not give, and another quotes a number that has nothing to do with the decision. The first answer uses the passage's own evidence.
:::

## Reading like an examiner: distractors {lvl=3 id=s-distractors label="Beyond the board"}

A good MCQ has one correct answer and three plausible wrong ones. Wrong options are called **distractors**, and they are not random. Examiners build them from the same few materials, so once you learn the types, you spot them quickly.

| Distractor type | How it works | Example (passage says: "Most riders use the scheme to reach a bus stop") |
|---|---|---|
| **Too narrow** | Takes one detail and calls it the main point | "A few riders go to the market" |
| **Too wide** | States something general the passage does not specifically say | "People enjoy cycling" |
| **Reversal** | Says the opposite of the passage | "Few riders use it to reach public transport" |
| **True but not in the passage** | A fact from real life | "Cycling reduces air pollution" (not claimed here) |
| **Extreme** | Adds *all*, *only*, *never* | "All riders use it to reach a bus stop" |
| **Right words, wrong meaning** | Copies the passage's words in a new order | "Bus stops reach most riders" |

The last type is the cruellest: it looks right because it sounds like the passage. Always test the meaning, not just the vocabulary.

:::qc
q: The passage says "Most riders use the scheme to reach a bus stop or metro station." Which option is an extreme distractor?
options: ["Every rider uses the scheme only to reach a bus stop", "Some riders use the scheme to reach a bus stop", "A large share of riders use it to reach public transport"]
answer: 0
right: Right. "Every ... only" goes far beyond "most", so it is the extreme option.
wrong: Compare each with "most". The second and third say about the same thing as "most". The first uses "every" and "only", which are far stronger.
:::

## Tone, purpose and the writer's stance {lvl=3 id=s-tone label="Beyond the board"}

Strong readers notice not only what is said but how. Three features recur in questions at the top end.

- **Purpose.** Why was this written: to inform, to persuade, to warn, to entertain, to criticise? A report informs; an editorial persuades; a notice instructs.
- **Tone.** The writer's attitude, carried by word choice: *admiring*, *sceptical*, *concerned*, *amused*, *neutral*. One test: replace a strong word with a plainer one and see what is lost.
- **Stance.** Where the writer stands. In a balanced passage, the writer may present two sides and lean towards one in the last paragraph. Find the paragraph where the writer stops describing others' views and says "I" or "we" or "the sensible position".

The writer of the boredom passage informs the parent gently, using humour ("treating the wrong patient") and a calm, reasonable tone. If a question asks for the tone, the best adjective would be *thoughtful* or *gently persuasive*, not *angry* or *neutral*. (Inference, the next lesson, builds on this.)

## Training your reading {lvl=3 id=s-train label="Beyond the board"}

Speed and accuracy come from habit. A short plan:

| Step | What to do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Glance at the question stems (not the options) | 20 seconds |
| 2 | Skim the passage: title, first lines of paragraphs, last line | 1 minute |
| 3 | Read the passage once at normal speed, marking the topic sentences | 2–3 minutes |
| 4 | Answer in order, scanning back for each | 30–60 seconds per question |
| 5 | Check any answer you were unsure of, once | Remaining time |

These timings are a suggested budget for practice, not an official rule [Inference]. Adjust them to your own speed.

Outside the exam, the best practice is to read widely and for enjoyment: newspaper editorials, popular science, reports. After each, say in one sentence what the writer wants you to think. If you can do that, you can answer most unseen-passage questions.

:::qc
q: Why is it a bad idea to bring your own opinion into a comprehension answer?
options: ["Because the question asks what the passage says, not what you think", "Because opinions are always wrong", "Because the examiner dislikes long answers"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The task is to report the passage faithfully; your own view belongs in a writing question, not here.
wrong: Opinions are not always wrong, but a comprehension question asks about the passage. The marking scheme looks for the passage's own point.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. An unseen passage tests whether you can understand a text you have never met; the answer to every question is on the page. {lvl=1}
2. Read in this order: glance at the questions, skim the passage for the gist, then go back to the page for each answer. {lvl=1}
3. Skimming is for the gist; scanning is for one detail. {lvl=1}
4. A paragraph has a topic sentence and support; the main idea is wide enough for the whole passage and narrow enough to say something. {lvl=1}
5. The Class 10 Reading section has a discursive passage and a case-based factual passage; check the current sample paper for the exact marks and format. {lvl=2}
6. Method for every question: locate, read around, verify against the page. {lvl=2}
7. A discursive passage has a claim, reasons, a counterpoint and a conclusion; signpost words (however, of course, therefore) mark the turns. {lvl=2}
8. In a factual passage read the title, headings and units first; keep counts and percentages apart; "majority" means more than half. {lvl=2}
9. True means the passage says it; False means it says the opposite; Not given means it does not say. {lvl=2}
10. Answer in the right shape: an option, a word taken from the passage, or a short, direct sentence in your own words. {lvl=2}
11. Distractors are too narrow, too wide, reversed, true-but-not-in-the-passage, extreme, or right words with the wrong meaning. {lvl=3}
12. Purpose, tone and stance show how the writer says things as well as what is said. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Reading the whole passage word by word before looking at any question: skim first, and read with a purpose. {lvl=1}
- Choosing a main idea that is a single detail, or one so general that it fits any passage. {lvl=1}
- Answering from common sense or memory when the question says "according to the passage". {lvl=2}
- Reading half a sentence and missing "not", "only", "although" or "however". {lvl=2}
- Treating Not given as False: if the passage is silent, the answer is Not given, even if the statement sounds unlikely. {lvl=2}
- Mixing counts and percentages, reading the wrong row of a table, or ignoring units. {lvl=2}
- Treating "largest group" as a "majority". {lvl=2}
- Copying three lines from the passage when a short direct answer was asked for. {lvl=2}
- Picking the option that sounds like the passage's words without testing its meaning. {lvl=3}
- Letting the tone of the passage persuade you to agree: understand the argument first, judge it afterwards. {lvl=3}
:::
