## The extract in brief {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

The chapter has three connected parts, all in Anne's own chatty voice.

**Why she writes.** Anne begins by saying that writing a diary feels odd, because she has never written anything before and thinks nobody will care about a thirteen-year-old's musings. Yet she wants to write. She recalls the saying that paper has more patience than people, and decides she will not let anyone read the notebook until she finds a real friend. The problem is that she does not have one, even though she has a loving family and many classmates she can laugh with. With them she talks only about ordinary things, and they never get closer. So she will make the diary her friend, named Kitty.

**Who she is.** Because Kitty would not understand her stories without background, Anne gives a short sketch of her life. Her father moved from Germany to Holland in 1933, her mother followed, and Anne and her sister Margot came a little later. She went to a Montessori nursery school, then to school, where her sixth-form teacher was a headmistress she was sad to leave. Her grandmother fell ill and died, and Anne still loves her and misses her. The sketch ends at the present date, 20 June 1942.

**What happened at school.** The class is nervous before the meeting at which teachers decide who will move up. Anne is mostly sure of herself, except in maths. Her maths teacher, Mr Keesing, gives her extra essays as punishment for talking too much. She argues in the first that talking is an inherited habit, and in the second he gives her another topic. For the third, titled "Quack, Quack, Quack, Said Mistress Chatterbox", she writes a poem with a friend's help. He enjoys it, reads it to the class, and stops punishing her.

answer: 1
right: Yes. Anne is not short of company but of closeness. Her stated reason is that she has no one true friend, so the diary will fill that place.

## Diary, journal, log, memoir {lvl=2 id=s-types label="Class 10"}

The chapter's warm-up asks you to separate four kinds of personal record. They look alike, but each answers a different need.

| Term | What it is | Typical feature | Mini example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diary | a book with a space for each day for your thoughts, feelings and events | personal, informal, uses "I" | "I woke up very late today and got a scolding." |
| Journal | a full record of a journey, a period or an event, written every day | descriptive, covers a trip or project | notes on a trip to Ooty and what you saw |
| Log | a written record of events with times and dates, usually official | timed, brief, factual | a list of meetings with times |
| Memoir | a record of a person's own life and experiences, usually a famous person's | written afterwards, shaped like a story | a film star recalling how he was cast |

**What makes Anne's diary special.** The sample entries in the exercise are short and mostly factual. Anne's entry does something different: it talks to a named friend, explains her feelings, tells a funny story in full and shows her character. That is why a diary like hers can become a document that historians and ordinary readers value, as Samuel Pepys's diary of the fire of London did.

:::qc
q: An entry reads: "10:30 a.m. Went to the office of the Director; 1:00 p.m. Lunch with Chairman." Which kind of record is it most like?
options: ["A memoir, because it shows a life", "A log, because it lists events with times and dates", "A journal, because it records a journey"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. The line-by-line times and appointments are the mark of a log, an official or work record. (A busy person might also keep it as a diary, but the time-stamped list points to a log.)
wrong: A memoir is a life story written for readers, and a journal records a journey or period in a flowing way. Which kind of record lists time and event, time and event?
:::

## Themes {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10"}

**Loneliness inside company.** The chapter's most serious point is Anne's admission that, on the surface, she has everything except one true friend. She blames herself a little ("Maybe it's my fault") and sees that nothing is likely to change. Notice that she is not self-pitying; she names the problem plainly and then acts.

**Writing as friendship.** She does not want to record cold facts, as most people do in a diary. She wants the notebook to be a friend. By naming it and addressing it, she makes writing feel like talking, which is why her entries sound so alive.

**Humour as strength.** Her comic account of the punishment essays turns an embarrassing moment into a story where she comes out on top. The same humour shows in the way she describes her arrival in Holland, and in the bets her classmates place on who will pass. Humour helps her cope with worry, and also draws the reader in.

**A teacher and a pupil, and mutual respect.** Mr Keesing starts as an old fogey in her eyes but gains her respect by taking the joke well. The ending is gentle: he is "always making jokes", and the punishment ends.

## The chatterbox essays {lvl=2 id=s-essays label="Class 10"}

Mr Keesing is Anne's maths teacher. She talks too much in his class, and he reacts in three steps. Each time, she finds a new way to answer.

| Round | Essay title set | What Anne did | Mr Keesing's response |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 'A Chatterbox' (three pages) | Argued that talking is a student's trait, that she would try to control it but could not cure it, since her mother talks as much or more and traits are inherited | He had a good laugh |
| 2 | 'An Incorrigible Chatterbox' | Handed it in, as before; the text does not say what she argued | He had nothing to complain about for two lessons |
| 3 | 'Quack, Quack, Quack, Said Mistress Chatterbox' | With Sanne's help she wrote it as a poem about a mother duck and a father swan whose three ducklings are bitten to death for quacking too much | He took the joke well, read the poem to the class and others, and gave her no more extra homework |

**Is Mr Keesing unpredictable?** Anne says teachers are the most unpredictable creatures on earth. Her own case supports her. After laughing at the first essay, he punishes her again; after the second, he seems satisfied for two lessons, then loses patience. When she answers with a poem he is not angry but amused, and he becomes cheerful. So he behaves in ways she cannot guess, which is her point.

**Is he strict?** A fair answer is: strict enough to act on repeated misbehaviour, but humane and humorous. He does not shout or punish severely; his punishment is writing, which Anne has to turn into a creative challenge. The evidence is that he laughs at her arguments and finally adopts her joke.

**What does it tell you about Anne?** She is quick-witted, creative and resourceful, and she refuses to be crushed by a punishment. Her motive is not to defy him but to win him over with wit. The line "I'd make sure the joke was on him" shows her playful competitiveness.

:::qc
q: What finally makes Mr Keesing stop punishing Anne for talking?

answer: 2
right: Right. Her poem about the quacking ducklings amuses him so much that he reads it out, and from then on he treats her talking as a joke.
wrong: The text gives no promise or letter. The turning point is the verse essay on the third topic, which he finds funny.
:::

## Compound words {lvl=2 id=s-compound label="Class 10"}

A **compound word** is made of two or more words joined (sometimes with a hyphen) to make one new meaning: *notebook, headmistress, long-awaited, stiff-backed*. Compounds can be nouns (homework), adjectives (stiff-backed) or verbs (baby-sit).

How to read one: split it and ask what each part adds. A *blockhead* is a head like a block, that is, a very stupid person. Use that thinking, but check the meaning, because compounds often move away from the sum of their parts.

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| heartbreaking | producing great sadness | The farewell was heartbreaking. |
| homesick | missing home and family very much | She felt homesick at the hostel. |
| blockhead | informal, a very stupid person | Don't be a blockhead; read the question. |
| law-abiding | obeying and respecting the law | A law-abiding citizen pays the tax. |
| overdo | do something to an excessive degree | Don't overdo the exercise on the first day. |
| daydream | think about pleasant things, forgetting the present | He likes to daydream in the last period. |
| breakdown | an occasion when vehicles or machines stop working | A breakdown delayed the bus. |
| output | something produced by a person, machine or organisation | The factory doubled its output. |

## Phrasal verbs {lvl=2 id=s-phrasal label="Class 10"}

**Why they exist.** English builds new meanings by pairing a common verb with a short particle (*in, up, back, out, along*). A phrasal verb is a **verb followed by a preposition or an adverb**. The pair is learnt as a unit, because the meaning may be different from either word: *get on* (board a bus) is guessable, but *get on in life* (succeed) is not.

| Phrasal verb in the text | Meaning | Does it match its parts? |
|---|---|---|
| plunge in | go straight to the topic | partly: you jump into the subject |
| kept back | not promoted (kept in the same class) | no |
| move up | go to the next grade | yes, fairly literal |
| ramble on | speak or write without focus | no |
| get along with | have a good relationship with | no |
| calm down | make (them) remain quiet | partly |
| stay in | stay indoors | yes |
| make up for | compensate | no |
| hand in | give an assignment, such as homework, to the teacher | partly |

**Separable or not?** Some phrasal verbs can be split by an object (*calm them down*, *hand it in*) and others cannot (*get along with her*). The chapter itself shows a split: "can't calm them down". Three-part verbs, like *make up for*, are also treated as units.

## Idioms and the dictionary {lvl=2 id=s-idioms label="Class 10"}

An **idiom** is a group of words with a fixed order whose meaning is not the sum of its words. If you translate one word by word, you get nonsense, so you must learn it whole.

| Idiom in the text | Meaning |
|---|---|
| quaking in its boots | shaking with fear and nervousness |
| lose heart | give up hope, become discouraged |
| for ages | for a very long time |
| the joke was on him | he became the one who looked foolish, so the tables were turned |
| caught my eye | attracted my attention |
| he'd had enough | he could not tolerate it any longer |
| laugh ourselves silly | laugh so much that we cannot control it |
| can't bring myself to | cannot make myself do something that feels unpleasant |

**Finding an idiom in a dictionary.** Look under the first meaningful word, skipping grammar words such as *a, the, for*. For *caught my eye* look under **catch** (the dictionary lists the base form, not the past tense) and then in the idiom section of the entry. In the Oxford entry for *eye* the idioms follow the ordinary meanings and are marked IDM.

**Idioms with heart.** The chapter has *not to lose heart*. Related expressions: *break somebody's heart* (cause deep sorrow), *close/dear to heart* (very important to you), *from the bottom of your heart* (with deep sincerity), *have a heart* (be kind and sympathetic), *have a heart of stone* (be unfeeling), and *your heart goes out to somebody* (you feel deep sympathy for them).

## Contracted forms {lvl=2 id=s-contract label="Class 10"}

**Why they exist.** In speech we run words together to save breath: *cannot* becomes *can't*. In writing, an apostrophe shows where letters are missing. Diaries, plays and novels use contractions because they imitate speech. Anne is writing to a friend, so her voice is spoken.

Some forms stand for two full forms:

| Contraction | Could mean | Example from the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| I'd | I had | "I'd better provide a brief sketch" (had better); "after I'd finished the rest" |
| I'd | I would | "I'd worry about that later"; "I'd make sure the joke was on him" |
| he'd | he had | "he'd finally had enough" |
| 's | is | "that's the problem"; "Mr Keesing's always making jokes" |

**Trap.** An apostrophe-s may also show possession, not a missing letter: *Grandma's candle* means the candle of Grandma. Only a contraction can be rewritten as two full words.

**When to avoid them.** Contractions belong to informal writing and speech. In a formal letter, an essay or a board answer, write the full forms.

:::qc
q: In "Mr Keesing's always making jokes", what does 's stand for?
options: ["has", "is", "belongs to"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. Mr Keesing is always making jokes. The 's here is a contracted is, not a possessive.
wrong: A possessive 's can be followed by a noun (Mr Keesing's class). Here it is followed by "always making", so it stands for a verb, is.
:::

## Writing your own diary entry {lvl=2 id=s-write label="Class 10"}

The chapter asks you to keep a diary for a week. Diary writing is not a standard long-answer format in the CBSE paper, so treat this as a skill [Unverified], but the habits help your writing in general.

| Element | What to do |
|---|---|
| Date and place | Start with the day and date |
| Address | Write to someone, as Anne does with *Dearest Kitty*, or just to yourself |
| Body | Choose one event and give detail: what happened, who said what, how you felt |
| Voice | First person, contractions, everyday expressions, a few words that "paint pictures" |
| Feelings | Show feelings, not only facts |
| Closing | A short sign-off, such as *Yours, Meera* |

Diary language has typical features: sentences without subjects (*Got up late*), fragments (*too bad, boring*), contractions and everyday expressions. Do not use these in formal writing.

**Model entry (original).**
Monday, 3 March. Dear Diary, I thought the day would be boring, and then the bus broke down in front of the sweet shop. We stood there for forty minutes, laughing ourselves silly at the driver's poetry about the engine. Got to school late, got a lecture, got a smile from Mrs Rao when I handed in my poem. Can't decide if I'm lucky or just a chatterbox. Yours, Meera

**Check before you finish:** one clear event; personal feelings; contractions used naturally; a closing line; no copied sentences.

## Voice and craft {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Direct address.** "Dearest Kitty" turns the diary into a letter. The reader is placed as Kitty's neighbour: a friend overhearing. Because Anne explains things "for Kitty" (her family sketch), the reader gets the background without feeling lectured.

**Tone and register.** The tone is chatty, mocking herself and others lightly, and sincere when she is serious. The register is informal: contractions, slang like "dummies" and "old fogey", dashes, exclamations. Short plain statements ("I don't have a friend") land hard because they follow long, bubbly sentences.

**Humour.** Several kinds work together:
1. *Hyperbole* (exaggeration): the whole class is "quaking in its boots".
2. *Comic escalation*: each new essay title is sillier than the last, ending in a nursery-rhyme quack.
3. *Reversal*: she plans to make "the joke" fall on the teacher, and it does.
4. *Self-mockery*: she calls the notebook "grandly referred to" as a diary, undercutting her own solemnity.

**Personification.** Giving the notebook a name and a role (a friend) is **personification**. The diary stops being an object and becomes a character, and that choice carries the whole chapter.

**Structure.** The extract moves from the inner (loneliness) to the outer (family history) to the lively (school), and ends on a note of laughter. The movement lets the reader meet Anne's depth before her wit.

:::qc
q: "Paper has more patience than people" is an example of
options: ["a simile, because it uses like or as", "personification, because paper is given a human quality", "a plain fact about paper"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. Patience belongs to people, and here paper is said to have it. Anne uses the idea to explain why she can tell a notebook what she cannot tell others.
wrong: A simile needs "like" or "as". Here the sentence gives paper a human quality, patience, which is personification.
:::

## Reading with hindsight {lvl=3 id=s-hindsight label="Beyond the board"}

**Dramatic irony.** The reader knows what Anne does not. The chapter's introduction tells us that within weeks she and her family will go into hiding, and that she will not survive the war. Anne worries about passing maths and teasing a teacher. Her ordinary worries become poignant.

**Was Anne right about her readers?** She guessed that nobody would care about the musings of a thirteen-year-old. The facts show otherwise. Her honest, specific voice made one girl's life feel like everyone's, and the diary became a document of the period. Specific detail often speaks louder than a general account.

**A private text made public.** Anne said she would show the notebook only to a true friend; the world became that friend, by accident. Readers should remember it was never written for them, and that it was adapted for the chapter ("with slight adaptation"). Notice what is chosen for a school extract: the lively, the funny, the human. The harshest context is in the introduction, not in the entry.

**Comparing with Pepys.** The chapter's listening task uses Samuel Pepys's account of the 1666 fire of London. Both diarists write for themselves, yet their records became history. Pepys is a witness to a public disaster; Anne is a witness to private feelings. Both remind us that ordinary diaries can become important sources.

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not call Anne selfish or unhappy; the text shows a loving, cheerful girl with one gap in her life, a true friend. {lvl=1}
- Do not say Kitty is a real person. She is the name Anne gives to the diary. {lvl=1}
- Do not mix up the three essay titles or say that Mr Keesing was angry at the poem; he found it amusing. {lvl=2}
- A character answer needs a trait, evidence and a comment; a plot retelling will not earn full marks. {lvl=2}
- An apostrophe-s is not always a contraction: Grandma's candle is possessive. {lvl=2}
- Keep a formal answer free of contractions and diary slang. {lvl=2}
- Do not quote long passages; use a short phrase and your own comment. Do not state facts about Anne's later life that are not in the chapter without labelling them [Unverified]. {lvl=3}
