## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

Gavin Maxwell lives in a cottage called Camusfearna in the West Highlands of Scotland. After his dog dies, he feels lonely and decides that the cottage, which is surrounded by water, would be a good place to try keeping an otter. In early 1956 he travels to southern Iraq. A friend tells him that otters are as common as mosquitoes in the Tigris marshes and that local people often tame them.

At Basra, Maxwell waits for his mail at the Consulate-General. He sends a cable, tries to telephone and fails three days running because of a broken line and a holiday. Five days after his friend leaves, the mail arrives, and in his bedroom he finds two Arabs and a squirming sack with a note: "Here is your otter..." (Part I). Out comes a creature coated in mud scales, like a small dragon. He calls him Mijbil, or Mij. The otter is aloof for a day, then climbs onto his bed, and soon goes wild with joy in the bath. Two days later Mij escapes and, in under a minute, turns on the bathroom tap himself. He plays with a ball and juggles marbles on his back.

Getting Mij to England is hard. The airline will take an animal only in a box no more than eighteen inches square. When Maxwell comes back from a short meal, Mij has torn the box's lining apart and is bleeding. With ten minutes left and the airport five miles away, Maxwell puts him back and races to the plane. A helpful air hostess keeps the fish cool and suggests that Mij sit on Maxwell's knee. Mij vanishes down the aircraft and causes panic ("A rat!"), then returns on his own and nuzzles Maxwell (Part III).

In London Mij invents a ping-pong game on a sloping suitcase and develops fixed habits on his walks, such as galloping along a school wall on the way home. Londoners guess he is a seal, a hippo, a beaver or even a brontosaur, and a labourer demands to know what he is supposed to be (Part IV).

## Maxwell: attitude and tone {lvl=2 id=s-maxwell label="Class 10"}

Maxwell narrates with affection and amusement. His love is clear in his words: he calls his attachment a thraldom and an "otter fixation", yet there is no regret in it. He is also **observant**: nothing Mij does escapes his notice, from the flick of a ball to the way he fumbles at a tap. He is **honest about his own mistakes** (he did not know otters and so was "quite unprepared" on the flight, Part III). He is **caring**: he removes every cutting edge from the box and rushes to the plane to keep Mij from harm.

The tone moves between wonder (the mud-coated "dragon"), comedy (the curry on his face, the labourer's glare) and worry (the bleeding otter). That mix of moods is what makes the account lively rather than dull.

## Grammar 1: would and used to {lvl=2 id=s-used-to label="Class 10"}

**Why we need the forms.** The simple past (*Mij ran*) tells us an event happened. But sometimes we want to say that something happened again and again in the past and has stopped. English keeps two forms for that.

**Rule 1: repeated actions.** Both *would* and *used to* can describe an action that happened regularly:
- *Mij would follow me without a lead* (the text).
- *When I was five, I used to follow my brother everywhere.*

**Rule 2: past states.** For a state, a situation or a thought (be, have, believe, own, like), only *used to* is correct:
- *Thirty years ago, more women used to be housewives than now.*
- *We used to believe there were ghosts in school.*

**A quick test.** Can you picture the thing as a repeated event with a start and an end, such as walking, visiting, or playing? Then both forms work. If it describes how things were (a state), use *used to* only.

| Sentence | Verb type | would | used to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Every evening we ___ walks on the beach. | action | yes | yes |
| Akbar ___ fond of music. | state | no | yes |
| My uncle ___ his holidays by the sea. | action | yes | yes |
| Few people ___ cars fifty years ago. | state (own) | no | yes |

**Common slips.** Do not write *would* before *be, have, own* or *believe* when describing the past. Do not add -d to the verb after *used to* (*used to went* is wrong; *used to go* is right). In questions and negatives, the base is *use to*: *Did you use to play?*

:::qc
q: Which sentence is NOT correct?
options: ["As a child he would play on the beach every day.", "As a child he would be afraid of dogs.", "As a child he used to be afraid of dogs."]
answer: 1
right: Right. Being afraid is a state, so *would* cannot be used. Use *used to* instead.
wrong: Look for a state of mind or being. Only *used to* works with that.
:::

## Grammar 2: noun modifiers {lvl=2 id=s-modifiers label="Class 10"}

**Why they exist.** English often squeezes a description into the words in front of a noun instead of adding a clause after it. A *dinner party* is shorter than *a party at which dinner is served*.

**The idea.** A modifier is a word that describes or limits a noun. Adjectives are the most common (*wide, flat belly*), but a noun can also do it (*the car keys*), and so can a proper noun (*the Maruti car keys*, *the Tigris marshes*, *the London streets*). More than one modifier can pile up.

| Phrase | Type of modifier | Note |
|---|---|---|
| an otter fixation | noun (otter) | a fixation on otters |
| the iron railings | noun (iron) | railings made of iron |
| the Tigris marshes | proper noun (Tigris) | marshes of the Tigris |
| the London streets | proper noun (London) | streets of London |
| soft velvet fur | adjective plus noun | soft (adjective) + velvet (noun) + fur |
| a four-footed soccer player | adjective plus noun | four-footed + soccer + player |

**Order.** A common pattern is opinion or size first, then age, colour, origin or material, then the noun modifier, and the main noun last: *the frightfully expensive golden Maruti car keys*.

**Common slips.** Noun modifiers are usually singular, even for plural things: *a shoe shop* (not *shoes shop*), *a three-hour film* (not *three-hours*).

## Grammar 3: a cloud of dust {lvl=2 id=s-quantity label="Class 10"}

We cannot count dust, snow, blood or advice. To talk about a part, a lump or a quantity of them, we use a **quantity phrase**: *a cloud of dust, a drop of blood, a pinch of salt, a piece of paper*. The phrase turns an uncountable thing into something we can picture and count (*two pieces of advice*).

| Phrase | Goes with |
|---|---|
| a bit of, a piece of | advice, information, news, land |
| a lump of | clay, sugar, coal |
| a bunch of | flowers, keys, grapes |
| a cloud of | smoke, dust |
| a gust of | wind |
| a pool of | blood, water |
| flakes of | snow |
| a heap of | stones, rubbish |

Many of these also work as pictures: a *cloud* of dust, like a cloud, rises and spreads. The phrase makes the writing more vivid than *some dust*.

## How the author makes us smile {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Comparisons.** Maxwell compares Mij to a "medievally-conceived dragon" (Part II), a footballer who dribbles with all four feet, and a "ricochetting bullet" for the speeding car in Basra. Each comparison borrows one quality (armour, skill, speed) and applies it to the scene. The comic effect comes because each is slightly exaggerated yet exactly right.

**Reporting other voices.** In Part IV, the Londoners' guesses (a seal, a squirrel, a walrus, a hippo, a leopard that changed its spots) are listed in a row. A list builds humour by repetition, and the labourer's gruff question forms the punchline. Maxwell is poking fun at himself too: he is giggling, not angry.

**Precise detail.** The tap that Mij sometimes screws tighter, the ball run down a sloping suitcase, the "every seventh" railing: details this exact make us believe the animal is real.

**Human feelings for an animal.** Maxwell compares Mij's habits to children's rituals and describes his tap trouble as irritation. This makes Mij seem human, but Maxwell never forgets he is an otter: the shredded box and the dash down the aircraft show a wild creature's needs.

**Point of view.** The story is told in the first person, so we see Mij only through Maxwell's eyes. The question in the book (Thinking about the Text 4) asks you to notice that Maxwell speaks for the otter. That is interpretation, not fact.
