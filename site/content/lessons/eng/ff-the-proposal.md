## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

The play is set in a drawing-room in Chubukov's house, and it happens in a single scene. Lomov, a large, hearty, suspicious landowner, arrives in evening dress and white gloves. Chubukov, his neighbour, greets him with a flood of pet names and assumes he has come to borrow money. He mutters this to himself, so only the audience hears it. Lomov, shaking with nerves, finally says he wants to ask for the hand of Chubukov's daughter Natalya. Chubukov is overjoyed, embraces him and goes to fetch her. Left alone, Lomov lists his many ailments and the reasons he must marry: he is thirty-five and needs a quiet life.

Natalya comes in, chatting about peas and hay. Before Lomov can propose, he mentions that Oxen Meadows, the land beside her birchwoods, belongs to him. She says it is hers. **Quarrel one** begins: both give long, tangled family histories, then shout "Mine!" and "Ours!". Chubukov joins in on his daughter's side. They trade insults about each other's relatives, and Lomov, nearly collapsing, staggers out.

Once he has gone, Chubukov rants about the "villain" who had the cheek to make a proposal. Natalya, who did not know, goes into hysterics and demands that he be brought back. Lomov returns, and she graciously admits the Meadows are his. But a few lines later, **quarrel two** begins, this time over which dog is better: her father's Squeezer or his Guess. Chubukov again joins in, and the quarrel now includes jealousy and old hunting grudges. Lomov falls into a chair; Natalya cries that he is dead. When he comes round, Chubukov hurriedly joins their hands. The couple kiss, call themselves happy, and within moments are arguing about the dogs again. Chubukov calls for champagne and the curtain falls.

## How a play works {lvl=1 id=s-play label="Foundation"}

A short story is told to you. A play is shown to an audience. That difference changes everything about how the writer works.

**No narrator.** A story can say "Lomov felt nervous." A play cannot, so Chekhov shows it: Lomov shivers, says "It's cold", drinks water and stumbles over Chubukov's name. We learn his state of mind from what he says and does.

**Dialogue carries the plot.** Every fact in the play comes out in conversation: Lomov's age, his health, the land history, even the proposal. This is why the characters keep explaining things to each other at length, such as the family history of the Meadows. Without it the audience would not know.

**Stage directions.** The words in square brackets are instructions to the actor, not words to be said aloud. They fall into a few kinds:

| Kind | Example from the play | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Entrances and exits | [Exit.], [Enter Chubukov.] | Moves characters on and off the stage and controls who hears what |
| Actions | [Clutches at his heart], [Drinks.], [Falls into an easy-chair] | Shows feeling and gives the actor something to do |
| Manner of speaking | [joyfully], [greatly moved], [teasing him], [yells] | Tells the actor the tone of voice |
| Aside | [aside] before a line | Says the line is for the audience, not the other characters |
| Sound or silence | [A pause.], [Hysterics.] | Controls timing |
| End | CURTAIN | Marks the close of the play |

**The setting is a stage, not a page.** There is one room, one afternoon, and three actors. A play with this little to look at must keep the audience interested through speech and action alone. That is why the quarrels get louder and more physical as the play proceeds.

:::qc
q: Which of these is a stage direction?
options: ["Oxen Meadows are mine!", "[Quickly steps to the carafe and drinks more water]", "You pettifogger!"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. It is in brackets, describes an action and is never spoken. The other two are lines the actor says aloud.
wrong: A stage direction is not spoken. It sits in brackets and tells the actor what to do. Which option fits?
:::

## Characters {lvl=2 id=s-chars label="Class 10"}

| Character | What we learn | Evidence in the play |
|---|---|---|
| Lomov | Thirty-five, large and hearty yet nervous, a hypochondriac, suspicious, touchy about property, wants a wife for convenience | He lists his twitching eyebrow and sleepless nights; he insists on the Meadows "on principle"; he plans to marry because he has "no time" to look for ideal love |
| Natalya | Twenty-five, practical and housewifely, proud, quick to take offence, cannot bear being wrong, but wants a husband | She is shelling peas when she meets him; she cannot let a point about her dog go; she is hysterical when she learns he came to propose |
| Chubukov | Loud, showy and affectionate in words, selfish and quick-tempered underneath, changes sides with his interest | He is sweet to Lomov when he thinks he is a rich bachelor and cruel when his land is questioned; he joins any quarrel and then asks Lomov to calm down |

**Is Chubukov sincere when he calls Lomov a son?** Weigh the evidence. He is delighted in the first scene, but he thinks Lomov has come to borrow money and shows no wish to help. His joy comes only after the word "marriage". Minutes later he calls him a scarecrow and a villain. So the warmth is about the match, which will join two estates, and not about Lomov himself.

**Is Natalya "in love"?** Her father says she is "like a lovesick cat", but she shows no sign of it. She greets Lomov with talk of hay, and picks a fight about the Meadows. She only breaks down when she hears the word "proposal", and what upsets her is losing the chance to marry, not Lomov himself. A fair answer: she wants to be married, and he is a suitable match, but she does not love him yet.

## Setting and theme {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10"}

**Setting.** One drawing-room, one afternoon. A single room keeps the focus on the three people and makes the quarrels feel claustrophobic and comic. The names, the roubles and the dessiatins (a Russian land measure of roughly 2.7 acres [Unverified]) place the story in rural Russia.

**Marriage as a business arrangement.** The introduction to the chapter says wealthy families seek ties with other wealthy families, to increase their estates. Lomov's own reasons are about age, health and housekeeping. Nobody mentions love. Even Chubukov's delight shows that this match is good for the land.

**Pride and petty quarrels.** The disputes are over a small field worth, by Natalya's own estimate, only about 300 roubles, and over dogs. Each person keeps going because losing the point feels like losing face. The characters are quarrelsome by habit; once a topic is open, they cannot let it go.

**Money and manners.** Chubukov's changing behaviour shows how self-interest shapes friendship. His neighbour is a "dear fellow" until his property is in question.

**A quarrel that never ends.** At the end the couple are engaged and bickering, which suggests the habit will follow them into marriage. The writer does not say it; the audience concludes it.

## Reported speech {lvl=2 id=s-reported label="Class 10"}

**Why it exists.** In a play you hear people's exact words. When you tell a friend what happened, you change them: "Lomov asked if he might count on her consent." The speaker is no longer present, so the pronouns, time words and tense have to shift to fit your point of view.

**The changes, with examples from the play.**

| What changes | Direct speech | Reported speech |
|---|---|---|
| Reporting verb | a question or statement | *asked* for questions; *said* or *declared* for statements |
| Present tense becomes past | "I'm getting a noise in my ears" | Lomov said that he was getting a noise in his ears |
| Present perfect becomes past perfect | "Why haven't you been here...?" | Natalya asked why he hadn't been there |
| *will* becomes *would* | "My mowers will be there" | Natalya declared that her mowers would be there |
| Pronouns shift | I, my, you | he/she, his/her, I or you according to who is speaking and who is listening |
| Time and place words | this very day, here | that very day, there |
| Respect words | "Honoured Stepan Stepanovitch..." | the adverb *respectfully* is added to the reporting clause |
| Questions lose the question word order | "What's the matter?" | Chubukov asked him what the matter was |

**A worked change.** Direct: Chubukov said, "Don't excite yourself, my precious one." Reported: Chubukov told Lomov not to excite himself and addressed him affectionately.

:::qc
q: Report this sentence: Natalya said, "My mowers will be there this very day!"
options: ["Natalya said that her mowers will be there this very day.", "Natalya declared that her mowers would be there that very day.", "Natalya declared that my mowers would be there that very day."]
answer: 1
right: Correct. *Will* becomes *would*, *My* becomes *her*, and *this very day* becomes *that very day*.
wrong: Check three things in the right answer: the tense of the helping verb, the pronoun, and the word of time. Only one option changes all three correctly.
:::

## Writing a board answer {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

Literature questions at Class 10 are of three kinds. The method is the same each time: claim, evidence, comment.

| Question type | What the examiner wants | Skeleton |
|---|---|---|
| Extract-based (short, about 40 words) | Who speaks, to whom, what it shows | Name the speaker and situation, then say what it reveals |
| Short answer (about 40 words) | One clear point with proof | Claim in one sentence, then a short phrase from the play, then your comment |
| Long answer (about 100-120 words) | A balanced answer with two or three points | Opening claim, two points each with evidence, short conclusion |

For "Is Chubukov sincere?", write: "No. At first he thinks Lomov has come to borrow money, which he refuses in an aside. His love for him as a son appears only after the word 'marriage'. His quick change to insults shows that his warmth depends on his interest."

## Farce {lvl=3 id=s-farce label="Beyond the board"}

A **farce** is a comedy that works by exaggeration. Its characters are types, its plots depend on misunderstandings and coincidences, and its pace is fast. We laugh at what happens, not at who gets hurt, because we know no real harm will come.

| Feature of a farce | Where it appears in the play |
|---|---|
| Exaggerated characters | Lomov, whose every nerve and joint is failing, and who is ready to fight a duel over a field |
| Trivial cause, huge reaction | A meadow worth 300 roubles and two hunting dogs lead to threats of lawsuits and shooting |
| Repetition | "Mine!" "Ours!" "Mine!" "Ours!" is repeated until it becomes a chant |
| Sudden reversals | Natalya goes from fury to pleading in a line; Lomov goes from dying to engaged |
| Physical comedy | Fainting, clutching the heart, running in and out, drinking water |
| A tidy, absurd end | The quarrels start up again right after the engagement |

Notice what a farce leaves out: deep feeling and consequences. We never worry for Lomov, because his illnesses come and go at convenient moments. The play invites us to laugh at human weakness, not to judge it.

:::qc
q: Why do the audience feel no real worry when Lomov seems to collapse?
options: ["The actor tells them it is fake", "Farce makes the events too exaggerated and comic to be taken as real danger", "Lomov has fainted many times in the earlier scenes"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. In a farce, the pace, the repetition and the absurd causes tell us the world is not meant to be taken seriously, so danger becomes a joke.
wrong: Think about the tone of the whole play. What kind of play lets a character nearly die and then be engaged within a page?
:::

## Dramatic irony and asides {lvl=3 id=s-irony label="Beyond the board"}

**Dramatic irony** means the audience knows what a character does not. The play uses it from the start.

1. We know Lomov came to propose. Natalya does not. Her insults about the Meadows are funny because we see her wrecking something she would like to keep.
2. Chubukov thinks Lomov wants money (aside), while we have just learnt the opposite when Lomov explains. His reaction changes comically.
3. After Lomov storms out, Chubukov reveals the secret in anger, and Natalya's lightning turn from rage to tears gives us the biggest laugh. She had been defending her pride; now she is grieving a chance.
4. At the end the couple agree to marry and at once begin to quarrel about the dogs. We see what the characters do not: they have not changed.

**Asides** are the playwright's trick for letting us inside a head. A play has no narrator, so a character has to say the hidden thought out loud. Chubukov's aside about borrowing money and Lomov's remark that it is "awfully cold" are examples. The other characters pretend not to hear, which the audience accepts as a convention.

**Comic contrast.** Dramatic irony gives a second source of fun: what people say versus what they mean. Chubukov calls Lomov "my angel" and "my darling" while thinking he wants a loan. The gap between the endearments and the aside is itself a joke.

## Chekhov's craft {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Structure.** The play repeats the same shape three times. A calm topic starts a polite exchange, one party takes offence, the other side answers, Chubukov joins, and Lomov collapses. The first round is over land, the second over dogs, the third (a rapid run of insults) over family reputations. Each round is shorter and faster, which builds the comic momentum.

**Language.** The characters are often vague and rude in the same breath. Lomov begins sentences he cannot finish, and Chubukov adds "and so on" and "and all that" to nearly everything, which shows a man who talks without thinking. Natalya's speech is blunt and domestic. The translation keeps some Russian features that strike an English ear as old: grown men are addressed as "my darling" or "my treasure", and phrases such as "Excuse me" and "I implore you" are very formal. Contemporary English would be plainer.

**Insults as characterisation.** Their insults are rarely about the dispute. They attack ancestors, health and character: drunkard grandfathers, a runaway aunt, a mother with a hump. Each insult shows what the speaker thinks is the worst thing to say. The more personal the insults get, the less each one really matters, which is another source of comedy.

**A look at the ending.** Natalya and Lomov agree to marry, and within moments she says the dog is worse again. The ending refuses to be tidy: the quarrel resumes before the champagne arrives. Compare any film comedy in which two people who quarrel end up together; the play pokes fun at that very pattern.

:::qc
q: What does the repeated "Mine!" and "Ours!" most clearly suggest?
options: ["The characters are very rich", "The characters care more about winning than about the point itself", "The characters are speaking in a foreign language"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. The Meadows are not worth much to either of them; the real fight is about not giving in, which is what makes the repetition funny.
wrong: Read the line again. Both say the land matters little to them, yet neither lets go. What does that show?

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not call the play a romance or a tragedy. It is a farce, and no one speaks of love. {lvl=1}
- Do not retell the whole play for a question on one character. Give a claim, a short piece of evidence, and a comment. {lvl=1}
- Stage directions are never spoken, so do not quote them as if a character said them. {lvl=1}
- An aside is heard by the audience, not by the other characters. Do not say a character "tells" Lomov something in an aside. {lvl=2}
- Do not say Lomov's illness is faked; the play leaves it open, and the humour is that it comes and goes with his stress. {lvl=2}
- In reported speech, change the pronoun by meaning, not by habit, and remember to change *this* to *that* and *here* to *there*. {lvl=2}
- Irony needs two sides: what the characters think and what the audience knows. Name both. {lvl=3}
- Do not quote long passages from the play in an answer. A short phrase with your own comment is better. {lvl=3}
:::
