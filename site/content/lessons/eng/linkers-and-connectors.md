right: Right. Although already marks the contrast, so a second contrast word (but) adds nothing and is wrong. One relation needs one linker.
wrong: Look at the first and third sentences. In the first, although and but both say "contrast", so one of them is extra. In the third, although has been put at the end of its clause instead of at the start. Only the second sentence uses one linker in the right place.

## Why we need linkers {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Read these two sentences next to each other.

- The bus was late. We reached school on time.

Something is missing. Did we reach on time because of the late bus, or in spite of it? The reader is left to guess. Now add one word.

- The bus was late, **but** we reached school on time.
- The bus was late, **so** we reached school late.

Same two facts, two different stories. A **linker** (also called a **connector** or **linking word**) is a word or phrase that tells the reader how one idea relates to the next. Ideas do not come with the relation attached; the writer has to say it. Without linkers, writing becomes a heap of short, equal-looking statements. With them, the reader can follow the reasoning.

Spoken language leans on tone and gesture to show the link. Written language has only words and punctuation, so it needs linkers more.

## The nine relations {lvl=1 id=s-relations label="Foundation"}

Do not memorise lists of words first. Learn the **relations**; the words follow.

| Relation | The question it answers | Typical linkers |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | What else? | and, also, moreover, besides, in addition, as well as |
| Contrast | What goes against this? | but, yet, however, although, though, while, whereas, nevertheless, on the other hand |
| Cause (reason) | Why? | because, since, as, for, because of, due to |
| Result | So what happened? | so, therefore, as a result, consequently, thus, hence |
| Condition | Under what condition? | if, unless, provided that, as long as, otherwise |
| Time and sequence | When? In what order? | when, while, before, after, until, as soon as, first, then, finally |
| Purpose | What for? | so that, in order that, in order to, lest |
| Example | Such as? | for example, for instance, such as, namely |
| Summing up | In short? | in conclusion, to sum up, in short, overall |

Cause and result are the same fact seen from opposite ends. *Because the road was flooded, the bus stopped* gives the cause first. *The road was flooded, so the bus stopped* gives the cause and then the result. Pick the linker that matches the direction you are writing in.

:::qc
q: Which relation does "so" show in "The shop was shut, so we went home"?
options: ["Result", "Contrast", "Purpose", "Addition"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The shop being shut caused us to go home, so the second clause is a result.
wrong: Ask what the second idea is to the first. Going home follows from the shop being shut. That is a result.
:::

## Coordinating conjunctions {lvl=1 id=s-coord label="Foundation"}

A **conjunction** joins words, phrases or clauses. The simplest kind is the **coordinating conjunction**, which joins things of **equal rank**: two words, two phrases, or two clauses that could each stand alone as a sentence.

There are seven, remembered as **FANBOYS**.

| Letter | Word | Relation | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| F | for | cause (formal) | *She stayed home, for she was unwell.* |
| A | and | addition | *Ria sings and Anil plays the guitar.* |
| N | nor | negative addition | *He did not call, nor did he write.* |
| B | but | contrast | *I like tea, but my brother prefers coffee.* |
| O | or | choice or warning | *Hurry, or you will miss the train.* |
| Y | yet | contrast, with surprise | *It was cheap, yet it was excellent.* |
| S | so | result | *It was cold, so we lit a fire.* |

Three habits to build.

1. **Comma before the conjunction when it joins two full clauses.** *The sun set, and the stars came out.* No comma is needed when it only joins two words or phrases: *tea and biscuits*.
2. **After nor, the helper verb comes before the subject.** *Nor did he write*, not *nor he wrote*.
3. **Use one conjunction per link.** *Although* it rained, *but* we played has two contrast words for one contrast. See the hook.

Coordinating conjunctions normally sit **between** the two things they join. You cannot move *but* to the front of the sentence and shuffle the clauses around the way you can with *although*.

## Subordinating conjunctions {lvl=1 id=s-subord label="Foundation"}

A **subordinating conjunction** joins a **main clause** to a **subordinate clause**, which depends on the main clause and cannot stand alone. The subordinate clause supplies the extra detail: when, why, how, under what condition.

*We stayed indoors* is a full sentence. *Because it was raining* is not; it leaves the reader waiting for the main idea. Put them together: *We stayed indoors because it was raining.*

| Relation | Subordinating conjunctions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Time | when, while, before, after, until, since, as soon as, whenever | *Call me as soon as you arrive.* |
| Cause | because, since, as | *Since you are free, please help me.* |
| Contrast | although, though, even though, while, whereas | *Although he is young, he is wise.* |
| Condition | if, unless, provided (that), as long as, in case | *Unless you hurry, you will be late.* |
| Purpose | so that, in order that, lest | *He saved money so that he could buy a bicycle.* |
| Result | so ... that, such ... that | *She was so tired that she fell asleep.* |
| Place | where, wherever | *Stay where you are.* |

### Order and the comma

A subordinate clause can come **first or second**.

- *Although it was raining, we played outside.* (subordinate first: comma after it)
- *We played outside although it was raining.* (subordinate second: usually no comma)

This is the great practical difference between the two kinds of conjunction. Because a subordinate clause is movable, a single sentence can be rearranged to stress different parts, and board "rewrite" questions often ask you to do exactly that.

### Fragments

If you write only the subordinate clause and put a full stop, you get a **fragment**: *We went home. Because it was late.* Fix it by joining: *We went home because it was late.*

### Unless, until and the hidden negative

*Unless* means *if ... not*. Do not add another *not* to it.

- Wrong: *Unless you do not study, you will fail.*
- Right: *Unless you study, you will fail.* (= If you do not study, you will fail.)

*Until* also works without a negative in the clause: *Wait here until I return.*

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["Unless you do not hurry, you will miss the bus.", "Unless you hurry, you will miss the bus.", "Unless you hurried, you will miss the bus."]
answer: 1
right: Right. Unless already means "if you do not", so the verb stays positive.
wrong: Unless carries the negative inside it. Adding not gives a double negative, and the past tense hurried does not suit a future warning.
:::

## Correlative conjunctions {lvl=2 id=s-pairs label="Class 10"}

Some conjunctions come in **pairs**, one half on each side of what they link. They are called **correlative conjunctions**.

| Pair | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| both ... and | two things together | *Both Mira and Kabir passed.* |
| either ... or | one of two | *You can either walk or take the bus.* |
| neither ... nor | not one, not the other | *Neither the milk nor the tea was hot.* |
| not only ... but also | addition with emphasis | *She is not only clever but also kind.* |
| whether ... or | two possibilities | *I do not know whether he will come or stay away.* |
| no sooner ... than | one event right after another | *No sooner had we sat down than the lights went out.* |
| hardly / scarcely ... when | the same idea | *Hardly had she left when it began to rain.* |

### Three rules that carry the marks

**1. Parallel structure.** What follows the first half should have the same grammatical form as what follows the second.

- Wrong: *She is not only clever but she is also kind.* (An adjective on one side, a whole clause on the other.)
- Right: *She is not only clever but also kind.* (adjective, adjective)
- Wrong: *He likes both to swim and cycling.*
- Right: *He likes both swimming and cycling.* or *He likes both to swim and to cycle.*

**2. Agreement with the nearer subject.** With either ... or and neither ... nor, the verb agrees with the subject **closest** to it.

- *Neither the captain nor the players **were** ready.*
- *Neither the players nor the captain **was** ready.*

**3. The right partner.** Neither pairs with **nor**, never with *or*; either pairs with **or**. *Neither he or I came* is wrong; *neither he nor I came* is right. No sooner pairs with **than**, never *when*; hardly pairs with **when** (or *before*), never *than*.

### Inversion after negative openers

When a sentence starts with *not only*, *no sooner*, *hardly* or *scarcely*, the helper verb jumps in front of the subject, as in a question.

- *Not only did she win, but she also set a record.*
- *No sooner had he left than it began to rain.*

The inversion is on the **first** half only. Also notice the tense: no sooner and hardly are used with the **past perfect** (had left), and the other event is in the past simple.

## Linking adverbs and transitions {lvl=2 id=s-adv label="Class 10"}

Words such as **however, therefore, moreover, besides, otherwise, nevertheless, meanwhile, thus** and phrases such as **as a result, in addition, on the other hand, for example** are not conjunctions in the strict sense. They are **adverbs or adverbial phrases** that link a sentence to the **previous** sentence or clause. They do the same job as conjunctions but are not allowed to join two clauses with a comma alone.

| Function | Linking words and phrases |
|---|---|
| Add | moreover, furthermore, besides, in addition, also, what is more |
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, still, on the other hand, in contrast, yet |
| Result | therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence, accordingly |
| Condition / warning | otherwise, in that case |
| Example | for example, for instance, in particular |
| Sequence | first, next, then, afterwards, meanwhile, finally |
| Sum up | in conclusion, to sum up, in short, overall |

### The punctuation rule

Compare:

- Wrong: *It was late, however we went on.* (This is a **comma splice**: two full sentences joined by a bare comma.)
- Right: *It was late; however, we went on.*
- Right: *It was late. However, we went on.*
- Right: *It was late, but we went on.* (A true conjunction may follow a comma.)

The pattern: **full stop or semicolon before the linking adverb, comma after it.** When however sits in the middle of its own clause, it takes a comma on each side: *We, however, went on.*

:::note title="Conjunction or linking adverb? A quick test" tone=tip
Try moving the word. A conjunction such as *but* must stay between the two clauses; you cannot say *It was late; we went on, but*. A linking adverb can wander: *We went on, however.* *However, we went on.* *We, however, went on.* If the word can move, it is a linking adverb and needs the semicolon or full stop.
:::

### Otherwise

*Otherwise* means *if not* (or *or else*) and warns of a consequence: *Take the medicine; otherwise, you will not recover.* It is a very common choice in board gap-filling when the second sentence names a bad result of ignoring the first.

:::qc
q: Which sentence is punctuated correctly?
options: ["The road was closed, therefore we turned back.", "The road was closed; therefore, we turned back.", "The road was closed therefore, we turned back."]
answer: 1
right: Right. Therefore is a linking adverb, so it needs a semicolon or full stop before it and a comma after it.
wrong: Therefore cannot join two clauses with a bare comma, and the third option has the comma in the wrong place. Use a semicolon before and a comma after.
:::

## Although, despite and their relatives {lvl=2 id=s-prep label="Class 10"}

Many linkers look interchangeable but need different grammar after them. The test is **what follows**.

| Meaning | Followed by a clause (subject + verb) | Followed by a noun or -ing form |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | although, though, even though | despite, in spite of |
| Cause | because, since, as | because of, due to, owing to |

- *Although it was raining, the match went on.* (it was raining: a clause)
- *Despite the rain, the match went on.* (the rain: a noun)
- *In spite of being injured, he finished the race.* (-ing form)
- *We were late because the bus broke down.* (clause)
- *We were late because of the breakdown.* (noun)

You can force a clause after despite with *the fact that*: *Despite the fact that it was raining, ...* That is correct but heavy; the plain *although* is usually better.

Common slips:

- *Despite of the rain* is wrong; it is **despite the rain** or **in spite of the rain**.
- *Although the rain, we played* is wrong, since although needs a clause.
- *Because the delay, we left* is wrong; use *because of the delay*.

### So that, so ... that, such ... that, in case

These four are often mixed up.

| Pattern | Idea | Example |
|---|---|---|
| so that | purpose: in order that | *She spoke slowly so that everyone could follow.* |
| so + adjective/adverb + that | result | *The bag was so heavy that I could not lift it.* |
| such a + noun + that | result | *It was such a heavy bag that I could not lift it.* |
| in case | as a precaution, because something might happen | *Take an umbrella in case it rains.* |

*In case* is not the same as *if*. *Take an umbrella if it rains* makes no sense, because you would be standing in the rain without one; *in case it rains* means you take it now, before anything happens.

### While and whereas

Both can mark contrast between two parallel facts: *Ravi is tall, whereas his sister is short.* *While* also has a time meaning (*while I was reading*), so *whereas* is clearer when contrast is intended.

## How the board tests linkers {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

Linkers turn up in several kinds of question. [Unverified: the exact mix and marks of each type in the current year's paper; check the latest sample paper.]

1. **Gap filling.** A short passage with blanks. The options are often several linkers. Name the relation between the sentence before and the sentence after the blank, then choose.
2. **Editing / error correction.** One word in a line is wrong, often a conjunction: a doubled contrast (*although ... but*), *despite of*, *neither ... or*, *unless ... not*, or a wrong relation (*therefore* where *however* is needed).
3. **Sentence joining or rewriting.** Combine two short sentences using a word you are given, or rewrite a sentence beginning with a given word, without changing the meaning.
4. **Writing tasks.** Letters, articles, notes and reports are marked in part for coherence, so well-placed linkers earn marks.

A routine that works:

- Read the sentence before the blank and the sentence after it.
- Ask: addition, contrast, cause, result, condition, time, purpose, example, or summing up?
- Choose a word of that relation.
- Check the grammar that follows: clause or noun? Comma, semicolon or full stop?
- If two linkers appear in one link, delete one.

:::worked title="Joining two sentences four ways"
Task: Join with the word given.
*The match was cancelled. It rained heavily.*

(a) **because**: *The match was cancelled because it rained heavily.* Cause is placed second.
(b) **so**: *It rained heavily, so the match was cancelled.* Cause first, result second, coordinating conjunction with a comma.
(c) **because of**: *The match was cancelled because of the heavy rain.* A noun phrase follows, so the clause had to be turned into one.
(d) **as a result**: *It rained heavily; as a result, the match was cancelled.* Linking phrase, with a semicolon before and a comma after.

The facts are identical in all four; only the structure changes. The board will tell you which word to use, so choose the matching pattern from the tables above.
:::

:::worked title="Filling a four-gap passage"
Fill the gaps with: however, because, moreover, for example.

*Many students skip breakfast (1) they feel they have no time. (2), this is a mistake. Breakfast gives the body energy for the morning, and (3), it helps students concentrate. A good breakfast does not need to be elaborate. (4), a banana and a glass of milk take only five minutes.*

(1) **because**: it gives the reason for skipping, and a clause follows. (2) **However**: the writer now goes against the habit just described, so contrast. (3) **moreover**: a second benefit is added. (4) **For example**: a banana and milk is an illustration of a simple breakfast. Each answer was found by naming the relation first, not by guessing a word.
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "Despite of the heavy traffic, he reached on time."
options: ["Despite of should be Despite", "he reached should be he had reached", "on time should be in time"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Despite is never followed by of; the long form is in spite of.
wrong: The linker is the problem. Despite already means "in spite of", so adding of is wrong.
:::

## Beyond the board: flow, register and judgement {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### Linkers cannot fix a broken argument

Moving from sentence to sentence smoothly takes more than a list of connectors. A passage with however, moreover and therefore in every line is tiring to read, and the words may not match the logic. Use a linker only where the relation needs announcing. Often a link is clear without one: *The soup was cold. I sent it back.* works because the result is obvious.

### Register: formal and informal pairs

| Informal | More formal |
|---|---|
| but | however, nevertheless |
| so | therefore, consequently |
| also | moreover, furthermore, in addition |
| about | regarding, concerning |
| if not | otherwise |
| anyway | in any case |

In a letter to a friend, *but* and *so* are natural. In a formal letter, a report or a speech, the right-hand column suits better. The mistake is not choosing one or the other; it is mixing them in the same piece without reason.

### Words that serve two jobs

Some linkers have more than one meaning, and context decides.

- **Since**: time (*since Monday*) or cause (*Since you are here, help me*).
- **As**: time (*as I walked*), cause (*as it was late*) or comparison (*as tall as*).
- **While**: time (*while I slept*) or contrast (*while he is rich, he is unhappy*).
- **Yet**: contrast (*cheap yet good*) or time (*not yet*).

If a sentence using *since* or *as* could be read in two ways, rewrite with *because* or *when* to avoid ambiguity.

### Starting a sentence with And or But

Many schoolteachers say never to begin a sentence with *and* or *but*. Careful modern writers do it for emphasis and rhythm, and published usage guides generally allow it. [Unverified: the stance of any particular board marking scheme.] For exam answers, the safe choice is to join clauses with these words or use a linking adverb, and keep a sentence-opening *But* for creative writing, if at all.

### Linking across sentences without a conjunction

Cohesion also comes from **repetition and reference**, not only from linkers.

- A pronoun picks up an earlier noun: *The committee met. It decided to wait.*
- A relative pronoun joins a clause to a noun: *The book that I borrowed was torn.*
- A summary word points back: *The river flooded, the bridge collapsed and the road closed. This chain of events cut off the village.*
- *The former* and *the latter* refer back to two things just named.

A well-linked paragraph uses all of these, with explicit linkers only at the main turning points: a contrast, a consequence, a conclusion.

### Sequencing in long answers

For an essay or a long answer, signpost the structure: *First, ... Secondly, ... Finally, ...* for steps, and *On the one hand, ... on the other hand, ...* for two sides. Do not open every paragraph with *Firstly*; vary the openers and let the content carry the order.

:::qc
q: "Take your coat ___ it gets cold later." (a precaution)
options: ["in case", "if", "unless", "although"]
answer: 0
right: Right. In case means "because it might happen", so you take the coat now, before the cold arrives.
wrong: The speaker takes the coat as a precaution, before anything has happened. That is in case, not if.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A linker tells the reader how one idea relates to the next. Learn the relations (addition, contrast, cause, result, condition, time, purpose, example, summing up), then the words. {lvl=1}
2. Coordinating conjunctions (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) join items of equal rank, sit between them and take a comma before them when joining two clauses. {lvl=1}
3. Subordinating conjunctions (because, although, if, unless, when, so that and others) introduce a dependent clause that can come first or second; a lone subordinate clause is a fragment. {lvl=1}
4. One relation needs one linker: not "although ... but", not "because ... so". {lvl=1}
5. Unless means if not; never add another not to it. {lvl=1}
6. Correlative pairs must be matched correctly (neither ... nor, either ... or, not only ... but also, no sooner ... than, hardly ... when) and what follows each half must have the same form. {lvl=2}
7. With either ... or and neither ... nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject. {lvl=2}
8. Linking adverbs (however, therefore, moreover, otherwise) need a full stop or semicolon before and a comma after, never a bare comma before. {lvl=2}
9. Although / though + clause; despite / in spite of + noun or -ing; because + clause; because of + noun. Never "despite of". {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: use linkers only where the relation needs announcing; match the register; watch words with two jobs (since, as, while); build flow with pronouns and reference as well. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "Although it rained, but we played": two contrast words for one contrast; use either although or but. {lvl=1}
- "Because he was ill, so he stayed home": the same error with cause and result. {lvl=1}
- "Unless you do not study": unless already means if not. {lvl=1}
- A subordinate clause punctuated as a sentence ("Because it was late."): a fragment; join it to a main clause. {lvl=1}
- Comma splice: "It was late, however we went on". Use a semicolon or full stop before however, and a comma after. {lvl=2}
- "Neither he or I": neither takes nor; either takes or. {lvl=2}
- "She is not only clever but she is also kind": keep the structures parallel (not only clever but also kind). {lvl=2}
- "Despite of", "Although the rain", "because the delay": match the linker to what follows (clause or noun). {lvl=2}
- "No sooner ... when" and "hardly ... than": the partners are no sooner ... than and hardly ... when. {lvl=2}
- Filling a blank by guessing a familiar word without naming the relation between the two sentences. {lvl=2}
- Stacking however, moreover and therefore in every sentence: use them where the relation needs announcing. {lvl=3}
- Using since or as when the meaning could be time or cause: rewrite with when or because. {lvl=3}
:::
