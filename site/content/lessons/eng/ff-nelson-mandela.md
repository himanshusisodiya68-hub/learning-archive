## What the extract is about {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

The extract is from Nelson Mandela's autobiography, *Long Walk to Freedom*. It describes a single day, **10 May 1994**, when he was sworn in as the first president of a South Africa elected by all its people. The book's opening note explains the background: apartheid separated people by race, Mandela and his party, the African National Congress, fought it for a lifetime, and in 1994 the first democratic elections made him the first black president.

Mandela tells the day in three movements.

1. **The ceremony.** The inauguration takes place in the sandstone amphitheatre of the Union Buildings in Pretoria, a place that was for decades the seat of white rule and is now the site of a gathering of many nations. His daughter Zenani accompanies him. F. W. de Klerk is sworn in as second deputy president and Thabo Mbeki as first, and then Mandela promises to uphold the Constitution. His short address thanks the guests and sets two aims: to free all people from poverty, suffering and discrimination, and to ensure that no one ever again oppresses another in this country.
2. **The display.** Jets, helicopters and troop carriers fly over in perfect order. The highest generals salute him. The two national anthems are sung, whites singing the black anthem and blacks singing the old anthem of the Republic.
3. **The reflection.** Mandela looks back across the century, thanks the generation of freedom fighters who made the day possible, and says what he learnt from them: courage, the natural tendency to love rather than hate, and the belief that freedom belongs to everyone. He closes with his own story of how a free boyhood turned into a hunger for his people's freedom.

## Key terms and apartheid {lvl=1 id=s-terms label="Foundation"}

You cannot read this chapter without knowing what the word **apartheid** means. It was a political system in South Africa that divided people by race and gave the white minority power over everyone else. Mandela dates its roots to the first decade of the twentieth century, when white South Africans settled their differences after the Anglo-Boer war and built a system of racial domination against the dark-skinned majority. By 1994, the book's news item says, the new government was installed after more than three centuries of white rule.

## Freedom, courage and the oppressor {lvl=2 id=s-themes label="Class 10"}

**Freedom grows.** Mandela traces how his idea of freedom matured in three steps.

| Stage | What freedom meant to him | Kind of freedom |
|---|---|---|
| Boyhood in the village | Running in the fields, swimming in the stream, roasting corn, riding bulls, as long as he obeyed his father and tribal custom | A real but childish freedom he calls an illusion |
| Student | Staying out at night, reading what he pleased, going where he chose | **Transitory** freedoms, only for himself |
| Young man in Johannesburg | Achieving his potential, earning his keep, marrying and having a family, not being obstructed in a lawful life | Basic and **honourable** freedoms |
| Member of the ANC | The freedom of his whole people to live with dignity and self-respect | A freedom that cannot be divided |

The turn comes when he sees that others who looked like him were also not free. This turns his hunger for his own freedom into a greater hunger for the freedom of his people. That hunger changes him completely: it takes a frightened young man and makes him bold, makes a law-abiding lawyer into a so-called criminal, a family-loving husband into a man without a home, and a life-loving man into someone who lived like a monk.

**Twin obligations.** A person has duties to family and also to community. In a humane society both can be met. In apartheid South Africa he could not do both, because serving his people meant leaving his family and living in secrecy.

**Courage.** He learnt it from comrades who risked their lives for an idea and stood up to torture. His definition separates courage from fearlessness: you can only be brave if you are afraid.

**Love and hate.** Nobody is born hating another person for colour, background or religion; hatred is taught, so love, which comes more naturally to the human heart, can also be taught. Even in prison a glimmer of humanity in a guard was enough to keep him going.

**The oppressor.** Mandela argues that the oppressor is not free: a man who takes away another's freedom is locked behind the bars of prejudice and narrow-mindedness. Both sides are robbed of their humanity, so liberation must include the oppressor too.

right: Yes. He says people must learn to hate, so they can be taught to love, because love comes more naturally to the human heart than its opposite.

## "Depths of oppression" and "heights of character" {lvl=2 id=s-depths label="Class 10"}

This is the idea students most often misread. Mandela is not saying that oppression is good, and he calls apartheid a deep and lasting wound that will take many years, if not generations, to heal. His point is more careful. The decades of cruelty had an **unintended effect**: they produced leaders of extraordinary courage, wisdom and generosity, whom he names (Oliver Tambo, Walter Sisulu, Chief Luthuli, Yusuf Dadoo, Bram Fischer, Robert Sobukwe). He then adds that his country's greatest wealth is not its minerals and gems but its people, "finer and truer than the purest diamonds".

To argue about this idea in a board answer, use three moves.
1. **State it fairly.** Mandela links great suffering and great character, without praising the suffering.
2. **Give his evidence.** The patriots who endured torture without breaking, and a people who held on to hope.
3. **Add an example of your own.** The freedom struggle in India produced figures such as Gandhi and Ambedkar, who faced discrimination and imprisonment and went on to shape the nation [the examples are general knowledge, not from the chapter].

You may also add a caution: oppression does not automatically create heroes. It can also break people, and those who endured it deserve credit for rising above it.

## "The" with names: the Oliver Tambos {lvl=2 id=s-the label="Class 10"}

**Why the rule exists.** A proper noun already points to one unique person, so English does not normally add *the* or a plural: we do not say *the Nelson Mandela* or *Nelson Mandelas*. A noun gets *the* when we need to pick out which one from many, and a plural when there are several.

**Why Mandela breaks it.** When he writes that apartheid produced "the Oliver Tambos, the Walter Sisulus", he is using each name as a **type**. The name becomes shorthand for a quality: courage and leadership. So *the Oliver Tambos* means many other men like Oliver Tambo, some less famous. The text's own examples show the two uses.

| Sentence | What the phrase means |
|---|---|
| Mr Singh invites the Amitabh Bachchans and the Shah Rukh Khans to his parties. | film stars of that calibre, people like them |
| Madhuri Dixit is the Madhubala of our times. | the person in our age who plays the role that Madhubala played in hers |
| History is the story of the Alexanders, the Napoleons and the Hitlers, but also of ordinary people. | rulers and conquerors of that type |

:::note title="How to use it" tone=tip
Use this device with names whose qualities everyone knows. Said aloud, it works like a quick comparison: "the Gandhis and the Ambedkars of our day" means people of their moral weight. In a board essay, one such phrase is enough; do not overuse it.
:::

:::qc
q: In "the Oliver Tambos", the phrase means
options: ["Oliver Tambo and a few named friends", "many men of the same kind as Oliver Tambo", "several people who share the name Oliver Tambo"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The name is used as a type, so the phrase means people of his kind, many of whom may be less well known.
wrong: It is not about the name itself. Think of the quality that the name stands for.
:::

## Nouns from verbs {lvl=2 id=s-nouns label="Class 10"}

**First principle.** A verb names an action (*govern*). To talk about the action as a thing, a topic or a result, we need a noun (*government*). English makes that noun by adding an ending to the verb, most often **-ation** or **-ment**. The base word may change a little.

| Verb | Noun | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| inaugurate | inauguration | drop -e, add -ion |
| install | installation | add -ation |
| form | formation | add -ation |
| govern | government | add -ment |
| dominate | domination | drop -e, add -ion |
| oppress | oppression | add -ion |
| emancipate | emancipation | drop -e, add -ion |
| deprive | deprivation | change -ive to -ivation |
| discriminate | discrimination | drop -e, add -ion |
| achieve | achievement | add -ment |
| demonstrate | demonstration | drop -e, add -ion |
| rebel | rebellion | double l, add -ion |
| constitute | constitution | drop -e, add -ion |

**Tip.** If you are unsure, look at the nearby noun in the chapter. If a verb ends in *-ate* the noun almost always ends in *-ation*. If it ends in *-ment* in English, the noun is ready-made. Check spelling in a dictionary when the base changes (for example *subjugate*, *subjugation*).

:::qc
q: What is the noun form of "assist"?
options: ["assistment", "assistance", "assisation"]
answer: 1
right: Yes, assistance. Not all verbs follow -ation or -ment. Some use -ance or -ence (assist, assistance; resist, resistance).
wrong: There are other noun endings besides -ation and -ment. Which ending fits assist?
:::

## Reading the contrasts {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

Mandela's prose runs on **balance**. Most important sentences have two halves that answer each other, and the words that join them show you where the contrast lies.

| Contrast | Halves | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Place | for decades the seat of white supremacy / now a rainbow gathering | now |
| Military | would once have arrested him / now salute him | not so many years before |
| Courage | not the absence of fear / the triumph over it | not ... but |
| The brave man | not he who does not feel afraid / he who conquers that fear | not ... but |
| Oppression | depths of oppression / heights of character | depths, heights (opposites) |
| Hatred | people learn to hate / can be taught to love | if ... they can |
| Boyhood | born free / later hungry for freedom | but, only when |

**Why this style works.** A balanced sentence feels fair and settled, as if each claim has been weighed. It also makes the sentence easy to remember, which suits a speech and a life story. The reader hears the past and present side by side, so progress is visible without being claimed.

**Voice.** The extract is first-person autobiography, so we hear Mandela's own feelings: awe at the flypast, pain that the earlier patriots cannot see the result, humility in "simply the sum". The inaugural speech inside it is formal and public, with a ringing repetition ("Never, never, and never again"), while the surrounding narration is quieter and reflective. The shift from public speech to private memory makes the day feel both national and personal.

**Rhetorical devices to name in an answer.**
- *Repetition* for emphasis: never, never, and never again.
- *Antithesis*: courage is not the absence of fear but the triumph over it.
- *Metaphor*: man's goodness is a flame that can be hidden but never extinguished; the chains on one are the chains on all.
- *Imagery*: the "rainbow gathering" of nations, and the new flag drawn by jets in smoke.

:::qc
q: Which device is used in "courage was not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it"?
options: ["Antithesis, a balanced contrast", "Onomatopoeia", "Alliteration only"]
answer: 0
right: Correct. The sentence sets two opposite ideas in matching form, joined by not ... but.
wrong: The sentence puts two opposed ideas side by side in a balanced shape. What is that device called?
:::

## Writing about freedom and prejudice {lvl=3 id=s-writing label="Beyond the board"}

Two exercises in the chapter ask you to speak and write about modern issues, which is where a Class 10 reader can go beyond the extract.

**For a short speech.** Mandela's own pattern is a good model: open with a vivid fact, state the big idea, give two or three supporting points, and close on a firm call. For the topic that true liberty is freedom from poverty, deprivation and all forms of discrimination, your points could be causes of poverty, discrimination by gender, religion or class, and the human rights guaranteed in a constitution.

**For the opinion paragraph.** The exercise asks whether there is colour prejudice in our own country and invites a humorous tone. A short verse in the book makes fun of the word "coloured" by listing the many colours a person turns during life. The best answers keep to 100 to 150 words, give one or two concrete examples (such as the fairness-cream advertisement or a matrimonial advertisement), and end with a sentence that links back to the chapter's belief that prejudice is learnt, so it can be unlearnt.

**What to avoid.** Do not copy phrases from the chapter into a long quote. Use a short phrase with a comment of your own. Do not compare countries unfairly or name individuals critically; keep to ideas and examples.

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not say Mandela was angry or revengeful on this day; the extract shows hope, humility and forgiveness. {lvl=1}
- Do not mix up the roles: de Klerk was sworn in as second deputy president and Mbeki as first. {lvl=1}
- Do not write that oppression was good because it produced great people. Mandela calls it a profound hurt with an unintended effect. {lvl=2}

- "The Oliver Tambos" is not a list of men named Oliver Tambo; it stands for people of his kind. {lvl=2}
