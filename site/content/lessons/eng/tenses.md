## Why the verb carries time {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Some languages never change a verb for time; you say "yesterday I go" and the word "yesterday" does the work. English puts the time on the verb itself. Compare:

- Asha **walks** to school.
- Asha **walked** to school.
- Asha **will walk** to school.

Nothing else in the sentence changed, yet the message did. This matters because a verb is the one word every sentence needs, so the time is never lost or left out. That is also why one wrong verb form is such a visible mistake: the reader hears the timeline break.

## Two questions behind every tense {lvl=1 id=s-two label="Foundation"}

A tense answers two separate questions about the action.

1. **When?** Present, past or future. This is the row of the table.
2. **What shape?** Is the action seen as a plain fact (**simple**), as in progress (**continuous**), as finished with a result that matters (**perfect**), or as in progress over a stretch of time up to a point (**perfect continuous**). This is the column.

Three times multiplied by four shapes gives twelve tenses.

| | Simple | Continuous | Perfect | Perfect continuous |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Present** | V1 (+s/es): *Neha plays chess.* | am/is/are + V-ing: *Neha is playing chess now.* | has/have + V3: *Neha has played chess since morning.* | has/have been + V-ing: *Neha has been playing chess for an hour.* |
| **Past** | V2: *Karan painted the gate.* | was/were + V-ing: *Karan was painting the gate at noon.* | had + V3: *Karan had painted the gate before the rain.* | had been + V-ing: *Karan had been painting for three hours when it rained.* |
| **Future** | will + V1: *Mira will visit the fair.* | will be + V-ing: *Mira will be visiting the fair at six.* | will have + V3: *Mira will have visited the fair by Sunday.* | will have been + V-ing: *Mira will have been visiting the fair for a week by then.* |

(V1 is the base verb, V2 the past form, V3 the past participle: go, went, gone.)

:::note title="Reading the table" tone=tip
Spot the pattern in the helpers. Continuous always has a form of **be** + **-ing**. Perfect always has a form of **have** + **V3**. Perfect continuous has both: **have been** + **-ing**. Only the helper changes for time: has/have, had, will have.
:::

:::qc
q: Which tense is "The students will have been studying for two hours by noon"?
options: ["Future perfect continuous", "Future perfect", "Future continuous", "Present perfect continuous"]
answer: 0
right: Right. It has will have been + V-ing, so it is the future perfect continuous.
wrong: Count the helpers. "will have been" holds both have (perfect) and been (continuous), and will puts it in the future.
:::

## The simple tenses {lvl=1 id=s-simple label="Foundation"}

**Present simple** (V1, add -s/-es for he, she, it) states facts and habits.

- Facts and general truths: *The Sun rises in the east. Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius.*
- Habits and routines: *Ravi walks his dog every morning.*
- Timetables and schedules: *The train leaves at 6.15.*

Signal words: always, usually, often, sometimes, never, every day, on Sundays.

**Past simple** (V2) states an action completed at a definite time in the past.

- *We visited the fort last winter.* *She did not call yesterday.*
- A string of past events: *He opened the door, looked out and shut it again.*

Signal words: yesterday, last week, in 1998, two days ago, then, once.

**Future simple** (will + V1) states a decision made now, a prediction, a promise or an offer.

- *I will carry that bag for you.* *It will rain by evening.*

Signal words: tomorrow, next month, soon, in a few days, probably.

Questions and negatives use **do/does/did** with the base verb, and the main verb loses its ending: *Does she play? She did not go.* Not "Did she went?"

## The continuous tenses {lvl=1 id=s-cont label="Foundation"}

Continuous tenses show an action **in progress** at a moment: unfinished, temporary, happening around that time.

- **Present continuous**: *Look! The cat is climbing the tree.* Also a temporary situation (*I am staying with my uncle this week*) and a fixed plan (*We are leaving tomorrow*).
- **Past continuous**: an action in progress at a past moment, or the background to a shorter event: *It was raining when we left.* Two actions in progress together: *Dad was cooking while I was studying.*
- **Future continuous**: in progress at a future moment: *At nine tomorrow I will be writing my exam.*

Signal words: now, at the moment, at present, while, when (with a shorter action), at 5 o'clock yesterday.

### Stative verbs do not take the continuous

Some verbs describe a state, not an action you can watch unfolding: know, believe, understand, like, love, hate, want, need, prefer, own, belong, seem, see (in the sense of "perceive"), hear. They stay in the simple form.

- Wrong: *I am knowing the answer.* Right: *I know the answer.*
- Wrong: *She is wanting a new bag.* Right: *She wants a new bag.*

Some verbs have both a state meaning and an action meaning. *I think he is honest* (opinion, simple) but *I am thinking about my holidays* (an activity, continuous). *The soup tastes good* but *She is tasting the soup.*

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["Do you understand the rule?", "Are you understanding the rule?", "Do you understanding the rule?"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Understand is a stative verb, so it takes the simple present, and the question uses do.
wrong: Understand describes a state of mind, not an action in progress. Use the simple form with do.
:::

## The perfect tenses {lvl=2 id=s-perfect label="Class 10"}

The perfect shape looks **back from a point** (now, a past moment or a future moment) at something already completed. The point is the key, not the action.

### Present perfect (has/have + V3)

It connects the past to **now**. It is used when the result matters now, or the period of time is not finished, or the exact time is not given or does not matter.

- Result now: *I have lost my pen* (so I do not have it).
- Experience up to now: *Have you ever seen a rhino?* *She has never flown.*
- Unfinished period: *I have read two books this week* (the week is not over).
- Recent news: *The bus has just left.*

Signal words: just, already, yet, ever, never, so far, recently, lately, this week, since, for.

:::note title="The classic board trap" tone=warn
A present perfect cannot go with a **finished-time word**: yesterday, last year, in 2015, two days ago, when (as a question about time). The moment you name a finished time, the link to now is cut, so use the past simple.

*She has gone to Agra yesterday.* is wrong. *She went to Agra yesterday.* is right. *She has gone to Agra.* is also right (no time named).
:::

### Since and for

Both measure how long a state or action has lasted, and both take a perfect form.

| Word | Followed by | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| since | a point in time (a date, a day, an event) | since 2019, since Monday, since I was six |
| for | a length of time | for five years, for two hours, for a long time |

*They have lived here **since** 2012. They have lived here **for** twelve years.* Never use the present simple with these: *I know him since 2015* is wrong; *I have known him since 2015* is right.

### Past perfect (had + V3)

It shows the **earlier** of two past events. It only makes sense when a second, later past moment is in view.

- *When I reached the station, the train had left.* (leaving came first)
- *She told me she had already eaten.*

Signal words: before, after, by the time, already, when (earlier action). If only one past event is being told, use the past simple, not the past perfect.

### Future perfect (will have + V3)

An action that will be **completed before** a future point: *By next June, I will have finished Class 10.* Signal words: by, by then, by the time.

:::qc
q: "I ___ this film three times, so I will not watch it again."
options: ["have seen", "saw", "am seeing", "had seen"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The count of experiences up to now, with no finished-time word, takes the present perfect.
wrong: No finished time is named, and the speaker is summing up experience up to now. That is the present perfect.
:::

:::qc
q: "She ___ for the exam since January."
options: ["is preparing", "prepares", "has been preparing", "will prepare"]
answer: 2
right: Right. Since January points to a past start and the preparation continues, so the present perfect continuous fits.
wrong: Since + a past starting point needs a perfect form. Preparation is still going on, so use has been preparing.
:::

## The perfect continuous tenses {lvl=2 id=s-pc label="Class 10"}

These stress the **duration** of an action up to a point, often with an effect still visible.

- **Present perfect continuous**: *My eyes are red because I have been reading all night.* *He has been waiting for forty minutes.*
- **Past perfect continuous**: *The ground was wet; it had been raining.* *We had been walking for two hours when the car stopped.*
- **Future perfect continuous**: *By May, Dad will have been working here for twenty years.*

Present perfect or present perfect continuous? Both are possible for "how long" (*I have lived / have been living here for years*). Use the **simple** perfect when the result or the count matters (*I have written five letters*) and the **continuous** perfect when the activity and its length matter (*I have been writing letters all morning*). Stative verbs stay simple: *I have known her for years*, not *have been knowing*.

## Sequence of tenses and linked clauses {lvl=2 id=s-seq label="Class 10"}

When a sentence has two clauses, their tenses must tell one consistent story.

### Narration: keep one time frame

If a story starts in the past, the main events stay in the past, and anything earlier takes the past perfect.

- Wrong: *Yesterday Tara goes to the market. She buys fruit and then meets a friend.*
- Right: *Yesterday Tara went to the market. She bought fruit and then met a friend.*
- With an earlier event: *She went home after she had bought the fruit.*

### Will after if and time words

In clauses beginning with **if, unless, when, before, after, as soon as, until**, the future is expressed by the **present simple**. The main clause carries the will.

- Wrong: *If it will rain, we will cancel the match.* Right: *If it rains, we will cancel the match.*
- Wrong: *I will call you when I will reach.* Right: *I will call you when I reach.*

Past counterparts: *If I had known, I would have told you* (past perfect in the if-clause, would have in the other).

### Reported speech

When the reporting verb is in the past (said, told, asked), the reported words shift back one step.

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| "I am tired." | He said that he was tired. |
| "I work here." | She said that she worked there. |
| "I have finished." | He said that he had finished. |
| "I went home." | She said that she had gone home. |
| "I will help." | He said that he would help. |

Exception: a universal truth does not shift. *The teacher said that the Earth moves round the Sun.*

## Tenses in the board's questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

The board paper tests tenses in three ways.

1. **Gap filling with the verb in brackets.** A short passage, usually with the verb given in its base form. Find the anchor words first, then decide the time and shape of each verb. Check that the passage keeps one story: if the first gap is past, the others are usually past or past perfect.
2. **Gap filling with options (multiple choice).** Eliminate options that break a hard rule: a perfect with a finished-time word, a continuous with a stative verb, will after if, a missing helper.
3. **Editing or error correction.** Each line holds at most one error, usually a wrong tense, the wrong form of V2 and V3, or a helper missing. Read the whole passage once for its time frame, then check each line against it.

A routine that works:

- Mark every time word (yesterday, since, by the time).
- Decide: finished or in progress? Before or after another event?
- Write the verb with its helpers, then check subject-verb agreement (*has*/*have*, *was*/*were*).
- Read the filled passage aloud in your head. A wrong tense usually sounds wrong.

:::worked title="A five-gap passage in thirty seconds"
Fill the gaps: *Last week my uncle (1) (take) us to the zoo. By the time we (2) (arrive), the gates (3) (already, open) and many families (4) (queue) at the counter. We (5) (stand) in line when it began to drizzle.*

Anchor: "Last week" makes the story past. (1) took, a plain past event. (2) arrived, in a by the time clause, which names the later past moment. (3) had already opened, which happened before we arrived. (4) were queuing, in progress at that moment. (5) were standing, in progress when the drizzle began.
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "He has bought this phone last month."
options: ["has bought should be bought", "this should be that", "last month should be the last month"]
answer: 0
right: Right. "Last month" is a finished time, so the past simple bought is needed instead of the present perfect.
wrong: Look at "last month". A finished-time word cannot go with the present perfect.
:::

## Beyond the board: shades of time {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

The twelve forms are the framework, but writers bend them to colour the story.

### Historic present

Narrators sometimes tell past events in the present simple to make them vivid: *In 1947, India wakes to freedom. Nehru rises to speak.* You see it in commentary (*Tendulkar steps down the pitch and drives*), news headlines (*Government announces new scheme*) and plot summaries. In an exam answer, stay in the past unless the question asks for a summary, where the present is the convention.

### Future in the past

When you look forward from a past moment, will turns into **would**: *She promised that she would write.* *He knew he would win.* The perfect form is would have: *By then, he would have left.* This is the same backshift you met in reported speech.

### Was going to

*Was/were going to + V1* is the plan or expectation that did not come true (or is not yet known to): *I was going to call you, but my phone died.* *It looked as if the roof was going to fall.* A parallel is *was about to*. Contrast with *I called you*, which simply happened.

### Present forms for the future

English often uses present tenses for the future when something is fixed.

- Present simple for a timetable: *The exam starts at 9.*
- Present continuous for an arrangement: *We are meeting at four.*
- *Will* for a decision made now; *be going to* for a plan already made or a prediction from present evidence: *Look at those clouds. It is going to rain.*

### Will not always means the future

*Will* also expresses habits and assumptions: *That will be the postman.* (I assume it is.) *Oil will float on water.* (It always does.) Reading for these shades is useful in comprehension passages even though the board rarely asks for them directly.

:::qc
q: "I ___ you, but the phone was switched off." (a plan that did not happen)
options: ["was going to call", "am going to call", "will call", "have called"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Was going to marks a past intention that did not happen.
wrong: The phone was switched off, so the plan failed, and the plan itself belonged to the past. That is was going to.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A tense answers two questions: when (present, past, future) and what shape (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous). Twelve tenses result. {lvl=1}
2. Simple = fact, habit or finished event; continuous = be + V-ing, in progress; perfect = have + V3, completed and looking back; perfect continuous = have been + V-ing, duration up to a point. {lvl=1}
3. Stative verbs (know, like, want, understand, belong) do not take the continuous. {lvl=1}
4. Present perfect links the past to now; never use it with yesterday, last year, in 2015 or ago. {lvl=2}
5. Since + a point in time; for + a length of time; both need a perfect form. {lvl=2}
6. Past perfect shows the earlier of two past events. {lvl=2}
7. After if, unless, when, before, after, as soon as and until, use the present simple for the future, not will. {lvl=2}
8. In reported speech after a past reporting verb, tenses shift back one step; universal truths do not. {lvl=2}
9. Narration keeps one time frame; only an earlier event steps back to the past perfect. {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: historic present, would for future in the past, was going to for a plan that failed, present tenses for timetables and arrangements. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "I am knowing", "she is wanting": stative verbs stay simple. {lvl=1}
- "Did she went?" After did, the verb is the base form: "Did she go?" {lvl=1}
- Forgetting -s/-es with he, she, it in the present simple: "She play" should be "She plays". {lvl=1}
- Present perfect with a finished-time word ("has gone yesterday"): use the past simple. {lvl=2}
- Using the present simple with since or for ("I live here since 2015"): use the present perfect or present perfect continuous. {lvl=2}
- "If it will rain", "when he will come": use the present simple in the if or when clause. {lvl=2}
- Using the past perfect when only one past event is told: it needs a later past point. {lvl=2}
- Switching tense mid-story without reason: stay in one time frame unless an earlier or later event demands a shift. {lvl=2}
- Not backshifting in reported speech after "said" when the speech is not a universal truth: "He said he is tired" should be "was tired". {lvl=2}
- Heavy use of the historic present in a formal answer: keep the past unless writing a summary. {lvl=3}
:::
