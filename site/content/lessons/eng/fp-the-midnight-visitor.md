## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

A young writer named Fowler has wanted to meet a real secret agent. He is taken to the hotel room of Ausable, who turns out to be fat, wheezy and American-sounding, and who has led him through a dull evening at a music hall. Fowler feels let down: this is nothing like the stories. Ausable guesses his disappointment and explains that a very important paper, one that people have risked their lives for, is about to reach him that night. Then he switches on the light.

A slim man with a fox-like face, Max, is already in the room, holding a small pistol. Ausable pretends only to be annoyed. He says Max has got in by the balcony, and complains that this is the second time in a month. Max says he used a passkey and knew nothing about a balcony. Ausable gives a long, irritated description of it: the room was once part of a bigger set of rooms, the balcony belongs to the next apartment and runs under his window, and anyone can reach it from an empty room two doors down.

Max orders both men to sit and wait for the report, which is expected at twelve-thirty, thirty-one minutes away. Then there is a knock. Ausable says it is the police, whom he has asked to check on him. Max panics. He climbs onto the sill to hide on the "balcony" until they leave, and drops. He screams once. The door opens, and it is only a waiter, Henry, with a drink Ausable had ordered. Fowler is stunned. Ausable explains calmly that there never was any police, and there is no balcony. Max has fallen from the sixth floor.

:::qc
q: Which of these did Ausable actually have in his room that night?
options: ["Police officers waiting outside", "A balcony under the window", "A waiter he was expecting, with a drink"]
answer: 2
right: Correct. Both the police and the balcony were invented. The only real thing at the door was Henry, whom Ausable was expecting.
wrong: Check the ending. Ausable says there were no police and that there is no balcony. Which person at the door was real?
:::

## Characters {lvl=2 id=s-chars label="Class 10 board"}

| Character | What we learn | Evidence in the story |
|---|---|---|
| Ausable | Calm, observant, witty, resourceful; underestimated because of his looks | He is "very fat" and wheezy, but never raises his voice; he keeps grumbling about the management as if nothing serious is happening; he times his lie perfectly |
| Max | Armed, confident and ruthless, but over-eager and easy to deceive | He has a pistol and a "fox-like" face; he gets angry at the knock; he believes a stranger's description of a balcony he did not know about |
| Fowler | Young, romantic, naive, quickly frightened | He wants adventure and is bored, then terrified; he jumps at the knock and cannot follow what has happened until the end |
| Henry | A waiter who arrives at the right moment | He brings the drink ordered for Ausable's return; he never knows he has been used in a plan |

Notice the pairings. Fowler expects glamour and finds an ordinary man, and Max expects a fat, helpless victim and finds a trap. Both have judged by appearance, and Ausable has used that mistake to win.

## How the trick works {lvl=2 id=s-trick label="Class 10 board"}

NCERT asks how Ausable makes Max believe in the balcony and what makes the story convincing. Here is the sequence, step by step.

| Step | What Ausable does | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Acts mildly surprised, not frightened, when he sees Max | A frightened man would look as if he has something to hide; a calm one looks harmless |
| 2 | Says Max must have come in "through that nuisance of a balcony" | The remark sounds like an annoyed hotel guest's complaint, not a plan |
| 3 | Gives a full history: the room was once part of a larger unit, the neighbouring room had the balcony, the balcony runs under his window, and it can be reached from an empty room two doors down | Real events have a history and a location. Specific detail makes a lie sound like memory |
| 4 | Says it happened last month and the management promised to block it | Adds a second, ordinary fact that Max cannot check |
| 5 | Mentions the police just before the knock | The knock now looks like proof of the story; Max has seconds to decide |
| 6 | Tells Max the door is unlocked and the police "will not hesitate to shoot" | Max has two bad choices; the window feels like the safer one |
| 7 | Lets Max step out | The lie asks Max to choose, not to be told what to do |

Why does Max believe it? He is already angry and rushed; he cannot see outside because the night "pressed blackly" against the window; and the lie explains something he did not know (that someone could have entered another way). A story that gives a person information they lack is easier to accept.

:::qc
q: Which feature of Ausable's description of the balcony makes it most convincing?
options: ["It is short and dramatic", "It is detailed and delivered as an annoyed complaint", "It is whispered, so that Fowler cannot hear"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. Plenty of ordinary detail, offered as a grumble, sounds like memory. A dramatic or secret manner would have sounded like a performance.
wrong: Look again at the way Ausable talks. He complains about the management, explains where the balcony is and says how a thief could reach it. What kind of voice is that?
:::

## Planned or improvised? {lvl=2 id=s-plan label="Class 10 board"}

NCERT asks when Ausable thought up his plan: from the start, or in response to events. The text lets us argue both sides, and a good answer says which is stronger and why.

| Evidence for a plan made in advance | Evidence for quick improvisation |
|---|---|
| He has ordered a drink "for when you returned", so Henry's knock arrives on time | He wheezes "you gave me quite a start", which suggests real surprise at Max |
| The room is on the sixth and top floor, and its only window is a single ordinary one | He asks how Max learned of the report, so he did not expect an intruder specifically |
| The balcony speech is long and smooth, as though known in advance | He needs only the knock and the sill to complete the trick |

**One possible answer.** Ausable did not plan this exact evening, but he had prepared his mind. He knew the report was valuable and had tried to protect it. When Max appeared, he saw at once that the knock, the window and the height could be joined into one plan, and he made it up as events unfolded. That is presence of mind: quick thinking built on earlier preparation.

## Theme: presence of mind {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10 board"}

The book defines **presence of mind** as the ability to think quickly and to act calmly and wisely in danger or surprise. Ausable shows all three parts. He thinks quickly (he links the knock to the police), acts calmly (he stays seated and grumbles) and acts wisely (he does not fight a man with a pistol).

The Talk about it exercise adds an important point: presence of mind comes from a mind that is already prepared. Someone who has thought through what to do in a kitchen fire or a choking emergency has a better chance of acting well when it happens. Ausable's habit, ordering a drink to arrive at a set time and expecting a waiter, shows that the unexciting preparation counts as much as the brilliant moment.

**A second theme: do not judge by appearance.** Fowler first dismisses Ausable as unglamorous, and Max underestimates him. Both mistakes are corrected by the ending. The story gently laughs at the film-style picture of a spy and offers a more realistic one: a patient, ordinary-looking person who notices things.

:::qc
q: According to the exercise, where does presence of mind come from?
options: ["Pure luck on the day", "Being physically strong", "A mind already prepared by thinking about possible dangers"]
answer: 2
right: Right. The exercise says that if you have thought about possible problems and how to act, you have a better chance in a real emergency.
wrong: Look at the second Talk about it exercise. It says presence of mind comes out of a state of mental preparedness.
:::

## The author's craft {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Misdirection.** The story first points us at the wrong thing. It spends its opening on Fowler's disappointment and Ausable's looks, so that we, like Fowler, think this is a story about a dull spy. The real action then comes from the side. The balcony speech is itself misdirection, since it keeps Max looking at the window while the knock does the work.

**Suspense and pace.** The setting is a single room and about half an hour, and the countdown ("thirty-one minutes") sets a clock. Short, plain sentences ("The doorknob turned.") speed up the final seconds. The reader's heart is with Fowler, who understands nothing.

**Irony.** There are three layers.
1. *Situational:* the man who seems helpless is the one in control.
2. *Verbal:* Ausable tells Max the police "will not hesitate to shoot", but it is the fall, not the police, that ends Max.
3. *Dramatic for the second reading:* once we know the truth, every earlier line (the complaint about management, the "extra protection") reads as a deliberate move.

**Twist ending and understatement.** The last line, that there is no balcony, is short and quiet. The narrator does not explain the trick or dwell on Max's death; it is left to the reader. Understatement of this kind often shocks more than a long description.

**Point of view and the title.** The story is told by a calm outside narrator who stays close to Fowler, so we learn things only when he does. The title promises a visitor at midnight, and the story keeps that promise while hiding a second twist: the real visitor Ausable was waiting for was Henry, not Max.

:::qc
q: Why does the author tell the story close to Fowler's view rather than Ausable's?
options: ["So that the reader, like Fowler, does not know the trick until the end", "So that Ausable's thoughts can be explained early", "Because Fowler is the hero"]
answer: 0
right: Yes. If we saw inside Ausable's head, we would know the balcony is false and the twist would vanish. Staying with the puzzled Fowler keeps the ending a surprise.
wrong: Think about what would happen to the ending if we knew Ausable's plan in advance.
:::

## Language: writing a character sketch {lvl=2 id=s-sketch label="Class 10 board"}

**Why it matters.** The story's first page is a model of character writing: it describes looks, voice, manner and what other people expect, all in a few lines. In the board paper you may be asked for a character sketch in 100 to 120 words.

**Four things a sketch needs.**

| Part | What to cover | Example from this story |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Build, face, dress | Ausable is very fat; Max is slender, with a pointed, fox-like face |
| Manner | Voice, movement, habits | Ausable wheezes and grumbles; Max speaks softly and waves his gun |
| Qualities | The traits you infer, each with evidence | Ausable is calm: he stays seated and keeps complaining |
| Effect | How others see him, and the point of the character | Fowler is disappointed; Max underestimates him; the ending reverses both |

**Choosing adjectives.** Prefer exact words to general ones. *Calm, composed, quick-witted, shrewd* say more than "good" or "clever". Use *adverbs* for manner (*said grimly, said moodily*). Two quick tests: could the adjective belong to anyone? Can you point to a line of evidence? If not, change it.

**Structure of a short sketch.**
1. A first sentence naming the person and the main impression.
2. Two or three qualities, each with one piece of evidence.
3. A closing sentence on what the character shows about the story's theme.

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not say Ausable killed Max or planned his death in advance; the text shows him using events as they came, and the narrator never states what happens to Max beyond the scream. {lvl=2}
- The knock is not the police: it is Henry the waiter. Do not mix up the invented police with the real waiter. {lvl=1}
- For "how does Ausable make Max believe?" name the lie, the detail that supports it, and the knock that forces a choice. A bare "he lies" earns little. {lvl=2}
- For "planned or improvised?" take a side and give evidence from both columns; an answer of "both" without reasoning is weak. {lvl=2}
- Do not call Ausable weak because he is fat. The story's point is that looks are no guide to ability. {lvl=2}
- In a character sketch, give each quality a line of evidence and keep quotations to a few words. {lvl=3}
- Do not copy lines from the chapter into an answer; paraphrase and keep any quotation short. {lvl=3}
:::
