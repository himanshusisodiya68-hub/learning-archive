## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

Lencho lives with his wife and sons in the only house in a valley, on top of a low hill. His ripe cornfield, dotted with flowers, needs only rain. When the rain comes he is delighted and jokes that the drops are coins. Then a strong wind rises and large hailstones pound the valley for an hour. The field turns white, the leaves are stripped from the trees and the corn is destroyed. That night the family is sorrowful and afraid of hunger, but they cling to one hope: help from God.

Lencho, who can write, composes a letter to God asking for a hundred pesos so that he can sow again and live until the next harvest. He addresses the envelope "To God", stamps it and posts it in town. The postman laughs, but the postmaster, after laughing too, is moved by such faith. He decides to reply. He gathers money from his staff, gives part of his own salary and asks friends for help, but manages only a little over half the amount. He signs the reply "God".

The next Sunday Lencho arrives to collect his letter. He is not surprised at all to see money, since he expects God to answer. But when he counts it he is angry, because it is short. He writes again, asks God to send the rest, and warns that it should not be sent by post, as the post office workers are a bunch of crooks.

:::qc
q: Who first reads Lencho's letter, and what does that person decide?
options: ["The postman, who throws it away", "The postmaster, who decides to answer it", "Lencho's wife, who posts it again"]
answer: 1
right: Correct. The postman only laughs and carries it to his boss; the postmaster, after a moment of laughter, resolves to reply so as not to shake the writer's faith.
wrong: Re-read the middle of the story. The postman shows the letter to his boss, and it is the boss who opens it and has the idea of answering.
:::

## Characters {lvl=2 id=s-chars label="Class 10"}

| Character | What we learn | Evidence in the story |
|---|---|---|
| Lencho | Hard-working, simple, sincere, unquestioning in his faith, quick to suspect others | He works "like an animal" in the fields; he writes with effort; he expects God never to err; he accuses the staff without checking |
| The postmaster | Kind, humorous, practical, respects another person's belief | He laughs, then turns serious; he starts a collection; he puts his own salary into it |
| The postman | Light-hearted, ordinary | He is amused by the address and hands over the letter himself |
| Lencho's family | Loving and resigned, share his hope | They comfort each other that no one dies of hunger |

Notice the contrast between the two main men. Lencho gives God the credit, and gives the post office the blame. The postmaster gives up money and gets neither credit nor thanks. His quiet generosity is the moral centre of the story.

## Setting and theme {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10"}

**Setting.** A solitary house in a valley, far from town. The isolation explains why Lencho feels no human help is possible and turns to God. A town with a post office is the only link to the wider world.

**Faith.** Lencho's faith is total: God sees everything, even what is in one's conscience, so God cannot make a mistake. This faith keeps the family going after the storm, so it is a strength. But it is also blind. When the amount is short he cannot imagine that God might have sent less, so he decides that people must have stolen it.

**Two conflicts.** The question in the book asks for these. The conflict of *humans against nature* is the hailstorm, which wipes out a year's labour in an hour. The conflict *between humans* is Lencho's distrust of the post office staff, although they have done him a kindness.

**Kindness and charity.** The postmaster's collection shows ordinary people can act as the hands of providence. The twist is that nobody can tell Lencho so.

## The author's craft {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Irony.** Irony is a gap between what we expect and what happens. Here there are several layers.
1. *Situational irony:* the people who did the favour are accused of the crime.
2. *Dramatic irony:* we, the readers, know who sent the money; Lencho does not.
3. *Gentle comic irony:* he asks God not to use the mail, yet the mail is how God's reply, and his own letter, travel.

The story never states a moral. It leaves the reader smiling and uneasy at once, which is a mark of subtle writing.

**Metaphor and simile.** Metaphors compare by saying one thing *is* another. "Huge mountains of clouds" carries the bulk of mountains over to the storm clouds. The raindrops become "new coins" in Lencho's mind, and this fits a farmer who counts rain as money. Hailstones are called "frozen pearls", a lovely image that turns sour when they do damage. Comparing the hail to "a plague of locusts" borrows the idea of an epidemic, spreading fast and leaving ruin. "An ox of a man" transfers an ox's strength and patient labour to Lencho. Notice how the metaphors move from hope (coins, pearls) to ruin (locusts) with the weather.

**Narrative voice.** The story is told in the third person by a calm narrator who sees into several minds: Lencho's, the postmaster's, even the postman's amusement. The narrator does not judge. Short sentences describe the hail with flat precision, which makes the destruction feel final. The narrator's detachment lets the irony land without comment.

:::qc

## Grammar 1: non-defining relative clauses {lvl=2 id=s-relative label="Class 10"}

**Why they exist.** Sometimes we want to add a fact about a noun without making a new sentence. A relative clause is a clause that hangs on a noun and begins with a relative pronoun (*who, whom, whose, which*). It answers the question "which one?" or tells us "also..." about the noun.

**Two jobs, two punctuations.**
- A *defining* clause picks out one person or thing from many and needs no commas: *The student who sits by the window is my cousin.* Remove it and we cannot tell which student.
- A *non-defining* clause adds extra detail about a noun we can already identify. It is wrapped in commas, or dashes. Remove it and the sentence still makes sense: *My cousin, who sits by the window, is a chess champion.*

**Choosing the pronoun.**

| Pronoun | Refers to | Example |
|---|---|---|
| who | a person (subject) | Dr Rao, who retired last year, still visits. |
| whom | a person (object) | Our teacher, whom we all admire, is leaving. |
| whose | belonging to a person or thing | The bridge, whose arches are 400 years old, is closed. |
| which | a thing or an idea | The library, which opens at nine, has a new wing. |

**Hidden pronoun.** In a non-defining clause with *who/which* plus *is/was*, writers may drop both and keep the commas or dashes: *The river — wide and slow — reached the sea.* is short for *The river — which was wide and slow — ...*

**Common slips.** Do not use *that* in a non-defining clause. Do not forget the closing comma when the clause sits in the middle of the sentence.

:::qc
q: Which sentence uses a non-defining relative clause correctly?
options: ["My brother who lives in Pune is a pilot.", "My brother, who lives in Pune, is a pilot.", "My brother, that lives in Pune, is a pilot."]
answer: 1
right: Right, if you have one brother. The clause only adds information about someone we can already identify, so it takes commas and the pronoun *who*, never *that*.
wrong: A non-defining clause needs commas on both sides and cannot use *that*. Only one option has both.
:::

## Grammar 2: negatives for emphasis {lvl=2 id=s-negatives label="Class 10"}

**First principle.** A negative word usually removes or denies something: *no corn* means corn is absent. But a speaker can also use the negative to strengthen a claim by ruling out every alternative. If I say "He did nothing but sleep", I do not deny he did something; I stress that sleeping was all he did.

**Common patterns.**

| Pattern | Plain meaning | Fresh example |
|---|---|---|
| nothing but / nothing else but | only | The child did nothing but cry all night. |
| no other ... than | only | She came for no other purpose than to say thank you. |
| not the slightest / not a single | none at all | He showed not the slightest interest. |
| never ... had | the first and only time | Never had I seen such a sunset. |
| nothing less than | exactly, no smaller thing | The result was nothing less than a miracle. |

**Inversion.** When a negative adverb (*never, hardly, not once*) begins the sentence, the verb moves before the subject: *Never had he known that address.* This word order sounds formal and adds weight.

**Trap.** Do not stack two plain negatives (*I don't know nothing*). In these emphatic patterns there is just one negative word.

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not retell the whole story when the question asks about a theme. Give a claim, one piece of evidence, one comment. {lvl=1}
- Do not say Lencho is stupid or greedy without evidence; the text shows him as sincere, hard-working and trusting, with one blind spot. {lvl=2}
- Irony needs two parts: what was expected and what happened. Naming only one earns half marks. {lvl=2}
- Do not call the postmaster's act a mistake. He knew the sum would fall short and acted anyway because he valued the faith. {lvl=2}
- In a non-defining clause, never use *that* and never omit the second comma. {lvl=2}
- Do not quote long passages in an answer. A short phrase with your own comment is better than a copied paragraph. {lvl=3}
:::
