## What makes a phrase an idiom {lvl=1 id=s-what label="Foundation"}

Take the sentence *The exam was a piece of cake.* If you read it word by word, you picture a slice of cake on an exam paper. But if you read it as a whole, you picture a student smiling after an easy test. The second reading is the right one.

That is the whole idea. An **idiom** is a fixed group of words whose meaning is different from the meaning of the separate words. We say it has two meanings:

- The **literal meaning** is what the words say on the surface: a slice of cake.
- The **figurative meaning** is the hidden meaning carried by a picture: something very easy.

An idiom is always used for its figurative meaning. Why does English have such strange phrases at all? Because people love a vivid picture. Saying *He kicked the bucket* is shorter and more colourful than a long sentence, and a phrase that people find useful gets repeated until it becomes fixed. After that, nobody needs the picture any more; the phrase simply means what it means.

### How an idiom differs from its neighbours

| Kind | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Literal phrase | Means exactly what the words say | a cup of tea |
| **Idiom** | Fixed phrase with a hidden meaning | a piece of cake (very easy) |
| Metaphor | A fresh comparison made on the spot | Time is a thief |
| Simile | A comparison using *like* or *as* | as busy as a bee |
| **Proverb** | A complete saying that gives advice or a truth | A stitch in time saves nine |
| Phrasal verb | Verb + small word with a new meaning | give up (stop trying) |

Two clues help you decide. First, an idiom is *fixed*: you cannot swap its words (nobody says *a slice of pie* for *a piece of cake*). Second, an idiom is a *phrase* that fits into a sentence, whereas a proverb is a whole sentence that can stand alone.

:::qc
q: Which of these is a proverb, not an idiom?
options: ["A stitch in time saves nine", "A piece of cake", "Under the weather", "Break the ice"]
answer: 0
right: Right. It is a complete saying that gives advice. The other three are phrases that fit inside a sentence.
wrong: A proverb is a whole saying that gives advice or a truth. Only one option is a full sentence with advice in it.
:::

:::qc
q: In which sentence is "a piece of cake" used literally?
options: ["She cut a piece of cake for her brother.", "The quiz was a piece of cake.", "Winning the match was a piece of cake.", "Driving a bike is a piece of cake for him."]
answer: 0
right: Right. Here the words mean exactly what they say: a slice of cake. In the others it means something very easy.
wrong: Ask whether anyone is really holding a slice of cake. Only one sentence is about actual cake.
:::

## Where idioms come from {lvl=1 id=s-source label="Foundation"}

Idioms are not random. They come from the everyday things people see, touch and do. Once you notice the source, many idioms become easier to remember, because you can see the picture.

| Source | Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Body | keep an eye on | watch carefully |
| Body | lend a hand | help |
| Body | cold feet | sudden fear before doing something |
| Animals | let the cat out of the bag | reveal a secret by accident |
| Animals | a wild goose chase | a useless search |
| Food | spill the beans | reveal a secret |
| Food | in a nutshell | in a few words |
| Weather | under the weather | slightly ill |
| Weather | a storm in a teacup | a big fuss over a small matter |
| Colours | green with envy | very jealous |
| Colours | out of the blue | suddenly and without warning |
| Money | cost an arm and a leg | be very expensive |
| Work | burn the midnight oil | study or work late at night |
| Sea and travel | in the same boat | in the same difficult situation |

The picture often hints at the meaning. *Cold feet* gives the feeling of hesitation, *green with envy* connects a colour with a feeling, and *burn the midnight oil* shows a lamp burning late into the night.

## Using idioms correctly {lvl=1 id=s-using label="Foundation"}

Three simple rules save you from most mistakes.

1. **Keep the words fixed.** An idiom is a chunk. *She spilled the beans* is fine (the tense changed), but *She spilled the peas* is not an idiom any more.
2. **Change only the grammar around it.** You may change the tense (spills, spilled), the person (I, she, they) and the number of the noun that belongs to the sentence. You may not add or drop words from the idiom itself.
3. **Do not translate.** The Hindi *naak mein dam karna* does not become *to make a breath in the nose*. Find the English idiom with the matching meaning (to give someone a hard time), or use plain words.

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| She spilled all the peas. | She spilled the beans. | Wrong word in the fixed phrase |
| It was a cake piece. | It was a piece of cake. | Wrong word order |
| He is under a weather. | He is under the weather. | Article changed |
| She lent me a leg. | She lent me a hand. | Body part changed |

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["The test was a piece of cake.", "The test was a cake piece.", "The test was a slice of cake.", "The test was pieces of cakes."]
answer: 0
right: Right. The idiom keeps its words and its order.
wrong: Only one option keeps all the words of the idiom in their fixed order.
:::

## Idioms in the board paper {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

Idioms rarely appear as a question on their own in the Class 10 paper. They turn up inside other tasks, so you need to recognise them in context. Check the current sample paper for the exact pattern each year [Unverified]. The usual forms are these.

1. **Meaning in a sentence (multiple choice).** A sentence has an idiom in it, and you choose the option that gives its meaning. Read the whole sentence, picture the situation, and eliminate options that only echo the words.
2. **Extract-based vocabulary.** In a prose or poetry extract, or a reading passage, a question asks for the meaning of a phrase or for a phrase "from the passage that means" something. Go back to the line and read the sentences before and after it.
3. **Fill in the blank with an idiom.** A sentence has a gap, and four idioms are offered. Choose the one that fits the meaning *and* the tone of the sentence.
4. **Correct use.** Spot the sentence where the idiom is used wrongly, or where one word has been changed.
5. **Using idioms in writing.** In a letter, article or speech, one well-placed idiom can lift the writing. It is not a separate question, but it earns credit for style.

:::note title="The echo trap" tone=warn
Wrong options in an idiom question are often built from the literal words of the idiom. For "She was under the weather", an option about rain or clouds is almost surely a trap. The right answer is about how she felt (slightly ill). Choose the option about the situation, not the one that echoes the words.
:::

:::qc
q: "The new manager decided to take the bull by the horns and fix the long delays." What did the manager do?
options: ["Faced the problem boldly", "Avoided the problem", "Went to a farm", "Asked for help"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Take the bull by the horns means to deal with a difficulty directly and bravely.
wrong: The manager is fixing a problem, so the idiom is about dealing with it directly, not avoiding it.
:::

## Reading an idiom from its context {lvl=2 id=s-context label="Class 10"}

Suppose the idiom is new to you. You are not stuck. Use this method.

1. **Say the sentence without the idiom** and ask what is missing. What is the speaker trying to tell you?
2. **Find the situation.** Who is speaking, to whom, and what has just happened?
3. **Check the feeling.** Is the tone happy, angry, afraid, amused?
4. **Try the literal reading.** If it makes no sense here, it is probably figurative.
5. **Test your guess.** Put your meaning back into the sentence in place of the idiom. If it reads naturally, you probably have it.

:::worked title="Working out an idiom you have never seen"
Read: *Everyone expected the old rival to be angry, but when he arrived with flowers, it was clear he wished to bury the hatchet.*

Step 1: Take the idiom out: "he wished to ___." Step 2: The rival arrived with flowers, so he is being friendly. Step 3: The mood is peace, not anger. Step 4: A hatchet is a small axe, and burying it is not something a visitor would do with flowers, so the phrase is figurative. Step 5: Try "he wished to make peace." That reads naturally. So *bury the hatchet* means to end a quarrel and become friends again.
:::

### Some useful signals

| Clue in the sentence | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| but, however, although | a contrast; the idiom may mean the opposite of the sentence before |
| so, therefore | the idiom is a result of what came before |
| an adjective or feeling word nearby | the idiom is about that feeling |
| an exclamation mark | strong emotion, such as surprise or delight |

:::qc
q: "After years of quarrelling, the two brothers decided to bury the hatchet." What happened?
options: ["They made peace", "They fought again", "They dug a hole", "They left home"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Burying the hatchet means ending a quarrel and making peace.
wrong: "After years of quarrelling" is the clue. The idiom is the natural next step: they ended it.
:::

## Idioms in your own writing {lvl=2 id=s-write label="Class 10"}

An idiom can make a letter, article or speech more alive. But it is a seasoning, not the main dish.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Use one fitting idiom to make a point | Pack three idioms into one sentence |
| Match tone: casual in a friendly letter | Use slangy idioms in a formal complaint |
| Place it where the meaning is clear | Use it as a decoration with no purpose |
| Use only idioms you are sure of | Guess at an idiom and risk the wrong one |

For instance, an informal letter to a friend might say *I have been burning the midnight oil for the board exams*, while a formal letter to the principal would be better with *I have been preparing intensively*. Both mean the same, but they have a different register.

### Writing a sentence that shows the meaning

When a question asks you to "use the idiom in a sentence", your sentence must show that you know the meaning. A sentence like *He broke the ice* shows nothing. A sentence like *A funny remark broke the ice, and soon everybody was chatting* proves the meaning through the context.

:::qc
q: Which sentence shows the meaning of "in the nick of time" best?
options: ["The fireman reached the house in the nick of time and saved the child.", "He used it in the nick of time.", "In the nick of time is an idiom.", "The nick of time was there."]
answer: 0
right: Right. The sentence shows a rescue that happened just before it was too late, so the meaning is clear.
wrong: A good sentence shows the meaning through the situation. Only one option does that.
:::

## Beyond the board: the life of an idiom {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### Where idioms really come from

Many popular origin stories are tidy but doubtful. Some well-known cases:

| Idiom | A common story | How safe is it? |
|---|---|---|
| let the cat out of the bag | A trader sold a cat in a bag instead of a piglet, and a buyer saw the trick | Popular but uncertain [Unverified] |
| raining cats and dogs | Animals washed off roofs in storms | No agreement among scholars [Unverified] |
| kick the bucket | From a bucket kicked away in a hanging, or from a slaughter frame | Origins disputed [Unverified] |
| show the white feather | A white feather in a fighting cock's tail was a sign of poor breeding, so it came to mean cowardice | Often given; not certain [Unverified] |
| by and large | A sailing term for sailing both into and across the wind | Commonly given [Unverified] |

The honest lesson is that when a source says "this phrase came from X", ask for the evidence. A phrase can have a clear origin (many come from the Bible, Shakespeare, sailing, cricket or archery), but plenty of origin stories are guesses added afterwards. In an exam you never need the origin, only the meaning.

### Flexible idioms

Not all idioms are equally rigid. Some can bend a little.

- **Passive:** *The beans were spilled* (still understood).
- **Modified for effect:** *She let the whole cat out of the bag.* (a playful stretch)
- **Shortened:** *A bird in the hand ...* (the listener finishes the proverb)

Skilled writers bend an idiom for a joke or emphasis, but they only do this because the reader knows the original. So the first step is always the fixed form.

### Clichés and mixed metaphors

A **cliché** is an idiom worn thin by overuse: *at the end of the day, last but not least, needless to say*. A writer who leans on clichés sounds as though they are not thinking. Use an idiom when it truly says what you need; otherwise, plain words are better.

A **mixed metaphor** is two pictures stuck together: *We must nip this problem in the bud before it snowballs.* Bud and snowball do not belong to the same picture. Check that your idioms in one sentence belong to the same picture.

### Idioms across languages

Every language has idioms, and the pictures differ even when the meanings match. The Hindi *ऊँट के मुँह में जीरा* (cumin seed in a camel's mouth) says what English says with *a drop in the ocean*. *नौ दो ग्यारह होना* (to turn nine and two into eleven) means to run away. These match in meaning, not in words, so translating word for word fails [Unverified: these glosses are from memory; check a Hindi dictionary]. When you meet an English idiom that has a Hindi cousin, link the two meanings and remember the English picture separately.

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. An idiom is a fixed phrase whose meaning is different from the meaning of its words; it has a literal meaning on the surface and a figurative one underneath. {lvl=1}
2. Learn each idiom as one unit, in a sentence, and by theme (body, animals, food, weather, colours, money). {lvl=1}
3. Keep the words and their order fixed; change only tense, person and the grammar around the idiom. {lvl=1}
4. A proverb is a whole saying that gives advice; an idiom is a phrase that fits into a sentence. {lvl=1}
5. Never translate an idiom word for word from another language; match meaning to meaning. {lvl=1}
6. In the board paper, idioms appear inside other tasks: meaning in a sentence, extract-based vocabulary, gap-filling and correct use. [Unverified: check the current sample paper] {lvl=2}
7. Work out an unknown idiom from context: remove it, find the situation and the feeling, then test your guess in the sentence. {lvl=2}
8. Wrong options often echo the literal words; choose the option that fits the situation. {lvl=2}
9. Match register: informal idioms in friendly writing, plain words in formal writing, and use only idioms you are sure of. {lvl=2}
10. Many tidy origin stories are folk etymology; avoid clichés and mixed metaphors. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Reading the idiom literally: "spill the beans" is not about beans. Ask what the whole sentence means. {lvl=1}
- Changing a word in the fixed phrase ("a cake piece", "spilled the peas"). {lvl=1}
- Confusing a proverb with an idiom: a proverb is a whole saying, an idiom is a phrase inside a sentence. {lvl=1}
- Translating a Hindi or other-language idiom word for word. {lvl=1}
- Picking the option that echoes the literal words (the echo trap). {lvl=2}
- Writing a sentence that uses the idiom but does not show its meaning ("He broke the ice."). {lvl=2}
- Using a casual idiom in a formal letter. {lvl=2}
- Mixing two idioms from different pictures in one sentence (a mixed metaphor). {lvl=3}
- Trusting a neat origin story without evidence; in the exam you need only the meaning. {lvl=3}
:::
