## Why words keep company {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Imagine a language where every word could go with every other word. You could learn the words one by one and build any sentence. English is not like that. Many words have **favourite partners**, and a sentence with the wrong partner sounds odd even when every word is spelt right and the grammar is perfect.

- You **make** a decision, but you **do** a favour.
- You **catch** a cold, but you **take** a bath.
- You say **heavy** rain and **strong** wind, never the other way round.

A pair that English habitually uses together is a **collocation**. The word comes from the idea of "placing together" [Unverified: Latin origin]. Native speakers do not build these pairs word by word; they remember them as ready-made **chunks**, the way you remember a phone number as groups of digits, not ten separate digits.

This matters for two reasons. First, wrong pairs are the most common reason that a correct-looking sentence sounds foreign. Second, a learner who stores chunks speaks faster and writes more smoothly, because the pair arrives as one piece.

:::note title="Meaning is not enough" tone=tip
A collocation is a matter of **habit**, not logic. A near-synonym usually makes sense, and the reader will understand you, but the sentence sounds slightly wrong. In exam writing that costs marks for "appropriate vocabulary".
:::

## The main kinds of pair {lvl=1 id=s-kinds label="Foundation"}

Collocations come in a few common shapes. Seeing the shape tells you what to ask when you are unsure.

| Shape | Examples | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| verb + noun | make a promise, take a break, keep a secret | Which verb does this noun expect? |
| adjective + noun | heavy rain, bitter truth, sound sleep | Which adjective does this noun expect? |
| adverb + adjective | deeply grateful, fully aware, bitterly cold | Which adverb does this adjective expect? |
| verb + adverb | listen carefully, apologise sincerely, sleep soundly | Which adverb does this verb expect? |
| quantity word + of + noun | a flock of sheep, a gust of wind, a pang of hunger | Which counting word does this noun expect? |
| word + preposition | good at, afraid of, depend on | Which small word follows? |

:::qc
q: Which pair is a collocation of the verb + noun type?
options: ["heavy rain", "take a photo", "deeply grateful", "good at"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Take is a verb and photo is its noun. Heavy rain is adjective + noun, deeply grateful is adverb + adjective, good at is a word + preposition.
wrong: Look for a verb followed by its noun. Only take a photo has that shape.
:::

## Make, do, have and take {lvl=1 id=s-mdht label="Foundation"}

These four verbs are small and they appear in hundreds of collocations. Most learners meet their first collocation errors here.

A rough rule helps. **Make** usually means to create or produce something that was not there before (a plan, a noise, a cup of tea). **Do** usually names a task, a job or an activity (the dishes, homework, work). **Have** often goes with meals, experiences and short activities (have lunch, have a look). **Take** often goes with a single action or a chosen step (take a photo, take a break). The rule has exceptions, so treat it as a guide and learn the phrases.

| make | do | have | take |
|---|---|---|---|
| a mistake | homework | breakfast, lunch | a photo |
| a decision | the dishes | a shower | a break |
| progress | a favour | a look | an exam |
| a noise | your best | fun | medicine |
| money | business | an accident | a seat |
| friends | harm, good | a cold, a headache | notes |
| a plan | research | a dream | place (to happen) |
| a speech | exercises | an argument | care of |

:::note title="Take a decision or make a decision?" tone=tip
Both are used. British English often says take a decision; American English says make a decision. Both are accepted in the Indian board paper. The mistake is do a decision.
:::

:::qc
q: "The teacher asked us to ___ notes while she explained the lesson."
options: ["do", "make", "take", "write down"]
answer: 2
right: Right. The pair is take notes. (Make notes is also used, but take is the standard in this sense, and write down changes the pattern.)
wrong: The collocation is take notes. Do notes is not used.
:::

## Adjectives that fit the noun {lvl=1 id=s-adj label="Foundation"}

An adjective is chosen by the noun, not by its meaning. The table shows pairs with a similar meaning and the noun that picks each.

| Adjective | Natural partners | Sounds odd with |
|---|---|---|
| heavy | rain, traffic, smoker, burden | wind, tea |
| strong | wind, tea, coffee, smell, evidence | rain, traffic |
| high | speed, price, temperature, fever | person (use tall) |
| tall | person, building, tree | price |
| deep | sleep, breath, thought, regret | rain |
| sound | sleep, advice, judgment | traffic |
| fast | food, train, learner | glance (use quick) |
| quick | glance, meal, decision | train |
| bitter | cold, truth, disappointment | smile |
| close | friend, relative, contact | price |

Notice the habit: rain and traffic take **heavy**, wind and flavour take **strong**, prices and speed take **high**, people and trees take **tall**. A fixed pair like *sound sleep* (also *sound asleep*) is simply learnt.

:::qc
q: "There was a ___ traffic on the highway this morning."
options: ["strong", "heavy", "big", "tall"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Heavy traffic is the collocation.
wrong: Traffic takes heavy. You can say heavy rain and heavy traffic, but strong wind.
:::

## More verbs that need the right noun {lvl=2 id=s-verbs label="Class 10"}

Many other verbs pick their nouns firmly. Learning them in families makes the choice quicker.

| Verb | Common collocations | Odd choice |
|---|---|---|
| keep | a promise, a secret, a diary, calm, quiet | hold a promise |
| break | a promise, the law, a record, the news, the ice | cut a promise |
| pay | attention, a visit, a compliment, a bill, a fine | give attention |
| catch | a cold, a bus, a train, fire, someone's eye | take a cold |
| draw | a conclusion, a picture, attention | take a conclusion |
| reach | a decision, an agreement, a conclusion | arrive a decision |
| raise | a question, money, an objection | lift a question |
| tell | a lie, a joke, the truth, a story, the time | say a lie |
| say | a word, sorry, a prayer, goodbye, yes | tell a prayer |
| give | a speech, a reason, advice, a warning | do advice |
| run | a business, a risk, a race, an errand | drive a business |
| win / lose | a match, an argument, a prize / a game, weight | gain a prize |

Other families behave the same way. **Commit** goes with wrong acts (a crime, a mistake, an offence), never with good ones. **Achieve** goes with goals and success (a goal, an aim, success). **Perform** goes with a task or a show (a task, an operation, a play).

:::qc
q: "The new rule will help us ___ the problem of waste."
options: ["do", "solve", "cut", "win"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Solve a problem. (You also face, tackle or deal with a problem.)
wrong: The verb that fits problem here is solve. Do, cut and win do not pair with problem in this sense.
:::

## Quantity phrases {lvl=2 id=s-quantity label="Class 10"}

English has special measuring words for particular things. We do not say a group of cards, but a **pack** of cards. The word before **of** is chosen by the noun.

| Phrase | Example |
|---|---|
| a pack / deck of | cards |
| a flock of | sheep, birds |
| a herd of | cattle, elephants |
| a swarm of | bees, locusts |
| a bunch of | keys, flowers, grapes, bananas |
| a piece of | advice, information, news, furniture |
| a loaf of | bread |
| a bar of | chocolate, soap |
| a pair of | scissors, trousers, shoes |
| a gust of | wind |
| a pang of | hunger, guilt |
| a ray of | hope, sunlight |
| a burst of | laughter, applause |
| a round of | applause |
| a stroke of | luck, genius |
| a spell of | cold weather, dry weather |

Advice, information, furniture and news have no plural, so they need a *piece of*. We say *a piece of advice*, not *an advice*, and *two pieces of furniture*.

:::qc
q: "She felt a ___ of guilt when she saw her friend's face."
options: ["pang", "herd", "bunch", "loaf"]
answer: 0
right: Right. A pang of guilt (or hunger) is a sudden sharp feeling.
wrong: A pang is a sudden sharp feeling. Herd, bunch and loaf do not pair with guilt.
:::

## Adverbs and prepositions as partners {lvl=2 id=s-adv label="Class 10"}

### Adverb partners

An adverb often intensifies an adjective or a verb, and each adjective has its usual intensifiers.

| Adjective or verb | Natural adverb | Odd choice |
|---|---|---|
| aware | fully, well, acutely | heavily aware |
| grateful | deeply, extremely | heavily grateful |
| cold (weather) | bitterly | bitterly pleased |
| different | completely, totally, entirely | heavily different |
| essential | absolutely | strongly essential |
| disappointed | bitterly, deeply | highly disappointed |
| recommended | highly, strongly | strictly recommended |
| listen | carefully, closely | listen heavily |
| apologise | sincerely, profusely | apologise strongly |
| debated | hotly, widely | |

### Dependent prepositions

Many words are followed by one fixed preposition, which is also a collocation.

| Word | Preposition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| good, bad, clever | at | She is good at maths. |
| afraid, fond, proud | of | He is proud of his school. |
| interested, involved | in | I am interested in birds. |
| depend, rely, insist | on | It depends on the weather. |
| suffer, recover | from | She is recovering from flu. |
| congratulate, compliment | on | We congratulated him on his win. |
| provide, supply | with | They provided us with books. |

The full rules of prepositions are in their own lesson. Here the point is that the preposition is part of the collocation and you learn it with the word.

## Traps from the mother tongue {lvl=2 id=s-mother label="Class 10"}

Many board-level errors come from translating a phrase word by word from Hindi or another home language. Hindi uses *karna* ("to do") for many things for which English uses *make*, and uses *kholna* ("to open") for appliances, where English says *switch on*.

| Word-for-word | Standard English |
|---|---|
| do a mistake | make a mistake |
| say a lie | tell a lie |
| open the light, open the fan | switch on, turn on the light, the fan |
| close the light | switch off, turn off the light |
| eat medicine | take medicine |
| do friends with her | make friends with her |
| make a photo | take a photo |
| do a speech | give or make a speech |
| do a noise | make a noise |
| do an accident | have an accident |
| see a dream | have a dream |
| strong rain | heavy rain |
| heavy wind | strong wind |
| do a phone call | make a phone call |

Do not "correct" the right-hand column. In particular, **give an exam** is common in Indian English, but the standard forms are *take an exam*, *sit an exam* and *appear for an exam*. In a board answer use *take* or *appear for*.

:::note title="A quick test for a doubtful pair" tone=tip
1. Say the phrase aloud. If it makes you hesitate, it is probably wrong.
2. Ask what the noun *usually* does: is it made, done, taken, had, given, kept or broken?
3. Try the verb families (make, do, take, have, give). Choose the one you have heard with this noun.
4. If unsure in an exam, rewrite with a safer verb: instead of *make a photo*, *take a photo*.
:::

:::qc
q: Which sentence is correct?
options: ["Please open the light, it is dark.", "Please switch on the light, it is dark.", "Please do the light, it is dark.", "Please make the light, it is dark."]
answer: 1
right: Right. You switch on a light (or turn it on). Open goes with doors, books and boxes.
wrong: Lights are switched on or turned on. Open, do and make do not pair with light.
:::

## Collocations in the board paper {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

The board paper rarely asks "What is the collocation?" The skill is tested in disguise (the exact format of the paper changes from year to year [Unverified]).

1. **Gap-filling (multiple choice or fill-in).** One option fits the meaning but not the partner. The right option is the one a fluent speaker would say.
2. **Editing and error correction.** A line may hide a wrong pair such as *did a mistake*. You replace the single wrong word.
3. **Reading comprehension and vocabulary.** Words in a passage often appear in set phrases; knowing the phrase helps you work out a meaning.
4. **Writing tasks (letters, notices, articles).** The marking looks at accurate and appropriate vocabulary. A formal letter with the right fixed phrases scores better than one in plain, vague words.

:::worked title="Editing four lines in a minute"
Each line has one error. Correct it.

1. *Last Sunday we did a picnic at the lake.* 
2. *Rani said a lie to her mother about the vase.* 
3. *Her mother was angry, but she took a decision to forgive her.* 
4. *By evening there was a strong rain and we left.* 

Line 1: *did* should be **had** (have a picnic). Line 2: *said* should be **told** (tell a lie). Line 3 has no error: take a decision is accepted, so do not change it. Line 4: *strong* should be **heavy** (heavy rain). Note: line 3 shows that not every unusual-looking pair is wrong, so only change a pair when you are sure.
:::

## Formal collocations for letters and notices {lvl=2 id=s-formal label="Class 10"}

Formal writing leans on fixed phrases. Learn them as units and your letter reads as confident.

| Purpose | Collocations |
|---|---|
| Complaint | lodge a complaint, bring to your notice, draw your attention to, cause inconvenience, take prompt action |
| Request | make a request, seek permission, grant leave, request you to, look into the matter |
| Application | apply for a post, submit an application, enclose a copy, attach a certificate, meet the requirements |
| Apology | tender an apology, express regret, apologise sincerely, regret the delay |
| Thanks | express gratitude, extend thanks, be deeply grateful, appreciate your help |
| Notices | hold a meeting, organise an event, take part in, be held on, be cancelled due to |

These phrases are for letters and notices. In a message to a friend, use plainer wording: *tell you* instead of *bring to your notice*.

:::qc
q: Which phrase is best in a complaint to the Principal?
options: ["I want to bring to your notice that the fans are not working.", "I want to tell you that the fans are broken, you know.", "I am doing a complaint that the fans are broken.", "I am giving a notice that the fans are broken."]
answer: 0
right: Right. Bring to your notice is a standard formal collocation for a complaint. Do a complaint is wrong; the pair is lodge or make a complaint.
wrong: Choose the formal, fixed phrase. Do a complaint and give a notice (in this sense) are not natural pairs, and the second is too casual.
:::

## Strong and weak pairs {lvl=3 id=s-strength label="Beyond the board"}

Not all collocations are equally firm. Think of a scale.

- **Free combinations**: almost any word fits. *A good / nice / big house.* Nothing is learnt here.
- **Weak collocations**: several partners, but some sound better. *A big / large / great number*; *great* is less usual.
- **Strong collocations**: one or two partners are overwhelmingly expected. *Bitterly cold; inflict damage; a torrential downpour*.
- **Restricted collocations**: the word hardly appears anywhere else. *Addled* is used almost only with *brain* or *egg*; *auburn* with *hair*; *shrug* with *shoulders*.
- **Idioms**: the meaning changes too. *Break the ice* does not mean to break ice. These go in the idioms lesson.

The dividing lines are not sharp, which is why dictionaries of collocations are built by counting what real writers do, not by following a rule. The practical rule is: when a pair is strong, use it, because the reader expects it. When a pair is weak, choose the one that gives the exact shade you need.

:::qc
q: Which of these is a restricted collocation (the word is used almost only in this pair)?
options: ["a big house", "auburn hair", "a good idea", "a new book"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Auburn is a reddish-brown shade used almost only for hair, so it is restricted. The others are free or weak combinations.
wrong: Look for a word that rarely goes with anything else. Auburn is used almost only for hair.
:::

## Feeling, formality and unlucky words {lvl=3 id=s-prosody label="Beyond the board"}

Words acquire a **mood** from their usual company. Corpus studies report that *cause* is used mostly with unpleasant things (cause damage, cause trouble, cause a delay), while *provide* is used mostly with helpful things (provide help, provide shelter) [Unverified: general finding from corpus linguistics]. This is called **semantic prosody**.

The consequence for a writer:

- Say *the heavy rain caused flooding*, but *the new scheme brought benefits* or *led to improvements*. *The scheme caused improvements* would sound wrong or sarcastic.
- *Commit* signals wrongdoing: commit a crime, a mistake, an error. Nobody *commits a kindness*; they *do* or *show* one.

The second point is **register**. Many informal and formal partners mean the same thing.

| Informal | Formal |
|---|---|
| ask for help | seek assistance |
| get a loan | obtain a loan |
| complain about | lodge a complaint about |
| look into it | investigate the matter |
| sort out the problem | resolve the issue |
| tell off | reprimand |
| say sorry | tender an apology |

In a chat use the left column; in a board letter use the right. Mixing the two (*kindly sort out my problem at your earliest*) sounds uneven.

## Building your own collocation bank {lvl=3 id=s-bank label="Beyond the board"}

You cannot memorise the whole list, so collect it as you read. Habits that work:

1. **Write the phrase, not the word.** In your notebook write *make headway* or *a deep sleep*, not *headway* or *deep*.
2. **Group by noun.** One page headed *decision* with take, make, reach, announce, final, difficult; another headed *attention* with pay, draw, catch, attract, undivided.
3. **Read with a pencil.** When a newspaper editorial or a passage uses an unexpected pair (*mounting pressure*, *sweeping changes*), underline it and copy it.
4. **Check with a collocations dictionary** when you are unsure, and in the exam when you cannot, choose the plainer, safer pair.
5. **Use it within a day.** Put the new phrase in one sentence of your own, aloud or in writing.

When you write, one precise pair does more than a pile of adjectives. Compare *a very big problem* with *a serious problem* or *a mounting problem*; and *very hungry* with *ravenous* or *extremely hungry*. A good collocation is often the shortest route to a vivid sentence.

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A collocation is a pair or group of words that natural English habitually uses together (heavy rain, make a decision). {lvl=1}
2. The pairing is a habit, not a rule of logic: strong and powerful are close in meaning, yet strong tea is right and powerful tea is not. {lvl=1}
3. The partner is picked by the other word. Ask which verb, adjective, adverb or measuring word the noun expects. {lvl=1}
4. Make creates (a plan, a noise, a mistake); do names a task (homework, the dishes, a favour); have goes with meals and short experiences; take goes with a step (a photo, a break, an exam). {lvl=1}
5. Tell a lie, a joke, the truth; say a word, sorry, a prayer. Keep or break a promise; pay attention; catch a cold; draw a conclusion. {lvl=2}
6. Measuring phrases are fixed by the noun: a pack of cards, a flock of sheep, a pang of guilt, a piece of advice. {lvl=2}
7. Word-for-word errors from the mother tongue (do a mistake, open the light, eat medicine) are the commonest collocation mistakes in the board paper. {lvl=2}
8. Formal writing needs the fixed formal pairs (lodge a complaint, bring to your notice, express gratitude). {lvl=2}
9. Pairs range from free to weak to strong to restricted; the stronger the pair, the more the reader expects it. {lvl=3}
10. Words carry the mood of their company (cause with trouble, provide with help); learn phrases in a notebook, grouped by noun. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "She did a mistake": the pair is make a mistake. {lvl=1}
- "He said a lie": tell a lie. Say goes with words, sorry and prayers. {lvl=1}
- "Open the light/fan/TV": switch on or turn on. Switch off or turn off for the reverse. {lvl=1}
- "Strong rain" and "heavy wind": heavy rain, strong wind. {lvl=1}
- "An advice" or "informations": advice and information have no plural and no a/an; say a piece of advice. {lvl=2}
- "Eat medicine": take medicine. {lvl=2}
- "Give an exam" in a board answer: use take, sit or appear for. {lvl=2}
- Treating every unusual-looking pair as wrong: take a decision is accepted, so only correct a pair you are sure about. {lvl=2}
- Using a vague word with an intensifier ("very big problem", "very good result") where one exact collocation would do ("serious problem", "excellent result"). {lvl=3}
- Casual pairs in a formal letter ("sort out", "get a loan", "look into it"): choose resolve, obtain and investigate. {lvl=3}
:::
