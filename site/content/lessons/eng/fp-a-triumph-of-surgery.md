## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

Mr Herriot, a veterinary surgeon, is shocked when he meets his client Mrs Pumphrey in the street with her dog Tricki. The dog has become hugely fat, with bloodshot eyes and a lolling tongue. Mrs Pumphrey explains that he seemed weak, so she has been giving him extras: malt, cod-liver oil, a bowl of Horlicks at night, and plenty of cream cakes and chocolates. The vet had told her to cut down on sweet things and give more exercise, but she says she cannot bear to refuse him. The gardener, who used to throw a ring for the dog to chase, has lumbago, so there has been no exercise either. Mr Herriot warns her sternly, and watches Tricki totter off in his little tweed coat.

A few days later, a distraught Mrs Pumphrey calls. Tricki refuses food, vomits and lies panting on his rug. The vet has already made his plan. He insists that Tricki be hospitalised for a fortnight. The lady nearly faints and is sure the dog will pine and die without her, but he takes a firm line, carries Tricki to the car in a blanket, and drives off while the whole household weeps and a flurry of beds, bowls and coats is thrown after him.

At the surgery, Tricki lies still and ignored by the other dogs. For two days he is given only water. On the third day he whimpers at the sound of the dogs in the yard, and soon he is out with them, rolling over, joining their rough games, eating at feeding time and even hunting rats at night. He has no medicine of any kind. Meanwhile, Mrs Pumphrey rings a dozen times a day. When the vet says Tricki is "convalescing", she starts to send gifts to build up his strength: fresh eggs, then wine, then brandy. Mr Herriot and his partners enjoy every bit of it.

After a fortnight the vet phones to say the dog has recovered. Mrs Pumphrey arrives in an enormous car. Tricki, now lean and lively, leaps from the vet's arms into her lap and covers her with licks. She weeps with thanks and calls it "a triumph of surgery", although there was never an operation.

:::qc
q: What does Mr Herriot actually do to cure Tricki?
options: ["He performs an operation on the dog's stomach", "He gives the dog a course of strong medicine", "He keeps him away from the rich food and lets him play and exercise with the other dogs"]
answer: 2
right: Correct. Two days without food, then ordinary meals and a lot of exercise with the household dogs. No treatment of any kind is needed, which is why the ending is funny.
wrong: Re-read the middle of the story. The text says plainly that Tricki had no medicinal treatment of any kind.
:::

## Key vocabulary {lvl=1 id=s-vocab label="Foundation"}

| Word | Meaning | Use in a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| rheumy | watery (of the eyes or nose) | The old dog's rheumy eyes followed me round the room. |
| listless | lacking energy and enthusiasm | The heat left everyone listless by noon. |
| lumbago | pain in the lower back | His lumbago kept him from lifting the box. |

| distraught | extremely worried and upset | The distraught mother searched the market for her child. |
| surgery | here, a place where a vet or doctor treats patients (the usual sense is an operation) | The surgery opens at nine. |
| scrimmage | a rough, confused struggle | The children's scrimmage for the ball left them muddy. |
| convalescing | recovering from an illness | She spent a week convalescing at her aunt's home. |
| lithe | flexible and graceful | The lithe gymnast sprang across the mat. |
| swooned | fainted | The heat was so great that she nearly swooned. |

Notice that *surgery* has two meanings. In "the surgery" the vet uses it for his clinic. In "a triumph of surgery" Mrs Pumphrey uses it for an operation. The title plays on the word's second meaning.

## Characters {lvl=2 id=s-chars label="Class 10"}

| Character | What we learn | Evidence in the story |
|---|---|---|
| Mr Herriot (the narrator) | Practical, tactful, humorous, firm when it matters, but also human enough to enjoy the gifts | He sounds severe and then takes a firm line; he sees the problem is the diet, not the dog; he accepts eggs, wine and brandy with cheerful honesty |
| Mrs Pumphrey | Wealthy, loving, lonely, anxious and totally lacking in common sense | Cannot refuse the dog cream cakes; "wrung her hands"; staff rush about with beds, bowls and coats; weeps and thanks him for a "triumph of surgery" |
| Tricki | Greedy, dull when overfed, an energetic dog once freed | "Tricki's only fault was greed"; at the surgery he runs, fights "like a tiger" for his share, hunts rats and leaps into Mrs Pumphrey's lap |
| Household staff, Hodgkin the gardener | Obedient to a rich employer; the story hints that nobody there dares to say no | The maids rush in and out; the gardener's lumbago ends the daily ring-throwing |
| The surgery dogs (Joe, the greyhound, and the gang) | Rough, friendly, honest in their dealings | They sniff Tricki, roll him over, then simply let him join in |

**Is the narrator rich?** No. The NCERT questions ask this directly. Mrs Pumphrey arrives in a car of "about thirty feet of gleaming black metal" with a chauffeur, while the narrator has his own, much smaller car and shares a modest surgery with partners. That is why a fortnight of eggs, wine and brandy feels like a treat to the household. He is a hard-working professional, not a wealthy man, which makes his honesty and common sense more admirable.

**How does he treat the dog?** Kindly, but not sentimentally. He wraps Tricki in a blanket, speaks to him gently ("Poor old lad"), gives him water, a warm bed and company, and lets him recover on his own terms. He cures him by treating him like a dog, not like a pet child.

## Setting and theme {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10"}

**Setting.** Mrs Pumphrey's grand house and car show wealth and comfort; the vet's surgery, with its yard, garden and a pack of dogs, shows the plain, active life that keeps animals (and people) healthy. The story moves from the first setting to the second and back again. The change of place is the cure.

**Theme 1: love without sense.** Mrs Pumphrey wants the best for Tricki, but her idea of the best is more food, more clothes and more comfort. The story shows that real care sometimes means refusing a request and making hard choices. Her loving "extras" nearly harm the very creature she adores.

**Theme 2: the simple cure.** Nature, food in measure and exercise do what money cannot. Tricki recovers by being allowed to be a dog.

**Theme 3: a gentle satire on the rich.** The story is lightly mocking of Mrs Pumphrey, not cruel. She can order bottles of wine and a chauffeur, yet she cannot see what is plainly in front of her. The humour comes from the contrast between her extravagance and the narrator's calm practicality.

**Theme 4: the vet's tact and a small compromise.** Mr Herriot does not tell her the truth in so many words ("it was only exercise and plain food") because it would hurt her and lose him a good client. He also does not correct her about the "surgery". Whether this is tact or a small dishonesty is a matter you can argue either way in a board answer.

:::qc
q: In the story, why is the narrator's treatment of Tricki described as "common sense" and not medicine?
options: ["He gives Tricki an injection that works quickly", "He removes the cause of the illness (overeating and no exercise) instead of adding a drug", "He sends Tricki to another doctor"]
answer: 1
right: Yes. The cause was too much food and too little exercise, so the cure is the reverse: simple food and plenty of running about. No medicine is needed.
wrong: The story clearly says Tricki had no medicinal treatment. Think about what caused the problem and what would undo it.
:::

## The author's craft {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**The first-person narrator.** Mr Herriot tells the story as "I", so we share his private thoughts: his worry at the start ("I was really worried about Tricki this time"), his plan, and his amused delight at the wine and brandy. This lets him comment without lecturing. He can say "Tricki's only fault was greed" in one flat sentence and let the reader draw the larger conclusion about the owner.

**Humour by exaggeration.** Hyperbole is deliberate overstatement. The household "roused" to carry out beds, bowls and coats; Mrs Pumphrey "almost swooned" and "everybody was in tears" as a dog goes off for a fortnight. The grand black car is "about thirty feet" long. The comedy lies in how disproportionate the fuss is.

**Humour by understatement.** Understatement is the opposite: saying less than is meant. Mrs Pumphrey calls malt, cod-liver oil and a nightly bowl of Horlicks "nothing much really". The narrator's mild phrase "I tried to sound severe" hides how hard he finds it to be strict with her.

**Comparison.** Tricki at the start is "like a bloated sausage with a leg at each corner", a simile that is funny and cruel at once. Later he is "fighting like a tiger" for his dinner, a simile that reverses the picture of the dog we met in the first scene. The pair of comparisons maps the change in Tricki neatly.

**Structure and contrast.** The story's two halves mirror each other. In the first, the dog is fed too much, carried and fussed over; in the second, he runs, fights and hunts. Even the two cars (the vet's small one and the lady's huge black one) are placed in contrast.

**Irony in the title and last line.** "A triumph of surgery" is Mrs Pumphrey's own phrase, spoken in tears of thanks. The reader knows there was no surgery, so the praise misses the point. It is also gently ironic that the narrator and his partners enjoy the "convalescent" gifts while the dog, who is meant to receive them, never sees any of it.

:::qc
q: Which technique is used when Mrs Pumphrey describes a nightly bowl of Horlicks, malt and cod-liver oil as "nothing much really"?
options: ["Hyperbole", "Understatement", "Metaphor"]
answer: 1
right: Right. She says far less than the truth, which makes the reader smile. Understatement plays down a big thing.
wrong: She is shrinking something large, not blowing it up. Which term means making less of a thing than it deserves?
:::

## Writing a character sketch {lvl=2 id=s-sketch label="Class 10"}

Board questions on this chapter often ask "What kind of person is the narrator?" or "Describe Mrs Pumphrey". Use a three-step structure.

| Step | What to write | Mrs Pumphrey example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Trait | One quality in a short phrase | She is loving but foolishly indulgent |
| 2. Evidence | A detail from the text (short quotation or paraphrase) | She cannot bear to refuse Tricki cream cakes and chocolates |
| 3. Comment | What it tells us or why it matters | Her kindness is harmful; she needs advice and does not take it |

A model answer to "Is the narrator tactful as well as full of common sense?" can run:

*Mr Herriot is tactful because he never humiliates Mrs Pumphrey. He asks questions politely, tries "to sound severe" without being rude, and later sends her happy reports about "the latest bulletins" rather than the plain truth that Tricki is just playing. He is commonsensical because he sees the real cause of the illness at once, and cures the dog by changing his diet and giving him exercise. Together, the two qualities show a vet who understands both animals and their owners.*

## Exam answering and the "Talk about it" prompts {lvl=2 id=s-exam label="Class 10"}

The "Talk about it" prompts are open-ended: you give an opinion and defend it. A reliable method is *Opinion, reason, example, closing thought*.

| Prompt | One way in |
|---|---|
| Are such people merely silly, or can their actions cause harm? | Say they can cause harm: Tricki nearly pays with his health; the staff are made to run errands; and an unchecked habit of spoiling someone can spread to others. |
| Are there parents like Mrs Pumphrey? | Yes: parents who can never say no, give a child money or sweets for everything, or do the child's work. Say, politely, that this can weaken the child rather than help. |
| What would you have done as staff or neighbour? | Gently suggest a walk or a cut in treats; if refused, keep quiet but not join in. Add that everyone lives with the lady's moods, so life would be both comfortable and frustrating. |
| What would you have done in the narrator's place? | Give the same advice, but be firmer with her and explain the reason, so she can care for Tricki herself. |

These are model suggestions, not the only acceptable answers.

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not say Mr Herriot operated on Tricki. He gives no treatment at all; the point of the title is that none was needed. {lvl=1}
- Do not call Mrs Pumphrey cruel or wicked. She loves Tricki; her fault is a lack of sense. {lvl=2}
- A character sketch needs a trait, evidence and a comment; a list of adjectives alone earns few marks. {lvl=2}
- When explaining irony, name both sides: what she says (surgery) and what happened (diet and exercise). {lvl=2}
- Do not retell the whole story for a short-answer question. Pick the one or two events that answer it. {lvl=1}
- In opinion answers, say clearly that it is your view, give a reason, and keep to the text. {lvl=2}
- Do not quote long passages. A short phrase with your own comment is better than a copied paragraph. {lvl=3}
:::
