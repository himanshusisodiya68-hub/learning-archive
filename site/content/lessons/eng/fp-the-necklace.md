## The story in order {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

Matilda Loisel is a pretty young woman born into a family of clerks, with no dowry and no chance of meeting a rich husband. She marries a small clerk in the office of the Board of Education. She is not unkind, but she is unhappy: she feels born for fine dinners, silver and dresses, and she hates her worn chairs and her husband's pleasure in a simple potpie. She avoids a rich convent friend, Mme Forestier, because each visit leaves her in tears.

One evening her husband comes home pleased, with an invitation to a ball at the Minister's residence. Matilda is not glad. She says she has nothing to wear. Loisel gives her four hundred francs, which he had saved to buy a gun, and she buys a dress. Then she complains that she has no jewels. Loisel suggests borrowing from Mme Forestier. Matilda visits her, looks through several pieces and chooses a diamond necklace in a black satin box. Her friend lends it at once.

At the ball Matilda is the most admired woman in the room. She dances happily until four in the morning while her husband dozes. They leave in a hurry, because she does not want the rich women to see her cheap wraps. At home she finds the necklace is gone.

They search the route, ask the police and cab offices, and advertise a reward, but find nothing. Loisel tells Matilda to write to her friend that the clasp is broken and being repaired, to gain time. After a week they give up and buy a similar necklace at a Palais-Royal shop for thirty-six thousand francs. Loisel uses eighteen thousand left by his father and borrows the rest at heavy interest. Mme Forestier accepts the box without opening it, with only a cold remark that it came back late.

The Loisels now live in poverty to repay the loan. They let the maid go, move to an attic and do all the hard work themselves. Loisel keeps books and copies at night. Matilda scrubs, hauls water and haggles over every coin. After ten years the debt is cleared, and she looks old and rough. One Sunday in the Champs-Elysees she meets Mme Forestier, still young, who hardly recognises her. Matilda proudly tells her everything. Mme Forestier is touched and horrified: her necklace was a copy, worth at most five hundred francs.

## Characters {lvl=2 id=s-chars label="Class 10"}

| Character | What we learn | Evidence in the story |
|---|---|---|
| Matilda Loisel | Pretty, proud, discontented and vain at the start; courageous, hard-working and brave in her fall | She weeps over her apartment and throws down the invitation; later she pays the debt "completely, heroically" |
| M. Loisel | Loving, thrifty, practical, loyal; he accepts her wishes and shares the burden | He gives up his gun money; he suggests borrowing; he works nights to repay |
| Mme Forestier | Rich, generous, trusting and careless; a friend who lends without question | She says "Choose, my dear" and later, in a cold voice, complains about the delay |

**How Matilda changes.** At the start she wants things she does not have and ignores the husband who adores her. The loss strips her of both the dream and the ability to pretend. Ten years of work make her rough, with red hands and a loud voice. Yet she is no longer the same person: she accepts the debt without blaming anyone and smiles with "proud and simple joy" at having paid. She is wiser in character, if not in knowledge.

**Loisel's loyalty.** He never reproaches her. He is the one who suggests the borrowed jewels, searches the streets all night, and later works two extra jobs. His fault, if any, is that he goes along with the plan to hide the loss instead of urging honesty.

## Theme: vanity, content and honesty {lvl=2 id=s-theme label="Class 10"}

**Vanity and discontent.** Matilda is unhappy before she loses anything. She measures her life against richer women and finds it small. This is why she cannot enjoy the invitation, and why she runs from the ball to hide her cheap wraps. Her discontent pushes her into borrowing the necklace, which sets the whole disaster moving.

**The cost of silence.** The real turning point is not the loss but the choice made afterwards. Having lost the necklace, the Loisels have two options: tell Mme Forestier the truth, or hide it. They choose to hide it, first with a false story about a broken clasp and then with a replacement. Ten years of toil follow from that decision.

**Appearance against reality.** The ball is an appearance: borrowed jewels on a woman in a new dress. The necklace is an appearance: it looks like diamonds and is paste. Even Matilda's "success" depends on how things look. The story asks us to look beyond surfaces.

**Fate and chance.** Matilda herself reflects, in the ten-year paragraph, that a small thing can ruin or save a person. A lost necklace, a missing cab number, a friend who never mentions the jewels were false: small events decide a whole life. Note that chance only sets the stage. The Loisels' own choices write the rest.

**Honesty.** The loss happens through carelessness, but the long punishment comes through a decision not to be honest. The book's discussion topic "Honesty is the best policy" invites exactly this reading.

:::qc
q: According to the story, what really changes the Loisels' lives?
options: ["Losing the necklace itself", "Their decision to hide the loss and replace the necklace", "Mme Forestier's anger"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Losing the necklace was a mishap. Hiding it, and repaying a false debt at great cost, is what turns a mishap into ten years of ruin.
wrong: Think about what the Loisels could have done differently after the loss. The cause is their choice, not the accident alone.
:::

## Answering the book's questions {lvl=2 id=s-answering label="Class 10"}

A good board answer has a claim, evidence and a comment. Here is how to build one for the main questions.

| Question | Claim | Evidence to use | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| How did the necklace change their lives? | It took them from modest comfort to ten years of hard poverty | They dismiss the maid, move to an attic, wash and scrub; Loisel copies at night | A borrowed ornament ended up costing their youth |
| What caused Matilda's ruin? | Her vanity and her silence | She cannot be content; she borrows jewels; she hides the loss | She could have confessed the same night or the next morning |
| What if she had confessed? | Mme Forestier would probably have told her the necklace was false | The jewels cost under five hundred francs | The Loisels would have saved ten years, though Matilda would have felt embarrassed |

For "what would you do", always give a plan, not a feeling: tell the friend at once, offer to pay or replace what is fair, and ask her to check the real value.

## The author's craft {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Irony.** There are three layers. *Situational:* a woman who wanted to look wealthy works like a servant. *Dramatic:* the reader fears early that the choice to hide the loss will hurt, and Matilda does not. *Cosmic or tragic:* the whole price is paid for a trifle, and the truth reaches her only once she has nothing left to lose.

**The twist ending.** The last two lines change the meaning of everything before. Until then the reader sees a harsh but fair punishment for vanity and dishonesty. After it, the reader sees how pointless the sacrifice was. Notice that the twist works only because the author never tells us the jewels' real worth. Mme Forestier does not open the box, and Matilda never asks.

**Foreshadowing.** Three small hints prepare the ending. Mme Forestier lends the most valuable-looking piece without any worry. She does not examine the box when it returns. And the black satin box is described only by its look, never its worth. A careful reader can sense that something is not as it appears.

**Symbol.** The necklace stands for false value: shine without substance. It also stands for Matilda's wish to be seen as someone she is not. The dress and the ball are symbols of the same wish.

**Narrative voice and tone.** The narrator watches from outside and moves between characters without judging. Short statements of fact ("It was all over for her") deliver big blows. The tone is cool and ironic, and the author lets the facts do the work. The one reflective passage, on how small a thing can ruin or save, is the only time the narrator steps back to comment.

**Structure.** The story is built like a balance: a short, bright first half (invitation, ball) and a long, grey second half (ten years). The ball is described in a few lines, the debt in many paragraphs. The proportions themselves make the point that a night of pleasure costs years of pain.

:::qc
q: Why does the twist ending work so well?
options: ["The author has told us early that the necklace is false", "The reader never learns the true value until the final line, so it recasts the whole story", "Mme Forestier explains everything at the start"]
answer: 1
right: Exactly. The twist works because the truth is withheld until the last line, and then it turns a story of fair punishment into one of pointless sacrifice.
wrong: A twist depends on hidden information. Who finally reveals the truth, and when?
:::
