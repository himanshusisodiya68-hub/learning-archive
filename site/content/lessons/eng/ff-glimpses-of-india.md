## What the three pieces are about {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

**A Baker from Goa.** The writer remembers the Portuguese days and their famous loaves. The people who ate those loaves may be gone, but the makers, the furnaces and the baker's bamboo staff are still there. Bakers are called **paders**, and the son often carries on the father's trade. As a child, the writer ran to greet the baker every morning, not for the bread the maid bought, but for the bread-bangles he hoped to be given. The piece then shows how bread belongs to Goan life: the bol as a marriage gift, sandwiches at an engagement, cakes and bolinhas at Christmas. It describes the baker's old long frock (the kabai), his monthly bills pencilled on a wall, and his plump, prosperous look, which gives a joke: anyone round and well-fed is "like a baker".

**Coorg.** Between Mysore and Mangalore lies Coorg (Kodagu), the smallest district of Karnataka. It has evergreen rainforests, spices and coffee. The rains are heavy, so the pleasant season runs from September to March. The people are fiercely independent, possibly of Greek or Arabic descent, with a proud military record: the Coorg Regiment is among the most decorated in the Indian Army, and General Cariappa, the first Chief of the Indian Army, was a Coorgi. The Kaveri, wildlife, adventure sports (rafting, canoeing, rappelling, rock climbing, mountain biking), the Brahmagiri hills, the island of Nisargadhama and the Tibetan monks of Bylakuppe all feature. A fact file tells you how to reach the district.

**Tea from Assam.** Two classmates, Rajvir and Pranjol, travel by train to Pranjol's home in Upper Assam, where his father manages a tea garden. They drink tea from a platform vendor, and Rajvir quotes a startling figure about tea consumption. Pranjol is not thrilled by the tea bushes outside because he has grown up among them, while Rajvir is delighted. Rajvir shares two legends about tea's discovery, and says that it was first drunk in China and reached Europe late. At the Dhekiabari estate, he shows that he has read enough to know the second flush from May to July yields the best tea, and Pranjol's father is impressed.

## Reading the baker piece closely {lvl=2 id=s-baker label="Class 10 board"}

**Tone.** The writer is looking back, so the dominant tone is **nostalgia** (a fond, wistful memory). Phrases about the furnaces still burning, the sound that can "still be heard" and the fragrance he "can still recall" are all backward-looking. Hope sits beside it: "the son still carries on the family profession". Humour appears in the lines about tiger and tea, where the author jokes that nobody brushed their teeth because even the tiger never did. Lines about festivals and prosperity are plain, matter-of-fact reporting.

**Tone question, model approach.**

| Line | Tone | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thud and jingle of the bamboo can still be heard | nostalgic | the writer misses what is fading |
| Maybe the father is not alive but the son carries on | hopeful | the tradition continues |
| The tiger never brushed his teeth | funny | a playful excuse |
| Cakes and bolinhas are a must for Christmas | matter-of-fact | stating a custom |
| The baker and his family never starved | matter-of-fact | reporting a fact |

**Why the baker matters.** The piece argues that the baker is not just a seller but part of a village's rhythm. He comes twice a day, he appears at weddings, engagements and Christmas, and he is a figure in local jokes (the half pant, the plump look). When a trade enters speech and festival, it is part of the culture.

**Dress and money.** The kabai, a one-piece frock to the knees, gave way to short trousers and a shirt. Accounts were written in pencil on a wall and cleared at the end of the month, a small sign of trust between baker and customers.

:::qc
q: Which of these best describes the tone of "I can still recall the typical fragrance of those loaves"?
options: ["Angry", "Nostalgic", "Naughty"]
answer: 1
right: Right. The word "still" and the focus on a remembered smell show a fond look back at childhood.
wrong: A remembered smell from childhood, introduced with "still", points to a fond, wistful look at the past.
:::

## Reading Coorg closely {lvl=2 id=s-coorg label="Class 10 board"}

**Kind of writing.** This is **travel writing**. It uses a lively personal voice ("I do, however, prefer to step aside for wild elephants") but organises facts the way a guide does: location, climate, people, rivers, adventure, hills and a fact file.

**The Kodavus.** The text offers a *possible* origin, not a proven one: "possibly of Greek or Arabic descent". Two clues are given. The first is a story about Alexander's soldiers settling in the south after a return home became impractical. The second is the long black coat with an embroidered waist-belt, the kuppia, which looks like the kuffia worn by Arabs and Kurds. Their customs of marriage, religion and war are described as distinct from the Hindu mainstream. Hospitality, valour, the Coorg Regiment and the freedom to carry firearms without a licence round out the portrait.

**Answering "What do you know about...?"**

| Topic | What the text says |
|---|---|
| People | Fiercely independent, martial, hospitable, proud of their sons' and fathers' valour |
| Main crop | Coffee, grown in estates under tree canopies |
| Sports | River rafting, canoeing, rappelling, rock climbing, mountain biking, trekking |
| Animals | Macaques, Malabar squirrels, langurs, slender loris, elephants, mahaseer fish, kingfishers |
| Getting there | Gateway is Madikeri; airports at Mangalore (135 km) and Bangalore (260 km); railheads at Mysore, Mangalore and Hassan; about 250 to 260 km by road from Bangalore |

:::qc
q: Why does the writer say the Kodavus are "possibly" of Greek or Arabic descent?
options: ["The origin is stated only as a theory and a story, not as proven fact", "The writer has proved it by research", "The Kodavus themselves deny it"]
answer: 0
right: Yes. The word "possibly" and the phrase "as one story goes" mark this as an unproven theory, so a careful reader repeats it as a theory too.
wrong: Look at the wording: "possibly", "as one story goes", "the theory of Arab origin". These tell you the origin is a theory, not proof.

## Reading Tea from Assam closely {lvl=2 id=s-tea label="Class 10 board"}

**The two boys.** Rajvir is curious, well-read and polite; he does his homework and wants to learn more. Pranjol is a little bored by what he has seen all his life: an everyday thing looks ordinary to the person who lives with it. They are fond classmates who tease each other ("Tell me another!").

**How the facts arrive.** Instead of lecturing, the writer lets Rajvir talk. We learn the tea legends (a Chinese emperor's boiling water and a few leaves; Bodhidharma's eyelids), that tea was first drunk in China, and that the words *tea*, *chai* and *chini* come from Chinese. That information comes dressed as conversation, which is easier to follow than a list of facts.

**Setting.** From the train, green paddy fields give way to tea bushes against wooded hills, with tall shade-trees and doll-like pluckers. Rajvir's excitement stands against Pranjol's calm, and the ugly factory building with smoke from its chimneys is the quiet contrast to the green.

**Tea's age.** The text gives 2700 B.C. for the first drink in China; that figure is the character's claim in the story, so quote it as his statement [Unverified as history].

:::qc
q: Why does Pranjol not share Rajvir's excitement on seeing the tea garden?
options: ["He dislikes tea", "He was born and brought up on a plantation, so it is familiar", "He is busy reading a detective book"]
answer: 1
right: Correct. The text says he had been born and brought up on a plantation; the book also shows him reading, but that is not the reason given.
wrong: Re-read the sentence that begins "Pranjol, who had been born and brought up...". The text gives the reason.
:::

## Comparing two pieces on the same subject {lvl=2 id=s-compare label="Class 10 board"}

The book places the writer's baker passage beside an extract adapted from Nandakumar Kamat's article on Goan paders. A board-style answer looks at four points.

| Point | The chapter's passage | Kamat's extract |
|---|---|---|
| Voice | Personal ("our elders", childhood memories) | Reporting; impersonal and informative |
| Focus | Feeling, sights and sounds, nostalgia | Why paders survived (door-to-door delivery, family knowledge) and the Portuguese gift of leavened, oven-baked bread |
| Facts | Furnaces still burn, son carries on the trade | The same survival fact, plus the reason for it |
| Picture of the baker | A warm, vivid, cheerful figure | A trade explained, with less human detail |

Both give a true picture. The facts are broadly the same, but the first makes you *see and feel* a baker while the second makes you *understand* why the trade endures. That is the point of the question: purpose decides style.

**Brochure into personal account.** An impersonal brochure says, "The beach has golden sand and palm trees." A personal account adds "I woke to the smell of frying fish, and the sand was already warm at seven". Add at least one sense (sound, smell, touch), one person and one feeling.

## Language work in this chapter {lvl=2 id=s-language label="Class 10 board"}

**1. Collocations.** A collocation is a pair or group of words that sound right together. Native speakers choose them by habit, not by rule: *tales of valour*, *coastal town*, *rope bridge*, *evergreen forest*. Odd pairs sound strange though the grammar is fine (*a pleasant pillow*).

| Noun | Sensible adjectives from the box | Why |
|---|---|---|
| culture | unique, ancient | cultures can be special and old |
| tradition | ancient, unique | traditions have age |
| experience | unforgettable, unique, terrible | an experience can be strong or rare |
| surprise | sudden, unforgettable | a surprise comes without warning |
| weather | terrible | weather is judged good or bad |

**2. Nouns built from verb + particle.** A verb and a small word can fuse into a noun: *downpour* (a heavy fall of rain), *upkeep* (the cost or work of maintaining), *dropout* (a person who leaves a course), *walk-in* (an unscheduled visit or interview), *breakthrough* (a major discovery), *bypass* (a road that goes round a town), *outlook* (a way of looking at life), *overthrow* (to remove a government). Note that some are written solid, some with a hyphen, so check a dictionary.

**3. -ing versus -ed adjectives.** One word is the **cause**, the other the **feeling**.

| Form | Describes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| -ing | the thing, event or person that causes the feeling | Chess is an interesting game. The trek is an exciting idea. |
| -ed / -en | the person who feels it | I am interested in chess. We are excited about the trek. |

A quick test: if it fits "makes me feel..." use -ing; if it fits "I feel..." use -ed. *The match was boring* but *I was bored*.

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["The film was very bored.", "The film was very boring.", "I was very boring after the film."]
answer: 1
right: Right. The film causes the feeling, so it is boring; a person who feels it would be bored.
wrong: The -ing form describes what causes the feeling (the film), while the -ed form describes who feels it.
:::

## Craft: how genre shapes what is shown {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Three genres, three contracts.** A **pen-portrait** promises a person seen through memory, so it can be affectionate and selective. **Travel writing** promises a place plus practical advice, so it mixes feeling with facts and ends with a fact file. A **narrative with dialogue** promises characters, so information has to come out of what they say. Reading well means asking which contract the writer signed.

**Sensory writing.** The baker piece is built from sound (*jhang, jhang*, the thud and jingle) and smell (fragrance of loaves). Coorg uses sight (misty hills) and smell (coffee in the air). Tea from Assam uses sight (a sea of tea bushes). Good descriptive writing picks one or two senses and gives them room.

**Humour and understatement.** The tiger and tea joke is a small piece of comic understatement: a child's logic given in an adult's calm voice. It keeps nostalgia from turning sentimental.

**Hidden history.** The chapter mentions the Portuguese, Alexander's army and the British-era planters only briefly, but each hints at how cultures mix. Goa's bread came with the Portuguese; Coorg's coat may echo a traveller's story; tea moved from China to India and Europe. The country the chapter shows is a country of arrivals and blends. (The tea passage in the exercises says Indians at first shunned the drink and that the British began plantations in Assam and Darjeeling; treat the exact history as the passage's claim.)

**Writing from your own experience.** Strong travel writing shows, it does not label. "The temple was beautiful" tells; "the brass bells shook every time a pilgrim passed" shows. Use that habit whenever the exam asks you to describe a place.

:::qc
q: Which sentence shows rather than tells?
options: ["The town was very peaceful.", "By nine the shutters were still down and a single dog slept in the middle of the road.", "Everyone loved the town."]
answer: 1
right: Yes. The dog and the shutters let the reader reach the idea of peace without being told.
wrong: A sentence that shows gives concrete details the reader can see. Which option does that?
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not say the paders vanished with the Portuguese; the text says the eaters of loaves might have vanished but the makers are still there. {lvl=1}
- Do not state the Kodavus' Greek or Arab origin as fact; the text calls it a possibility and a story. {lvl=2}
- Do not confuse the two tea legends: the Chinese emperor's boiled water belongs to China, while Bodhidharma's eyelids belong to the Indian legend. {lvl=2}
- On a tone question, giving only "happy" or "sad" earns little; use nostalgic, hopeful, funny or matter-of-fact, and quote a phrase. {lvl=2}
- Mixing -ing and -ed forms is the commonest slip: the film is boring, you are bored. {lvl=2}
- Do not copy whole sentences from the chapter in a writing task; use your own details and at most a short phrase in quotation marks. {lvl=3}
:::
