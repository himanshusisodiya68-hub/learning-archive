## Why English needs modals {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

A plain verb states a fact: *Asha swims.* But we also need to say things about the fact. Is Asha able to swim? Allowed to? Likely to? Does she have to? These are not facts about the world; they are the speaker's **attitude** to the fact: how sure, how willing, how obliged.

Rather than invent a new verb for each shade, English uses a small set of helpers placed before the main verb. Compare:

- Asha swims. (a fact)
- Asha **can** swim. (ability)
- Asha **may** swim. (permission, or a possibility)
- Asha **must** swim. (duty)
- Asha **should** swim. (advice)

The main verb *swim* never changes. Only the helper changes, and with it the speaker's message. That is the idea of a **modal verb**: a helper that adds a mood, a degree of certainty or obligation, to another verb.

## The modal verbs {lvl=1 id=s-list label="Foundation"}

The core modals are:

**can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must**

Close relatives that behave like modals in some ways: **ought to, need, dare, used to, had better**.

## Three rules that set modals apart {lvl=1 id=s-rules label="Foundation"}

Modals are helpers, so they follow different rules from ordinary verbs.

| Rule | Ordinary verb | Modal |
|---|---|---|
| No -s with he, she, it | She plays. | She **can** play. (not *cans*) |
| The next verb is the bare base form (no to, no -s, no -ing) | She wants **to play**. | She can **play**. (not *can to play*, *can plays*, *can playing*) |
| Questions and negatives need no do/does/did | **Does** she play? She **does not** play. | **Can** she play? She **cannot** play. |

Why these rules? A modal is already a helper, like do or have. A helper does not need another helper, and it carries no meaning of person or time of its own, so there is nothing for -s or -ed to attach to. The main verb that follows has no job except to name the action, so it stays bare.

Questions are made by moving the modal to the front: *Can she play? Should we go? Must you leave?* The negative is formed with **not** after the modal: *cannot* (one word), *may not*, *must not*, *should not*. Short forms: *can't, couldn't, mayn't (rare), mustn't, shouldn't, won't, wouldn't, shan't.*

:::note title="The one-word trap" tone=warn
*Cannot* is written as one word. *Can not* as two words is acceptable only when *not* belongs to something else: "You can not only read but also write." In the board paper, write *cannot* or *can't*.
:::

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["She can sings well.", "She can to sing well.", "She can sing well.", "She cans sing well."]
answer: 2
right: Right. After a modal the verb is the bare base form.
wrong: A modal is followed by the plain base verb: no -s, no to, no -ing. Only "can sing" fits.
:::

## What each modal does {lvl=1 id=s-uses label="Foundation"}

Most modals have more than one job. Here are the main ones.

| Idea | Modal | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Ability | can, could | *Meera can solve this puzzle. When I was six I could ride a bicycle.* |
| Permission | can, may, could | *May I borrow your dictionary? Can I leave early?* |
| Request | could, would, can, will | *Could you pass the salt? Would you open the door, please?* |
| Possibility | may, might, could | *It may rain tonight. She might be at the library.* |
| Advice | should, ought to | *You should drink more water. You ought to apologise.* |
| Duty, rule, necessity | must, have to | *Students must wear uniform. Passengers must show tickets.* |
| Offer, suggestion | shall, can | *Shall I carry your bag? Shall we go out for tea?* |
| Promise, decision, prediction | will | *I will help you. Tomorrow will be sunny.* |

### Can and could, may and might

**Can** is for present ability and informal permission; **could** is for past ability (*I could swim at five*) and for polite requests (*Could I sit here?*). A general rule: *could* is not the past of can in every use. For a single past success, use *was able to*: *The firefighter was able to reach the top floor* (not *could reach*, when it is one particular achievement).

**May** and **might** are both used for possibility. *Might* suggests a weaker chance, and *may* a stronger one, though the difference is small. For permission, **may** is the polite and formal choice: *May I come in?*

### Shall and will

*Shall* is used with **I** and **we** for offers and suggestions: *Shall I open the window? Shall we begin?* It is not common with other subjects except in formal rules and legal texts (*The tenant shall pay rent on the first of the month*). In everyday English, **will** is used for future with all persons: *She will come. They will leave.*

### Should and ought to

Both give advice or say what is the right thing. *You should save some money. You ought to thank her.* The negative of *ought to* is *ought not to*. Many speakers avoid it and say *shouldn't* instead.

### Must and have to

*Must* often expresses the speaker's own feeling of duty (*I must lose weight*) or a rule (*Cars must stop at red lights*). *Have to* says the duty comes from outside (*I have to wake up at six for school*). In the present, the difference is small. In the **past** and the **future**, *must* has no form, so *had to* and *will have to* are used.

### Would

*Would* has several jobs: polite request (*Would you mind waiting?*), past habit (*Every summer we would visit our grandparents*), reported *will* (*She said she would come*), and the result clause of a conditional (*If I had a car, I would drive to school*).

:::qc
q: Which modal fits an offer? "___ I carry your bag?"
options: ["Shall", "Must", "Might", "Ought"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Shall with I or we is the standard way to make an offer or a suggestion.
wrong: An offer is a question about what you might do for someone. With I, that is "Shall I ...?"

## Must not and do not have to {lvl=2 id=s-neg label="Class 10"}

This is the most tested contrast in the section, because the two look as if they should mean the same thing and do not.

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **must not** (mustn't) | Forbidden. You are not allowed. | *You mustn't smoke in the hospital.* |
| **do not have to** (don't have to) | Not necessary. You may if you wish. | *You don't have to wear a tie.* |
| **need not** (needn't) | Not necessary (same as don't have to, but more formal). | *You needn't come tomorrow.* |
| **cannot / can't** | Not able, or not allowed. | *You can't park here.* |

Think of a gate. *Must not* is a closed gate. *Do not have to* is an open gate that you are not forced to walk through.

## Modals for certainty: deduction {lvl=2 id=s-deduce label="Class 10"}

Modals can also tell how sure the speaker is about something. They are arranged on a scale from nearly certain to barely possible.

| Certainty | Affirmative | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| About 95% | must | can't / cannot |
| About 50% | may, might, could | may not, might not |

- *The lights are on, so they **must** be at home.* (I am nearly sure.)
- *That **can't** be Ravi. He is in Chennai.* (I am nearly sure it is not.)
- *She **may** be late; the roads are flooded.* (It is possible.)

Notice that the opposite of *must* in this sense is **can't**, not *mustn't*. *He must be tired* has the negative *He can't be tired*.

## Modals in the past {lvl=2 id=s-past label="Class 10"}

To talk about a **past** situation with a modal, use **modal + have + V3** (the past participle). The modal keeps the speaker's attitude and *have + V3* moves the situation into the past.

| Pattern | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| must have + V3 | I am nearly sure it happened | *The road is wet. It must have rained.* |
| can't have + V3 | I am nearly sure it did not happen | *She can't have seen us, or she would have waved.* |
| may / might / could have + V3 | It is possible it happened | *He might have missed the bus.* |
| should have + V3 | It was the right thing, but it did not happen (criticism, regret) | *You should have told me earlier.* |
| shouldn't have + V3 | It happened, but was wrong | *I shouldn't have shouted.* |
| needn't have + V3 | It happened, but was unnecessary | *We needn't have hurried; the train was late.* |
| could have + V3 | It was possible but did not happen | *She could have won if she had practised.* |

Two pairs are easy to confuse:

- *He **should have** called* means he did not call, and that was a fault.
- *He **needn't have** called* means he did call, and it was not required.

:::worked title="Reading the situation behind the modal"
*"The cake is gone. The dog must have eaten it."* The speaker did not see it, but the evidence points one way, so the guess is strong: **must have + V3**.

*"The door was locked, so she can't have gone out."* The speaker is nearly sure she did not go out: **can't have + V3**.

*"You went to the shop by taxi? You needn't have; it is only two minutes away."* The action happened but was wasted effort: **needn't have + V3**.
:::

:::qc
q: "I waited for an hour and he never came. He ___ me."
options: ["should have called", "needn't have called", "must call", "can have called"]
answer: 0
right: Right. He did not call and the speaker blames him, so should have + V3.
wrong: He never came or called, and the speaker is annoyed. The action did not happen and was expected, so should have called.
:::

## When a modal has no form: substitutes {lvl=2 id=s-sub label="Class 10"}

Modals have no infinitive (no *to can*), no -ing form (no *musting*) and no past participle. Therefore two modals cannot stand together, and a modal cannot follow *to* or *be*. When a form is needed, borrow it from a substitute.

| Modal | Substitute | Example |
|---|---|---|
| can | be able to | *She will be able to drive next year.* (not *will can*) |
| must | have to | *We had to leave early.* *We will have to wait.* |
| may | be allowed to / be permitted to | *Students are allowed to use calculators.* |
| shall / will | be going to | *She is going to apply.* |

*Hoping to be able to attend* is correct. *Hoping to can attend* is not.

## Other modal-like verbs {lvl=2 id=s-quasi label="Class 10"}

**Need and dare** have two lives. As modals they appear mostly in questions and negatives: *You needn't worry. Need I go? How dare you speak like that?* As ordinary verbs they take -s, to and do-support: *He needs to rest. She doesn't need to come. They did not dare to jump.*

**Used to** means a past habit or state that no longer holds: *We used to live in Kanpur.* (We no longer do.) Its question and negative use *did* and **use to**: *Did you use to play chess? I did not use to like tea.* Do not confuse it with *be used to* + -ing (be familiar with): *She is used to waking up early.*

**Would and used to for past habits.** Both describe repeated past actions: *Every evening we would play cricket.* Only **used to** works for past states: *The house used to belong to my uncle.* (Not *would belong*.)

**Had better** gives strong advice with a warning: *You had better leave now, or you will miss the bus.* It uses the bare verb with no to, and the short form is *you'd better*.

**Would rather** shows preference: *I would rather walk than take a bus.* The verb after *would rather* is also bare.

:::qc
q: Which sentence is correct?
options: ["We used to played here as children.", "We used to play here as children.", "We use to played here as children.", "We were use to play here as children."]
answer: 1
right: Right. Used to is followed by the base verb.
wrong: Used to takes the base form of the next verb: used to play. Check for a stray -ed or -ing.
:::

## Modals in reported speech {lvl=2 id=s-reported label="Class 10"}

When the reporting verb is in the past (said, told, asked), the modal usually moves one step back, as other tenses do.

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| "I can swim." | He said that he **could** swim. |
| "I may come." | She said that she **might** come. |
| "I will help you." | He said that he **would** help me. |
| "Shall I open the door?" | She asked whether she **should** open the door. |
| "You must finish today." | The teacher said that I **must** finish that day. (or *had to*) |
| "You should rest." | The doctor said that I **should** rest. |
| "I could swim as a child." | He said that he **could** swim as a child. |

**No change** for modals already in the past form: *could, might, would, should, ought to*. *Must* usually stays *must* or becomes *had to* when it is an obligation.

Requests with *can/could/would you* are often best reported with a verb of request: "Could you open the window?" becomes *She asked me to open the window.* Suggestions with *shall we* become *suggested + -ing*: "Shall we go to the park?" becomes *He suggested going to the park.*

:::qc
q: Report: He said, "I may be late."
options: ["He said that he might be late.", "He said that he may be late.", "He said that he would be late.", "He said that he can be late."]
answer: 0
right: Right. May shifts to might in reported speech after a past reporting verb.
wrong: After said, a modal moves one step back. May becomes might.
:::

## Modals in the board's questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

Modals appear in the board paper in four ways.

1. **Gap filling.** Choose the modal that fits the meaning: certainty, duty, advice, permission, ability. Read the whole sentence and ask what the speaker is trying to say.
2. **Editing / error correction.** One word is wrong in a line. Look first at the word after each modal: it must be a bare base verb. Then check double modals (*will can*), a missing *have* in past modals (*should gone*), and a wrong V2 in place of V3 (*should have went*).
3. **Sentence transformation.** Rewrite a sentence using a modal, or without it, keeping the meaning: *It is necessary for you to submit the form* becomes *You must submit the form.*
4. **Reported speech.** Backshift the modal correctly.

A routine for error correction:

- Underline every modal.
- Check what follows it: base verb? no to? no -ing?
- If the sentence is about the past, is it modal + have + V3?
- Check that the question or negative has no extra do/does/did.

:::worked title="Rewriting with modals"
*It is necessary for every visitor to sign the register.* Necessity from a rule: **must**. *Every visitor must sign the register.*

*Perhaps the match will be cancelled.* Possibility: **may**. *The match may be cancelled.*

*I advise you to see a dentist.* Advice: **should**. *You should see a dentist.*

*Is it possible for you to lend me a pencil?* Polite request: **could** or **can**. *Could you lend me a pencil?*

*It is not necessary for you to bring a gift.* No obligation: **needn't** or **don't have to**. *You needn't bring a gift.*
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "You should to see a doctor about that cough."
options: ["should to see should be should see", "see should be seeing", "a doctor should be the doctor"]
answer: 0
right: Right. After a modal the base verb stands alone, without to.
wrong: Look at what comes between should and see. A modal is followed directly by the bare verb, so to must go.
:::

## Beyond the board: finer shades of modality {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### Politeness is a ladder

Past modal forms are used as present-time polite forms because distance in tense creates distance between speakers. The more distant the form, the more polite the request:

*Can you help?* (casual) < *Could you help?* (polite) < *Would you be able to help?* (more formal) < *I wonder whether you might be able to help.* (very formal and tentative).

### Might and may in the past

*May have + V3* and *might have + V3* are equal as guesses about the past. But *might have* alone can show a missed chance: *You might have been killed!* (it did not happen, but it was possible). Compare *You could have been killed.*

### The subjunctive-like uses

A few set phrases keep an older use of **may** for wishes: *May you succeed! May God bless you.* They put *may* before the subject. This is used in speeches and greeting cards, not in daily talk.

### Will for habit and assumption

*Will* can describe typical behaviour or a guess: *Accidents will happen.* (they always do) *That will be the postman.* (I assume it is) The negative of this use is *won't*: *The engine won't start.* means it refuses to, even as a stubborn object.

### Modal tone in writing

Hedging with modals makes writing more careful. Compare *This proves that the policy failed* with *This suggests that the policy may have failed.* In an essay or a review, a well-placed *may*, *might* or *would* is a sign of balance. Overuse, though, makes writing weak: do not hedge every line.

### Could and was able to

*Could* is for general ability in the past (*As a child, she could climb any tree*). For one specific success, use *was able to* or *managed to*: *Despite the storm, the crew was able to land the plane.* In the negative, *couldn't* works for both general and specific cases: *She couldn't open the door.*

:::qc
q: Which sentence shows a single past success correctly?
options: ["Though the road was blocked, we were able to reach the village.", "Though the road was blocked, we could reach the village once.", "Though the road was blocked, we can reach the village."]
answer: 0
right: Right. For one particular achievement in the past, was or were able to is the natural choice.
wrong: A single past success uses was/were able to or managed to; could is for general ability, and can is present.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. Modals add the speaker's attitude to a verb: ability, permission, possibility, advice, duty, certainty. {lvl=1}
2. The core modals are can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would and must. {lvl=1}
3. After a modal the verb is the bare base form: no -s, no to, no -ing. Questions and negatives need no do/does/did. {lvl=1}
4. Can is ability; may is permission (formal) or possibility; shall is for offers with I and we; should and ought to give advice; must is duty. {lvl=1}
5. Must not forbids; do not have to and needn't mean there is no obligation. {lvl=2}
6. For certainty: must (nearly sure it is), can't (nearly sure it is not), may, might, could (possible). {lvl=2}
7. Past modals use modal + have + V3: must have, can't have, may have, should have, needn't have. {lvl=2}
8. Two modals cannot stand together. Use substitutes: be able to, have to, be allowed to. {lvl=2}
9. In reported speech after a past reporting verb, can becomes could, may becomes might, will becomes would, shall becomes should or would; must stays or becomes had to. {lvl=2}
10. Polite requests rise in formality from can to could to would you be able to; use was able to for a single past success. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "She can sings", "He must to go", "You should to see": the verb after a modal is bare. {lvl=1}
- "Do you can swim?" and "Does she must go?": modals form questions by moving to the front. {lvl=1}
- "She cans", "He musts", "It mays": modals take no -s. {lvl=1}
- "You mustn't come" when you mean there is no need: use "You needn't come" or "You don't have to come". {lvl=2}
- "He should have went": after have, use V3, "gone". {lvl=2}
- "He must be tired" negated as "He mustn't be tired": for a negative deduction use "can't". {lvl=2}
- "I will can help you next week": two modals together; use "I will be able to help you". {lvl=2}
- "He said that he can come": backshift after a past reporting verb, "could come". {lvl=2}
- "Would" for a past state ("I would live in Agra as a child"): use "used to" for states. {lvl=2}
- Writing "can not" as two words in a plain sentence: write "cannot" or "can't". {lvl=2}
- Confusing "should have done" (it did not happen) with "needn't have done" (it happened but was unnecessary). {lvl=3}
:::
