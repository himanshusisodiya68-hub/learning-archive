## Why writers use devices {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Think about telling a friend that your day was bad. You can say, "It was a bad day." Or you can say, "It was one of those days when even the bus ran away from me." The second version shows a mood, makes your friend smile, and tells more than the facts.

That is the whole idea of a **literary device**: a deliberate technique that makes a text say more than its plain words. Writers use them for three jobs.

1. **To make you see.** A fresh comparison turns a flat statement into a picture.
2. **To make you feel.** A hint of trouble to come makes a quiet scene tense.
3. **To make you think.** An ending that reverses what you expected makes you ask why.

Devices fall into two families, and it helps to keep them apart.

| Family | It works on | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Narrative devices** | the story as a whole: who tells it, in what order, what is hinted, how people and events are shown | point of view, irony, foreshadowing, flashback, characterisation, conflict, symbolism |
| **Figures of speech** | a phrase or sentence: how the words compare, exaggerate, sound or stretch their meaning | simile, metaphor, personification, alliteration, hyperbole, onomatopoeia |

You will notice the two families work together. A symbol is built from a story's events; a metaphor is built from its words.

## Point of view: who is telling? {lvl=1 id=s-pov label="Foundation"}

Every story has a **narrator**, the voice that tells it. The narrator's position is the **point of view**, and it decides what you can know.

| Point of view | Marker | What you get | What you lose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First person** | I, we | closeness: you share one person's thoughts and feelings | you know only what that person knows or admits |
| **Third person limited** | he, she, they; the narrator follows one character | you enter one mind while the story is told from outside | other characters stay mysterious |
| **Third person omniscient** | he, she, they; the narrator sees into many minds | the big picture, and what no one character knows | less personal feeling, sometimes more distance |

Examples from the prose chapters, in our own words:

- In the **Anne Frank** diary pages, Anne writes "I" and addresses her diary as a friend. We hear her fears, moods and small jokes directly, and we cannot see the world beyond what she sees from hiding.
- In **Mijbil the Otter** and in the black aeroplane story, a first-person narrator tells of an animal and a flight that leave him astonished. We feel his surprise, and we can only guess whether he is reading the events correctly.
- In **A Letter to God**, the narrator stands outside the farmer and the postmaster and sees into both of them: we know that Lencho truly believes in God, and that the postmaster is moved and amused. A first-person voice could not tell us both.

:::note title="A quick test for point of view" tone=tip
Underline the pronouns used by the narrator, not by the characters in dialogue. Narrator says I or we: first person. Narrator says he or she and tells you what one person thinks: third limited. Narrator tells you what several people think: omniscient.
:::

:::qc
q: A story says, "Mira thought the room looked friendly, but Karan secretly hated it." Which point of view is this?
options: ["Third person omniscient", "First person", "Third person limited", "Second person"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The narrator enters the minds of both Mira and Karan, so the story is told from above, not from one character.
wrong: Check whose thoughts the narrator reports. Both Mira and Karan are opened up, so the narrator knows more than any one character. That is omniscient.
:::

## Characterisation: showing who someone is {lvl=1 id=s-char label="Foundation"}

A writer can tell you a character is brave, or show it. Both are **characterisation**.

- **Direct**: the narrator says it. "Asha was a patient girl."
- **Indirect**: you work it out from what the person says, does, thinks, looks like, or how others treat them.

Compare two ways of showing a careful person: "Hari was careful" and "Hari checked the gas knob twice before leaving, then went back to check it a third time." The second is stronger because you reached the conclusion yourself.

Indirect characterisation has five main sources, easy to remember as **SALTO**: what the character **S**ays, **A**ctions, **L**ooks, **T**houghts, and how **O**thers react.

Examples from the prose chapters, in our own words:

- **Lencho** (A Letter to God) is shown by action: he stands in his field, reads the sky for rain, and, after the hailstorm, writes a letter to God asking for money. We learn about his faith, his hope and his stubbornness without being told.
- **Valli** (Madam Rides the Bus) is shown by her choices: a young girl who plans a bus trip by saving coins, goes alone, and is determined to see the world her own way.
- **The Maharaja** (The Tiger King) is shown by what he does and says: a man who hunts one tiger after another, and orders ministers about, whose pride matters more than reason. The writer is gently mocking him.

A **flat** character has one main trait and stays the same. A **round** character has several traits and may surprise us. A **dynamic** character changes during the story; a **static** character does not. In Bholi, the girl who begins as timid and silenced ends as a confident young woman who refuses an unfair marriage. That change is the heart of the story.

:::qc
q: In a story, a boy gives his only umbrella to a stranger and walks home in the rain without a word. Which kind of characterisation is this?
options: ["Indirect, through action", "Direct, through the narrator", "Direct, through dialogue", "Not characterisation"]
answer: 0
right: Right. We learn his generosity from what he does. No one says "he is kind".
wrong: Notice that the narrator never calls him kind. We infer it from his action, which is indirect characterisation.
:::

## Conflict: what drives the story {lvl=1 id=s-conf label="Foundation"}

A story needs something to push against. **Conflict** is the struggle between a character and an opposing force. Without it nothing is at stake.

| Kind of conflict | The opposing force | Example in our own words |
|---|---|---|
| **Person versus nature** | storm, hail, drought, sea | Lencho's field is flattened by hail; the pilot in the black aeroplane story fights a dark storm in the sky |
| **Person versus person** | another character | In The Proposal, two neighbours quarrel over land and then over dogs, just when one has come to propose marriage |
| **Person versus society** | rules, customs, unfair systems | Mandela's long fight against apartheid; Bholi refuses a marriage forced on her by greed |
| **Person versus self** | fear, doubt, temptation | The young seagull in His First Flight fears the edge, though his own wings are ready |

Most stories mix these. The **plot** moves through five steps: the opening (we meet people and place), rising action (trouble grows), climax (the turning point), falling action (the result begins to show) and resolution (where things settle).

:::qc
q: In "His First Flight", the young seagull is afraid to leap off the ledge. What kind of conflict is this mainly?
options: ["Person versus self", "Person versus nature", "Person versus society", "Person versus person"]
answer: 0
right: Right. His real enemy is his own fear. Nothing outside prevents him from flying.
wrong: The ledge is not stopping him by force. What stops him is his own fear, so the conflict is inside him.
:::

## Figures of speech: the basic five {lvl=1 id=s-fig label="Foundation"}

A **figure of speech** is a phrase that does not mean exactly what it says, for effect. Five are met most often.

| Figure | What it does | Example (original) | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simile** | compares two unlike things using *like* or *as* | The crowd moved like a river after rain. | makes the crowd's motion flow and swell |
| **Metaphor** | says one thing *is* another, with no *like* or *as* | The classroom was a beehive. | sudden, buzzing noise and busy movement |
| **Personification** | gives a human action or feeling to a thing or idea | The old fan groaned through the afternoon. | the fan seems tired and complaining |
| **Alliteration** | repeats the same starting sound in nearby words | Slowly the soft snow settled on the silent street. | a hushed, gentle sound |
| **Hyperbole** | exaggerates on purpose | I have told you a million times. | stress, annoyance or humour |

Examples from the prose chapters, in our own words:

- In A Letter to God, the farmer sees the first big raindrops as coins. The comparison shows that, to a poor farmer, rain means money, which is why the hailstorm that follows hurts so badly.
- In Anne Frank's diary, a diary is treated as a living friend who is told secrets. This is a kind of personification, and it reveals how lonely she is.
- In The Tiger King, the court officials describe the Maharaja's feats in absurdly large terms. This hyperbole is how the writer shows flattery and makes the king look foolish.

:::note title="Simile or metaphor? Look for the signal word" tone=warn
If the comparison has **like** or **as**, it is a simile. If it simply says one thing is another, it is a metaphor. "Her voice was like honey" is a simile; "Her voice was honey" is a metaphor. A sentence such as "He runs like a deer" is a simile even though it is about speed, not looks.
:::

:::qc
q: "The sun was a golden coin in the sky." Which figure of speech is this?
options: ["Metaphor", "Simile", "Personification", "Alliteration"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The sun is said to be a coin, with no like or as.
wrong: Look for like or as. There is none, so the comparison is a metaphor, not a simile.
:::

## Irony: when the opposite happens {lvl=2 id=s-irony label="Class 10"}

**Irony** is a gap between two things that should match. There are three common kinds.

| Kind | The gap | Example (original) |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbal irony** | the speaker says the opposite of what they mean | After the bus leaves without you, a friend says, "Lovely. Right on time." |
| **Situational irony** | the outcome is the opposite of what was expected | A fire station burns down. |
| **Dramatic irony** | the reader knows something the character does not | We know the necklace is fake, but Mathilde does not (see below). |

Examples from the prose chapters, in our own words:

- **A Letter to God** (situational irony): the postmaster and his staff kindly collect money for a stranger's letter, and the farmer blames them for taking part of it. The people who helped are called thieves; the farmer trusts God, who did nothing visible.
- **The Necklace** (situational irony): Mathilde and her husband spend ten hard years repaying the money for a lost diamond necklace, and then learn that the original was cheap imitation. The sacrifice was for nothing.
- **The Tiger King** (situational irony): a king who has defeated ninety-nine tigers is finally brought down by a toy tiger made of wood. His death comes from the thing he thought harmless.
- **The Proposal** (comic irony): a man comes calmly to propose, but he and the woman fall into bitter arguments about land and dogs, and the proposal happens only after the quarrel.
- **The Midnight Visitor**: the young writer expects a famous spy to look dramatic. The ordinary, slightly comical man who does not look like one is the one who outwits a dangerous visitor.

Irony has an effect: it makes the reader laugh, wince or reflect on human weakness. The question to ask is, "What does the gap show about the character or the world?"

:::note title="How to answer an irony question" tone=tip
Say what was expected and what happened, then say what it shows. For example: "It is ironic that the farmer accuses the postmaster's men of cheating, because they gave him the money. It shows how his trust in God leaves no room for human kindness." That is a complete answer.
:::

:::qc
q: Mathilde spends ten years paying for a necklace and discovers it was worthless. This is
options: ["Situational irony", "Verbal irony", "Alliteration", "Personification"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Her huge sacrifice ends in the opposite of what she expected: it was for a worthless thing.
wrong: Nothing is said in an opposite tone, so it is not verbal. The outcome is the reverse of what a reader expects, which is situational irony.
:::

## Foreshadowing and flashback: playing with time {lvl=2 id=s-time label="Class 10"}

A story does not have to run in a straight line. Two devices bend time.

**Foreshadowing** is a hint of what will come. It builds suspense, and it makes the ending feel earned, not random. Hints can be a prophecy, a warning, an odd detail, or a mood in the weather.

- In **The Tiger King**, the opening prediction that the Maharaja will be killed by a tiger hangs over every hunt that follows. Every tiger he kills seems to push him closer to a fate he is trying to avoid.
- In **A Letter to God**, the opening shows a farmer who has staked everything on rain, and the first rain brings hope. That hope is also a hint of how far he can fall, because everything he owns rests on one crop.
- In **The Necklace**, the opening picture of a woman who is never content with her life hints that vanity will cost her.

**Flashback** jumps back from the story's present to explain the present. It usually answers "why is this character like this?"

- A narrator who stands in an old bakery and recalls childhood mornings with the village baker, as in **A Baker from Goa**, is telling the past in memory. The present moment is just the trigger for the memory. [Unverified: how much of the chapter is framed as recollection.]
- An original example: *The old teacher locked the classroom for the last time. Forty years earlier, on this same step, a nervous girl had held her first chalk.* The second sentence is the flashback; it explains why the moment weighs so much.

There is also **flash-forward**, a leap ahead, such as the passage of ten years in The Necklace, where time skips from the party night to the years of paying back. And a **time gap** (ellipsis) simply leaves out dull stretches.

:::qc
q: A detective story begins with a cat that stares at a locked drawer, which later turns out to hold the clue. This opening detail is
options: ["Foreshadowing", "Flashback", "Hyperbole", "A simile"]
answer: 0
right: Right. An odd early detail that matters later is foreshadowing.
wrong: Nothing jumps back in time, so it is not a flashback. An early hint of what comes later is foreshadowing.
:::

## Symbolism and imagery {lvl=2 id=s-symbol label="Class 10"}

A **symbol** is a concrete thing that stands for a bigger idea. A lamp may stand for hope; a locked door for a closed mind. Symbols work best when they come from the story itself.

- **The mustard seed** in The Sermon at Benares: the Buddha asks a grieving mother to bring it from a house where no one has died. She learns that no home is free from death. The seed stands for the universal fact that all suffer loss.
- **The necklace** in The Necklace: a glittering object that stands for status, vanity and the price of wanting to look richer than you are.
- **Hail and rain** in A Letter to God: the rain stands for hope and abundance; the hail, for sudden loss. The weather mirrors Lencho's fortunes.
- **The dawn** at the end of Mandela's piece: a new beginning for a nation. [Unverified: whether the dawn appears in the NCERT extract.]

**Imagery** is language that appeals to the senses: sight, sound, smell, taste and touch. It is the raw material of both symbols and figures of speech. The description of the hills, rain, mist and coffee in the Coorg piece (Glimpses of India) is imagery that lets a reader almost smell the place.

A related tool is a **motif**, a repeated element that builds a theme, such as the recurring weather in A Letter to God.

| Question | How to answer |
|---|---|
| What does X symbolise? | Say what it stands for, then link it to the story's events. |
| What is the effect of the imagery? | Name the sense it uses, then say the mood or idea it creates. |

:::qc
q: A character plants a tree at the end of a hard year in a story. The tree most likely symbolises
options: ["Hope and a fresh start", "Failure", "Anger", "Nothing in particular"]
answer: 0
right: Right. A planted tree faces the future, so it naturally stands for hope and growth. Look at the story's other clues to confirm.
wrong: A tree being planted is a forward-looking act. Symbols usually draw on what the object naturally suggests, so hope and a fresh start fit best.
:::

## More figures of speech {lvl=2 id=s-more label="Class 10"}

Beyond the basic five, board extracts and poetry questions use these.

| Figure | Meaning | Example (original) |
|---|---|---|
| **Onomatopoeia** | a word that imitates a sound | The tin roof clattered and the rain went pitter-patter. |
| **Oxymoron** | two opposite words together | a deafening silence; bitter-sweet |
| **Understatement** (litotes) | saying less than is true | After the flood, "It is a bit wet outside." |
| **Antithesis** | placing opposite ideas in a balanced way | To err is human; to forgive, divine. |
| **Paradox** | a statement that seems contradictory but holds truth | The more you know, the more you realise how little you know. |
| **Rhetorical question** | a question asked for effect, not for an answer | Who does not want to be free? |
| **Repetition** | repeating a word or phrase for emphasis | Never, never, never give up. |
| **Apostrophe** | addressing an absent person or a thing directly | O wind, why do you tease me? |
| **Euphemism** | a gentle word for something harsh | passed away for died |
| **Metonymy** | a thing named by something closely linked to it | The crown (the king) refused. |
| **Pun** | a play on a word's two meanings | I used to be a baker, but I could not make enough dough. |
| **Transferred epithet** | an adjective moved from the person to a thing | a sleepless night (the person is sleepless) |

In Anne Frank's diary, the address to "Kitty" is a form of apostrophe: she talks to the diary as to a person. Mandela's speech, in the part about the nation's future, relies on repetition and a rising tone to give a promise the force of a vow. [Unverified: exact wording and devices in the NCERT extract.]

:::qc
q: "It was a deafening silence in the hall." Which figure of speech is this?
options: ["Oxymoron", "Simile", "Hyperbole", "Pun"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Deafening (very loud) and silence (no sound) are opposites joined together.
wrong: Look at the two key words. Loud and silent contradict each other, and they are placed side by side: that is an oxymoron.
:::

## Answering device questions in the board paper {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

The CBSE English paper does not ask you to list devices from memory. It puts devices inside **extract-based questions** and short-answer questions on the prose chapters, and (in the poetry extract) it often asks, "Identify the figure of speech", sometimes as an MCQ. [Unverified: exact pattern in the current sample paper.]

A routine that works:

1. **Read the extract once** for the situation and the mood.
2. **Spot the mark.** Is there a like or as? An opposite? A hint? A sound effect? A thing acting like a person?
3. **Name** the device in the simplest accurate term.
4. **Quote** only the key words (a short phrase), not the whole line.
5. **State the effect** in one sentence: what picture, feeling or idea it gives the reader.

:::worked title="From label to full answer"
Question: *"The farmer's heart was a field waiting for rain." What figure of speech is used, and what does it show?*

Step 1. There is no like or as, and a heart is said to be a field: **metaphor**.
Step 2. A field waits for rain and depends on it. So the heart, like the field, hopes and depends on something outside itself.
Step 3. Full answer: "It is a metaphor. The farmer's heart is compared to a field that waits for rain, which shows how much his hopes depend on the weather." (An original sentence, written for this lesson.)
:::

Types of question you will see:

| Question type | Likely wording | What to include |
|---|---|---|
| MCQ | "The line 'life is a journey' is an example of" | the correct device only |
| Short answer (about 20 words) | "What is ironic about the postmaster's reaction?" | the gap, then what it shows |
| Extract-based | "Why does the author compare X to Y?" | the comparison, then the effect on the reader |
| Long answer (about 100 words) | "How does the writer build humour?" | two or three devices, each with evidence and effect |

:::qc
q: Which answer earns the most marks for "What is the effect of the line 'the wind whispered in the empty street'?"
options: ["Personification: the wind seems to speak softly, which creates a lonely, quiet mood.", "It is personification.", "The street is empty.", "The wind is a human."]
answer: 0
right: Right. It names the device, points to the words and states the effect.
wrong: Look for the answer with all three parts: the name, the evidence and the effect on mood.
:::

## Effect over label: beyond the board {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

A good reader asks "why this device here?" before "what is it called?" The label is a starting point.

### Devices work together

A single passage can use several tools at once. Take an original sentence: *The hailstones hammered the roof, and the farmer watched his year of labour flatten like grass under a stampede.* It has **personification** (the hailstones hammer, as if they were workers with tools), a **simile** (the labour flattens like grass), and **imagery** (sound and sight). The combined effect is violence and helplessness. A strong answer names the strongest device and then says how the others add to the same feeling.

### Tone and mood

**Tone** is the writer's attitude (gentle, mocking, bitter, admiring). **Mood** is the feeling the reader gets (tense, calm, sad). Devices carry both. In The Tiger King, exaggerated praise and a ridiculous cause of death give a **satirical** tone: the writer is poking fun at pride and flattery. In the Anne Frank diary, the casual first-person voice is tender and sometimes light, which makes the danger around her feel more real.

### Satire, irony and understatement

**Satire** uses humour, exaggeration or irony to criticise a person or a habit. The Tiger King is a satire on vanity and blind belief in prophecy. A good reader separates the narrator's voice from the writer's view: when a narrator seems to praise the king, the writer may intend the reverse.

### Unreliable narrators and what is left unsaid

A first-person narrator may be mistaken, biased or hiding something. A reader should ask "How would the scene look from another person's view?" When the narrator of the black aeroplane story is relieved to land and later learns that no other aircraft was seen, the story leaves open whether he was helped, dreamed, or simply misread. The ambiguity is a device too.

### Subverting the device

Writers sometimes use a familiar tool and then twist it. A story may foreshadow disaster and end in a gentle surprise, or use a bright simile to describe something grim. Notice when the device goes against the mood; it is almost always on purpose.

### Writing with devices

In your own writing, use devices sparingly and freshly. One striking comparison does more than five stale ones. Avoid clichés such as "as busy as a bee" in marked work; a precise, new image shows more skill.

:::qc
q: A narrator describes a greedy ruler in extravagant praise, but every example shows him as foolish. The writer's attitude is best called
options: ["Satirical", "Admiring", "Neutral", "Sorrowful"]
answer: 0
right: Right. The praise is used to mock the ruler. The gap between the words and the evidence is the clue.
wrong: Compare the narrator's words (praise) with the evidence (foolish acts). When praise is used to criticise, the tone is satirical.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A literary device is a deliberate technique that adds meaning, feeling or effect; narrative devices shape the story, figures of speech shape the phrase. {lvl=1}
2. Point of view is who tells the story: first person (I), third limited (one mind), third omniscient (many minds). {lvl=1}
3. Characterisation is direct (the narrator tells) or indirect (speech, actions, looks, thoughts, reactions of others). {lvl=1}
4. Conflict is struggle against nature, another person, society or oneself, and it drives the plot. {lvl=1}
5. Simile uses like or as; metaphor says one thing is another; personification gives human traits to things; alliteration repeats starting sounds; hyperbole exaggerates. {lvl=1}
6. Irony is a gap: verbal (opposite meaning), situational (opposite outcome), dramatic (reader knows more than the character). {lvl=2}
7. Foreshadowing hints at the future; flashback goes back in time to explain the present. {lvl=2}
8. A symbol stands for a larger idea (the mustard seed for universal loss); imagery appeals to the senses. {lvl=2}
9. Every device answer has three parts: the name, the evidence from the text, and the effect on the reader. {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: devices work together to build tone and mood; satire uses praise or exaggeration to criticise. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Calling a "like" or "as" comparison a metaphor, or a no-signal comparison a simile: check for the signal word. {lvl=1}
- Confusing the author with the narrator: the narrator I in a story is not always the writer. {lvl=1}
- Naming a device and stopping: always add what it shows or does. {lvl=1}
- Saying a character "is a symbol" with no link: a symbol needs both the thing and the idea it stands for. {lvl=2}
- Mixing up irony with coincidence or bad luck: irony needs a gap between expectation and result. {lvl=2}
- Calling every flashback foreshadowing, or the reverse: flashback looks back, foreshadowing hints ahead. {lvl=2}
- Quoting a whole paragraph as evidence: use the shortest phrase that proves the point. {lvl=2}
- Piling up device names in a long answer without explaining any: pick the two or three that matter. {lvl=3}
- Reading a satirical or ironic passage literally: ask whether the praise is meant. {lvl=3}
:::
