## Why every sentence needs a verb {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Take any complete sentence and remove the verb: *Asha the school.* Nothing is said. Put a verb back and the words come alive: *Asha reached the school.*

A sentence exists to say something about someone or something. The part that says it is the **predicate**, and its heart is the **verb**. This is why the verb is the one word no full sentence can do without, and why a mistake in it is so easy for the reader to notice.

A verb does one of three jobs.

| Job | What it tells you | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Action** | what someone or something does | *The goalkeeper dived.* |
| **State or link** | what someone or something is, or seems | *The soup smells delicious.* |
| **Help** | the time, mood or shape of another verb | *She has finished. He can swim.* |

The rest of this lesson follows these jobs. First the verbs that do and are, then the small helpers that give English its questions, negatives and tenses, and then the rules that keep the verb in step with its subject.

## Kinds of verb: action, linking and auxiliary {lvl=1 id=s-kinds label="Foundation"}

### Action verbs

An action verb names something you can do: run, write, think, grow. Some actions are visible (*kick*) and some are mental (*remember, decide*).

### Linking verbs

A linking verb does not show action. It joins the subject to a word that describes it or renames it. The commonest is **be** (am, is, are, was, were). Others: seem, become, remain, look, feel, taste, smell, sound, turn.

- *Mira is a doctor.* (renames the subject)
- *The milk turned sour.* (describes the subject)

After a linking verb, use an **adjective**, not an adverb, because the word describes the subject and not an action.

- Right: *The cake tastes good.* *She looks tired.* *I feel bad about it.*
- Wrong: *The cake tastes well.* *I feel badly about it* (in standard school grammar).

The same word can be an action verb or a linking verb. *She tasted the soup* (action: she did something to the soup) but *The soup tasted salty* (link).

### Auxiliary verbs

An **auxiliary** (helping) verb has no full meaning of its own in the sentence. It sits in front of a main verb and adds time, mood or shape. Together they form a **verb phrase**: *has been waiting, can swim, did not go*.

There are two groups.

1. **Primary auxiliaries: be, have, do.** They also work as main verbs on their own (*She has a bike. They do their homework. He is tall.*) but as helpers they build forms.
2. **Modal auxiliaries: can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must**, plus the semi-modals **ought to, need, dare** and **used to**.

:::qc
q: In "The children were playing in the park", which word is the auxiliary verb?
options: ["children", "were", "playing", "park"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Were helps the main verb playing to make the past continuous.
wrong: The main verb is playing. The word that helps it (be + -ing) is were.
:::

## The three forms of a verb {lvl=1 id=s-forms label="Foundation"}

English builds nearly everything from three forms of a verb, plus -ing and -s.

| Name | What it is | go | walk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base (V1)** | the dictionary form | go | walk |
| **Past (V2)** | the simple past | went | walked |
| **Past participle (V3)** | used after have, has, had and in the passive | gone | walked |
| **-ing form** | used after be, and as a noun or adjective | going | walking |
| **-s form** | present tense with he, she, it | goes | walks |

**Regular verbs** make V2 and V3 by adding -ed (*walk, walked, walked*). They are the majority of verbs, and every new verb that enters the language is regular. **Irregular verbs** keep old forms from earlier English, which is why they are the common, everyday verbs (be, go, have, see) that were used so often that people never gave up their older shapes.

| V1 | V2 | V3 | | V1 | V2 | V3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| begin | began | begun | | fly | flew | flown |
| break | broke | broken | | go | went | gone |
| bring | brought | brought | | put | put | put |
| buy | bought | bought | | see | saw | seen |
| cut | cut | cut | | swim | swam | swum |
| drink | drank | drunk | | take | took | taken |
| eat | ate | eaten | | teach | taught | taught |
| write | wrote | written | | speak | spoke | spoken |

Two patterns help you learn them: V1-V2-V3 all alike (*put, cut, hit*), and V2 and V3 alike (*bring, buy, teach*). The pattern that causes most mistakes is the one where V2 and V3 differ (*saw/seen, went/gone, wrote/written*). Use V2 alone, and V3 only after a helper.

- Wrong: *I have saw the film. He done his work.*
- Right: *I have seen the film. He did his work.* or *He has done his work.*

Spelling notes: drop the silent e before -ing (*make, making*); double the final consonant of a short stressed syllable (*stop, stopped, stopping*); change y to i after a consonant (*study, studies, studied*), but keep y after a vowel (*play, played*).

## Do-support: questions and negatives {lvl=1 id=s-do label="Foundation"}

To make a question or a negative with **be, have (as an auxiliary) or a modal**, you can use the verb itself.

- *She is ready. Is she ready? She is not ready.*
- *They can swim. Can they swim? They cannot swim.*
- *He has finished. Has he finished? He has not finished.*

An ordinary action verb (play, read, go) cannot move in front of the subject, so English brings in **do, does or did** as a stand-in helper. That helper takes the tense, and the main verb returns to its base form.

| Statement | Question | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| She **plays** chess. | **Does** she **play** chess? | She **does not play** chess. |
| They **went** home. | **Did** they **go** home? | They **did not go** home. |
| I **like** it. | **Do** you **like** it? | I **do not like** it. |

Why the main verb goes back to V1: the helper already shows the tense and person. Showing it again on the main verb would count it twice. That is the reason behind the classic mistakes *Did she went?* and *Does he knows?*

Do-support is also used for emphasis (*I do like it!*) and to avoid repeating a verb (*She runs faster than I do*).

:::qc
q: Choose the correct negative of "Ravi writes poems."
options: ["Ravi does not writes poems.", "Ravi do not write poems.", "Ravi does not write poems.", "Ravi not writes poems."]
answer: 2
right: Right. Does carries the -s, and the main verb is the base form write.
wrong: The helper does shows he, she or it in the present. After it, use the base form write, with no -s.
:::

## Modal auxiliaries: what they do {lvl=2 id=s-modals label="Class 10"}

A modal does not tell you when something happens. It tells you how the speaker sees it: possible, certain, allowed, required, advisable. That is why English has several and why they cannot be swapped carelessly.

### Four rules shared by every modal

1. A modal is followed by the **base form** with no to: *She can swim. They should wait.* (Exception: *ought to*.)
2. A modal has **no -s** for he, she, it: *He must go*, not *He musts go*.
3. A modal makes its own questions and negatives, with no do: *Can you help? You may not enter.*
4. A modal has no -ing or V3 form. When the modal itself needs a past or future, borrow another expression (see *must* below).

### What each modal means

| Modal | Main meanings | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **can** | ability; permission (informal); possibility | *She can solve it. Can I go out?* |
| **could** | past ability; polite request; weaker possibility | *He could read at four. Could you pass the salt?* |
| **may** | permission (formal); possibility | *May I come in? It may rain.* |
| **might** | weaker possibility than may; politeness in the past | *She might be late.* |
| **shall** | offer or suggestion with I and we; a firm command or promise | *Shall I open the window? You shall have your prize.* |
| **will** | future; decision; request; habit | *I will help you. Will you wait?* |
| **would** | future in the past; polite request; past habit | *He said he would come. Would you mind?* |
| **should** | advice; duty; what is expected | *You should rest. The bus should be here.* |
| **must** | strong obligation; logical certainty | *You must wear a seat belt. She must be at home.* |
| **ought to** | moral duty or advice (like should) | *We ought to help the old.* |

### Obligation and its relatives

- **Must** is a duty felt by the speaker. **Have to** is an outside rule. They overlap in the present, but only *have to* has a past and a future: *I had to leave early. You will have to pay.* (Must has no past form of its own.)
- **Mustn't** means *forbidden*. **Needn't** or **don't have to** means *not necessary*. These are not opposites of must in the same way:
  - *You mustn't touch the wire.* (Do not.)
  - *You needn't come with me.* (It is optional.)
- **Should** is softer than must: advice, not an order.

### Used to

*Used to + base verb* describes a past habit or state that no longer exists: *We used to play on this road* (we do not now). Do not confuse it with **be used to + -ing**, meaning *be accustomed to*: *I am used to waking early.* The first is a verb pattern, the second is an adjective phrase with *to* as a preposition.

### Need and dare

As a modal (mostly in questions and negatives) **need** and **dare** take no -s and are followed by the base form: *He needn't worry. Dare she speak?* As ordinary verbs they take to and do-support: *She needs to rest. He doesn't dare to ask.* In exam English you will meet both forms; the safe choice is to keep each form complete, not half of each. *He needn't to go* mixes the two and is wrong.

:::note title="One sentence, two modals" tone=warn
You cannot stack modals in standard English: *She will can come* and *You must should go* are wrong. Replace one with an equivalent: *She will be able to come. You must go.* The same goes for the pair *should* and *ought*: *you should ought to* is not acceptable.
:::

:::qc
q: "The lights are on, so she ___ be at home."
options: ["may not", "must", "used to", "needn't"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Must can express a logical conclusion from evidence, here the lights.
wrong: The speaker is drawing a conclusion from the lights, which is a strong certainty. That is the logical use of must.
:::

## Agreement of subject and verb {lvl=2 id=s-agree label="Class 10"}

A present-tense verb has to agree with its subject in **number**. A singular subject takes the -s form (*The bird sings*) and a plural subject takes the base form (*The birds sing*). In the past tense, only *be* still shows the difference (*was/were*) and *have* in the present (*has/have*).

The reason this goes wrong is that the real subject is not always the word next to the verb.

### Rules that earn marks

1. **Ignore the words in between.** The verb agrees with the head noun: *The box of pencils is on the table.* *The quality of the mangoes was poor.*
2. **Each, every, either, neither, one, everyone, nobody and somebody take a singular verb.** *Each of the students has a ticket.* *Neither of the answers is correct.* *Everybody was present.*
3. **Phrases like as well as, along with, together with and in addition to** do not make a plural. The verb agrees with the first subject: *The captain, along with his team, is waiting.*
4. **Two subjects joined by and** take a plural verb: *Rahul and Sana are friends.* (Exception: one person or thing: *Bread and butter is my breakfast.*)
5. **Or, nor, either...or and neither...nor:** the verb agrees with the subject **nearer** to it: *Neither the teacher nor the students were late.* *Either the students or the teacher was late.*
6. **A number of** takes a plural verb (*A number of people were waiting*); **the number of** takes a singular verb (*The number of students is rising*).
7. **Collective nouns** (class, team, committee, family) take a singular verb when seen as one unit (*The team is ready*) and a plural when seen as separate people (*The team are arguing about the plan*). The board key will usually expect the singular.
8. **Uncountable nouns and nouns that look plural:** *The news is good. Mathematics is my favourite subject. The scissors are on the desk.* (Scissors, trousers and spectacles are always plural. News, mathematics, physics, economics and politics take a singular verb.) *The police are here* is plural.
9. **Distances, amounts and times** treated as one total are singular: *Five kilometres is a long way. Ten rupees is enough.*
10. **There is and there are:** the verb agrees with the noun that follows. *There is a pen on the desk. There are two pens on the desk.*

:::worked title="Choosing the verb in one pass"
Fill the gap: *The number of visitors to the fair (is/are) rising, but a number of stalls (has/have) closed.*

Step 1: "the number of visitors" is about the number, which is one thing: *is*. Step 2: "a number of stalls" means several stalls, so a plural verb: *have*. Answer: *The number of visitors to the fair is rising, but a number of stalls have closed.*
:::

:::qc
q: "Neither of the boys ___ present."
options: ["are", "were", "is", "have been"]
answer: 2
right: Right. Neither takes a singular verb, so is.
wrong: Neither refers to each of the two boys singly, so the verb is singular. Of the options, only is is singular and present.
:::

## Transitive and intransitive verbs {lvl=2 id=s-trans label="Class 10"}

A **transitive** verb needs an **object** to complete its meaning: *She opened the door.* (opened what?) An **intransitive** verb does not: *The door opened.* The same verb can often be either; the question is whether something receives the action.

Only transitive verbs can form a passive: *The door was opened by her.* This is why *The accident was happened* is wrong: happen is intransitive.

A few pairs are confused so often that examiners keep returning to them.

| Intransitive (no object) | Transitive (needs object) |
|---|---|
| **rise, rose, risen**: *The sun rises.* | **raise, raised, raised**: *They raise the flag.* |
| **lie, lay, lain**: *She lay down.* | **lay, laid, laid**: *He laid the book on the desk.* |
| **fall, fell, fallen**: *Leaves fall.* | **fell, felled, felled**: *They felled the tree.* |
| **sit, sat, sat**: *Sit here.* | **seat, seated**: *She seated the guests.* |

Note the trap in the second row: the past of *lie* (recline) is *lay*, which looks like the present of the transitive verb *lay*. *Lie* in the sense of "tell an untruth" is regular: *lie, lied, lied*.

## Finite and non-finite forms {lvl=2 id=s-finite label="Class 10"}

A **finite** verb has a subject, shows tense and agrees with the subject (*she plays, they played, he has gone*). It is what makes a sentence a sentence. A **non-finite** verb does not show tense or agree with a subject. There are three kinds.

| Form | What it looks like | Used as | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Infinitive** | to + V1 | noun, or tells purpose | *To win was her dream. She came to help.* |
| **Gerund** | V-ing | noun | *Swimming is good exercise.* |
| **Participle** | V-ing (present) or V3 (past) | adjective, or part of a clause | *a running tap, a broken chair, Hearing the bell, he rose.* |

A sentence can have many non-finite verbs but needs at least one finite one. *Wanting to win, Arun practised every day.* Here **practised** is finite; *wanting* and *to win* are non-finite.

### Which verbs take which form

Some verbs are followed by an infinitive, others by a gerund, a few by either. There is no law behind it; it is idiom, and it is learned in groups.

- Verb + **to-infinitive**: want, decide, hope, plan, promise, refuse, learn, manage. *She decided to leave.*
- Verb + **gerund**: enjoy, finish, avoid, mind, suggest, practise, keep. *He enjoys reading. I suggest waiting.*
- Either, with the same meaning: begin, start, like, love, hate. *It began to rain / raining.*
- Either, with a change in meaning: **remember, forget, stop.** *I stopped to ask* (I paused in order to ask) but *I stopped asking* (I gave it up). *Remember to lock the door* (do not forget a future task) but *I remember locking it* (I recall the past act).

After a preposition, always use the gerund: *She is good at singing. He left without saying goodbye. I look forward to meeting you.* (Here *to* is a preposition, so the gerund follows.)

## Verbs in the board's questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

Verb knowledge is tested in four ways.

1. **Gap filling with helpers.** Choose be, have, do or the right modal. First decide the meaning the sentence needs (ability, duty, advice, certainty), then fit the form.
2. **Editing and error correction.** Typical planted errors: a wrong auxiliary (*has/have*, *was/were*), a missing helper, a verb form after did or a modal, and subject-verb agreement.
3. **Sentence reordering and transformation.** For example, changing a statement to a question or a negative using do-support, or rewriting "It is not necessary to wait" as "You needn't wait".
4. **Question tags.** A tag mirrors the helper of the main clause and reverses its polarity.

### Question tags in one table

| Main clause | Tag | Example |
|---|---|---|
| positive | negative | *She plays the violin, doesn't she?* |
| negative | positive | *They haven't left, have they?* |
| contains be, have or a modal | repeat that helper | *You can swim, can't you? He is late, isn't he?* |
| ordinary verb with no helper | use do, does, did | *You like tea, don't you?* |
| I am | aren't I | *I am late, aren't I?* |
| Let's | shall we | *Let's go, shall we?* |
| imperative | will you | *Close the door, will you?* |
| nobody, nothing, never and other negative words | positive tag | *Nobody called, did they?* |

Note that the tag uses a pronoun for the subject (*they*, not *nobody*), and for words like *nobody* and *everybody* the pronoun is **they**.

:::worked title="An editing line in three checks"
Find the error: *The news about the results are good.*

Check 1: what is the real subject? *The news*, not *results*. Check 2: is *news* singular or plural? Singular, though it looks plural. Check 3: the verb must match. Correction: *The news about the results is good.*
:::

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["He can sings very well.", "He can sing very well.", "He cans sing very well.", "He can to sing very well."]
answer: 1
right: Right. A modal is followed by the base form, with no -s and no to.
wrong: The modal can is followed directly by the base form of the verb. No -s, no to.
:::

## Beyond the board: finer shades of the verb {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### Modal perfects: speaking about the past

A modal plus **have + V3** lets you judge a past event.

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| must have + V3 | strong conclusion about the past | *He must have missed the bus.* |
| can't / couldn't have + V3 | strong belief it did not happen | *She can't have seen us.* |
| may / might have + V3 | possibility about the past | *They might have lost their way.* |
| should have + V3 | what was right but did not happen | *You should have called.* |
| needn't have + V3 | it was not necessary, but it was done | *We needn't have hurried.* |
| would have + V3 | what did not happen, because a condition was not met | *I would have helped if you had asked.* |

### Could for ability: general or specific?

*Could* works for a general past ability: *She could swim at five.* For one particular success on one occasion, English prefers **was/were able to** or **managed to**: *The firefighter was able to reach the child* (not *could reach* in a positive statement). In a negative, either works: *He couldn't open the box.*

### Have as main verb, have as helper

*Have* (possess) in British exam English may form questions without do (*Have you a pen?*), but most modern writing uses do-support: *Do you have a pen?* When *have* means eat, take or experience, do-support is required: *Did you have lunch? She doesn't have breakfast.* The board is unlikely to pick the older pattern as the only correct answer, so use do-support when in doubt.

### Phrasal verbs

A **phrasal verb** is a verb plus a small word (an adverb or preposition) that together make a new meaning: *give up* (stop), *look after* (care for), *put off* (postpone), *run into* (meet by chance). The meaning of the whole cannot be guessed from the parts, which is why they are learned as units.

Some are **separable**: *Turn the light off / Turn off the light.* With a pronoun object the pronoun must go in the middle: *Turn it off*, not *Turn off it*. Others are **inseparable**: *Look after the baby; look after her.*

### Shall and will

Modern usage has largely dropped the old rule that *shall* goes with I and we for the plain future and *will* with the others. Today *will* serves for all persons in the plain future, while *shall* is kept for offers and suggestions with I/we (*Shall we begin?*) and formal commitments (*The tenant shall pay the rent monthly*). [Unverified] some board keys still follow the old rule; follow the instruction in your paper.

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A verb is the heart of the predicate; every sentence needs one. Verbs act, link or help. {lvl=1}
2. Linking verbs (be, seem, become, look, taste, feel) are followed by adjectives, not adverbs: The soup tastes good. {lvl=1}
3. Auxiliaries are be, have and do (primary) and can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must (modal). They help a main verb. {lvl=1}
4. The forms are V1, V2, V3, -ing and -s. Use V2 alone and V3 only after have, has, had or be (passive). {lvl=1}
5. Ordinary verbs make questions and negatives with do, does or did, and the main verb returns to the base form. {lvl=1}
6. A modal takes the base form directly: no -s, no to, no do, and no second modal. {lvl=2}
7. Must is duty or certainty; mustn't is forbidden; needn't or don't have to is not necessary. Should is advice. {lvl=2}
8. The verb agrees with its real subject, not the nearest noun. Each, every, either, neither, one and everyone are singular. {lvl=2}
9. Transitive verbs take an object and can form a passive; intransitive ones cannot. Know rise/raise, lie/lay, fall/fell. {lvl=2}
10. Finite verbs carry tense and agree with the subject; non-finite forms (infinitive, gerund, participle) do not. After a preposition, use the gerund. {lvl=2}
11. Modal plus have + V3 judges the past: must have, can't have, should have, needn't have. {lvl=3}
12. Phrasal verbs are learned as units; a pronoun object goes inside a separable one (turn it off). {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "Does he knows?" or "She didn't went": after do, does and did, the main verb is the base form. {lvl=1}
- "I have saw", "he has went", "they have wrote": after have, has or had, use V3 (seen, gone, written). {lvl=1}
- "The soup tastes well", "she looks beautifully": after a linking verb use the adjective. {lvl=1}
- "He can sings", "she must to go", "I musts": no -s and no to after a modal. {lvl=2}
- "She will can come", "you must should go": two modals cannot stand together; use be able to or have to for the second. {lvl=2}
- "You mustn't come" when you mean it is not necessary: use needn't or don't have to. {lvl=2}
- "Each of the girls have a prize", "Neither of them are ready": each and neither are singular. {lvl=2}
- "The news are good", "Mathematics are difficult": these nouns look plural but take a singular verb. {lvl=2}
- Using the nearest noun as the subject: "The cost of the books are high" should be "The cost of the books is high". {lvl=2}
- "The sun is raising", "I laid down": confusing intransitive rise or lie with transitive raise or lay. {lvl=2}
- "I look forward to meet you": to is a preposition here, so use the gerund: to meeting. {lvl=2}
- "He could save the child" for one specific success: prefer was able to or managed to. {lvl=3}
