## Why English has so many look-alikes {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

You might expect every idea to have its own sound and its own spelling. English does not work like that, and there are three reasons.

1. **There are only so many short sounds.** English words are often short (*bat*, *bark*, *fair*), and a language can make only so many short sounds. With thousands of meanings and few short sounds, different words end up sharing one.
2. **English borrows from many languages.** The river *bank* and the money *bank* came into English by different routes, from different languages, and happened to land on the same spelling. [Unverified: usually traced to a Scandinavian word for the river bank and an Italian word for a moneychanger's bench.]
3. **Speech changed but spelling stayed.** Printing fixed the spellings while pronunciation kept moving. Words that once sounded different came to sound the same, but each kept its own spelling. That is why *night* and *knight* sound alike and look different.

This matters to you because the spelling is a clue to the meaning. For homophones, the spelling is the only thing that separates them, so you cannot pick the right one by ear.

## Four kinds of twin {lvl=1 id=s-kinds label="Foundation"}

The names sound alike, which is a joke in itself. Learn them by what each pair shares.

| Name | Same sound? | Same spelling? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Homophone** | yes | no | *pair / pear*, *sea / see*, *knight / night* |
| **Homonym** | yes | yes | *bat* (animal) / *bat* (cricket), *bank*, *bark*, *fair* |
| **Heteronym** (a kind of homograph) | no | yes | *lead* (to guide) / *lead* (the metal), *tear* (rip) / *tear* (a drop) |
| **Paronym** (confusable) | nearly | nearly | *affect / effect*, *accept / except*, *principal / principle* |

(**Homograph** is the wide word: any words spelt alike. Homonyms and heteronyms are both homographs.)

Textbooks do not always agree on these names. Some use *homonym* for homophones and homographs together, so do not be alarmed if another book defines them differently. In this lesson a homonym shares **both** sound and spelling, and an exam will usually accept your definition if you give an example with it.

:::note title="A quick way to remember" tone=tip
*Homo* means "same", *phone* means "sound", *graph* means "writing" and *nym* means "name" (the Greek roots of these words). So a homophone has the same sound, a homograph the same writing, and a homonym the same name (the same spelling and sound). [Unverified: etymology from memory.]
:::

:::qc
q: Which pair are homophones?
options: ["sea / see", "lead (metal) / lead (guide)", "bat (animal) / bat (cricket)", "affect / effect"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Sea and see sound the same but are spelt differently and mean different things.
wrong: Homophones sound alike but are spelt differently. Only sea and see do that. The lead and bat pairs share their spelling, and affect and effect only come close.
:::

## Homophones: spell by meaning {lvl=1 id=s-homophones label="Foundation"}

Because homophones sound the same, your ear cannot help. The only tool is the meaning of the sentence. Start with the pairs you meet every day.

| Homophones | Meanings | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **to / too / two** | towards; also or excessively; the number 2 | *I went to the shop, too, and bought two pens. It was too late.* |
| **there / their / they're** | in that place; belonging to them; they are | *They're leaving their bags over there.* |
| **your / you're** | belonging to you; you are | *You're sure this is your seat?* |
| **its / it's** | belonging to it; it is or it has | *It's raining, and the dog is shaking its coat.* |
| **hear / here** | listen with the ear; in this place | *Come here so you can hear me.* |
| **whole / hole** | complete; an opening | *The whole village fell into the hole.* |
| **peace / piece** | calm; a part | *A piece of cake brought peace to the room.* |
| **plain / plane** | simple, or a flat area; an aeroplane | *The plane landed on the plain.* |
| **write / right / rite** | put words down; correct, or a direction; a ceremony | *Write the right answer.* |
| **weight / wait** | how heavy; to stay till something happens | *The weight made us wait.* |
| **meet / meat** | come together; flesh used as food | *Meet me at the sea.* |
| **sun / son** | the star; a male child | *The son sat in the sun.* |

Three triples to learn carefully, because the board loves them:

- **there / their / they're**: *there* is a place (it contains *here*), *their* shows ownership, *they're* is a squeezed *they are*.
- **to / too / two**: *two* is the number (with a *w*, like *twin* and *twice*); *too* means "also" or "more than enough" (the extra *o* is the extra amount); *to* is everything else.
- **its / it's**: the apostrophe in *it's* stands for a missing letter (*it is*). If you can say "it is" there, use *it's*; if not, use *its*.

:::note title="The substitution test" tone=tip
When unsure, read the sentence with the long form. *They're* becomes *they are*, *you're* becomes *you are*, *it's* becomes *it is*. If the sentence still makes sense, use the short form with the apostrophe. If not, use the other word.
:::

:::qc
q: "The team lost ___ match, but ___ proud of ___ effort."
options: ["their, they're, their", "there, their, they're", "they're, there, their", "their, their, they're"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Their match (ownership), they're (they are) proud, and their effort (ownership).
wrong: Test each gap with the long form. "They are proud" fits the second gap, and the first and third gaps show ownership, so they need their.
:::

## Homonyms: one spelling, many meanings {lvl=1 id=s-homonyms label="Foundation"}

A homonym looks and sounds the same every time, so the sentence is your only guide. Study how the neighbouring words decide.

| Word | Meaning one | Meaning two |
|---|---|---|
| **bat** | *The bat hung upside down in the cave.* (animal) | *He swung the bat and hit a six.* (cricket) |
| **bark** | *The dog's bark scared the thief.* (sound) | *The bark of the neem tree is bitter.* (outer layer) |
| **fair** | *It was a fair decision.* (just) | *We went to the village fair.* (an event) |
| **bear** | *A bear came out of the forest.* (animal) | *I cannot bear this noise.* (tolerate) |
| **spring** | *Flowers bloom in spring.* (season) | *The spring in the pen broke.* (coil of metal) |
| **match** | *We watched the match.* (a game) | *Strike a match.* (a stick that lights) |
| **light** | *Switch on the light.* (brightness) | *The box is light.* (not heavy) |

### Is it two words or one word with two senses?

Dictionaries separate two cases.

- **Homonyms** are really two different words that share a spelling. The two *banks* have different origins and nothing in common. A dictionary gives each its own headword, often with a small raised number.
- **Polysemy** is one word whose meaning has stretched over time. The *foot* of a person and the *foot* of a hill are one word with related senses (the lowest part). A dictionary lists these under one headword.

A useful test: if you can explain how one meaning grew out of the other, it is polysemy. If you cannot, they are homonyms.

:::qc
q: In which sentence does "bear" mean "tolerate"?
options: ["I cannot bear the heat any longer.", "The bear climbed the tree.", "The bear cubs played by the stream.", "A bear is a heavy animal."]
answer: 0
right: Right. "Cannot bear the heat" means cannot put up with it.
wrong: Three sentences talk about the animal. Only "cannot bear the heat" uses the word as a verb meaning to put up with.
:::

## Confusables: words that come close {lvl=2 id=s-paronyms label="Class 10"}

Homophones sound identical. **Confusables** only come close, in sound or spelling, which is the very reason for the mix-up. They matter for the board because a wrong word in a sentence is a real word and so looks fine at a glance.

For each pair below, learn the meaning, the part of speech and the hook.

| Pair | Meanings | Example | Memory hook |
|---|---|---|---|
| **affect / effect** | affect (verb): to influence; effect (noun): a result | *The rain affected the match; its effect was a draw.* | A for action, E for end result |
| **accept / except** | accept: to receive or agree; except: apart from | *Everyone, except Rahul, accepted the gift.* | except = ex-clude |
| **principal / principle** | principal: head, or main; principle: a rule or belief | *Our principal praised her principles.* | The principal is your pal |
| **stationary / stationery** | stationary: not moving; stationery: paper, pens, envelopes | *The bus was stationary outside the stationery shop.* | stationery has e, as in envelope |
| **complement / compliment** | complement: something that completes; compliment: praise | *The scarf complements her dress; she got a compliment.* | compliment has an i, as in "I like it" |
| **council / counsel** | council: a group that governs; counsel: advice (or to advise) | *The council sought counsel from the experts.* | counsel has an s, like "say" (advice); the council is the group |
| **lose / loose** | lose: to fail to keep or win; loose: not tight | *Do not lose the loose button.* | loose has the extra o, loose as a goose |
| **weather / whether** | weather: the state of the sky; whether: if | *I wonder whether the weather will hold.* | whether = if |
| **desert / dessert** | desert: dry land (or to abandon); dessert: the sweet course | *The camel crossed the desert; we ate the dessert.* | dessert has two s: you want seconds |
| **later / latter** | later: afterwards; latter: the second of two | *Of tea and coffee I chose the latter; we will talk later.* | latter = last of the two |
| **elicit / illicit** | elicit: to draw out; illicit: illegal | *The question elicited an honest reply. Illicit trade is a crime.* | illicit = illegal |

:::note title="Spot the exceptions" tone=warn
The common use is the one to learn first, but a few of these have a second job. *Effect* can be a verb meaning "to bring about": *The new principal effected many changes.* *Affect* can mean "to pretend": *He affected a calm he did not feel.* The board almost always tests the common use.
:::

:::qc
q: "The new rule will have a good ___ on the students."
options: ["effect", "affect", "affected", "affects"]
answer: 0
right: Right. After "a good" you need a noun, and the noun meaning a result is effect.
wrong: The words "a good" show that a noun is needed. The noun meaning "result" is effect; affect is normally a verb.
:::

:::qc
q: Choose the correct sentence.
options: ["Everyone accept Meera has arrived.", "Everyone except Meera has arrived.", "Everyone expect Meera has arrived.", "Everyone excepted Meera has arrived."]
answer: 1
right: Right. Except means "apart from", and leaves Meera out. Accept means "receive".
wrong: You need a word meaning "apart from". That is except. Accept means to receive, and expect means to look forward to something.
:::

## Same word, two spellings: noun and verb {lvl=2 id=s-pos label="Class 10"}

Some pairs are the same word spelt differently depending on its job. In British spelling, which CBSE material generally follows, the **noun** takes **c** and the **verb** takes **s**. There is a trick: *noun* comes before *verb* in the alphabet, and *c* comes before *s*.

| Noun (c) | Verb (s) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **advice** (rhymes with *ice*) | **advise** (rhymes with *wise*) | *I advise you to take her advice.* |
| **practice** | **practise** | *Daily practice helps. Practise the song daily.* |
| **licence** | **license** | *He has a driving licence. The shop is licensed to sell medicine.* |
| **device** | **devise** | *The device was made to a plan that he devised.* |
| **prophecy** | **prophesy** | *The prophecy came true. He prophesied rain.* |

Other pairs use a different ending to mark noun and verb, and the sound changes too.

| Noun | Verb |
|---|---|
| **breath** (short vowel) | **breathe** (long vowel) |
| **cloth** | **clothe** |
| **belief** | **believe** |
| **proof** | **prove** |
| **loss** | **lose** |

In American spelling *practice* and *license* serve as both noun and verb. If you write for an Indian board paper, use the British pattern and stay consistent. [Unverified: check your own syllabus guidance if unsure.]

:::qc
q: "The doctor gave me good ___ about my diet."
options: ["advice", "advise", "advices", "advised"]
answer: 0
right: Right. "Good" shows a noun is needed, and the noun is advice with a c. Advice is also an uncountable noun, so it never takes an s.
wrong: After "good" you need a noun. The noun is advice (c). Advise (s) is the verb, and "advices" does not exist as a normal English word.
:::

## When the meanings, not the spellings, cross {lvl=2 id=s-meaning label="Class 10"}

Some look-alikes are different not in spelling but in use. Mix-ups here show up in editing tasks.

| Pair | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **fewer / less** | fewer with things you can count; less with quantity you cannot count | *Fewer cars, less traffic.* |
| **between / among** | between for two (or clear pairs); among for a group | *Share the sweets among the four of us. Sit between Ria and me.* |
| **farther / further** | farther for physical distance; further for more or additional | *Delhi is farther than Agra. We need further details.* |
| **beside / besides** | beside = next to; besides = in addition to | *Sit beside me. Besides Hindi, she speaks Tamil.* |
| **rise / raise** | rise: goes up by itself, no object; raise: lifts something, has an object | *The sun rises. They raised the flag.* |
| **lie / lay** | lie: rest flat, no object; lay: put something down, has an object | *I lie down. I lay the book on the table.* |
| **ensure / insure / assure** | ensure: make certain; insure: cover with insurance; assure: promise someone | *Ensure the door is locked. I assure you it is.* |
| **sensible / sensitive** | sensible: practical, wise; sensitive: easily affected or hurt | *A sensible plan. A sensitive child.* |
| **historic / historical** | historic: important in history; historical: belonging to the past | *A historic victory. A historical novel.* |

Lie and lay are a trap because their past forms collide: the past of *lie* is *lay*, while the past of *lay* is *laid*. For rise and raise, check for an object: if something is being lifted, use *raise*.

:::qc
q: "There are ___ students in class today, so there is ___ noise."
options: ["fewer, less", "less, fewer", "fewer, fewer", "less, less"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Students can be counted, so fewer; noise cannot, so less.
wrong: Count what you can count. Students can be counted (fewer), but noise is a quantity (less).
:::

## How the board tests look-alikes {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

CBSE has no separate "homophone" question. The skill is tested inside other tasks. [Unverified: the exact format and marks change with the yearly sample paper, so check the latest one.]

1. **Reading comprehension: words in context.** You may be asked what a word means as used in the passage, or to find a word that means the same as, or the opposite of, a given one. Many words have several meanings, so read the whole sentence. For instance *bore* in "The villagers bore the hardship" means *endured*.
2. **Gap filling and multiple choice.** Options often include a confusable set (*affect, effect, affected, effected*). Decide the part of speech of the gap first; that usually leaves one option.
3. **Editing.** A line holds one error. The wrong word is often a look-alike: *site* for *sight*, *principle* for *principal*. Because it is a real word, you must read for meaning.
4. **Writing tasks.** Examiners mark spelling and word choice in letters, articles and speeches, so a stray *their* or *advise* costs marks.

A routine that works:

- Read the line for its meaning, not for its shape.
- Decide the part of speech the gap needs (noun, verb, adjective).
- Choose the word that fits that job and that meaning.
- Say the sentence again with your choice and with the long form (*they are*, *it is*).

:::worked title="Editing a look-alike in one pass"
Find and correct the error in each line.

1. *Our principle announced a holiday.*
2. *The cloth was too lose for the child.*
3. *Please give me some advise on my project.*
4. *He did not know weather the bus had left.*

Line 1: *principle* names a rule, but here a person is meant: *principal*. Line 2: *lose* is a verb; the gap needs an adjective meaning "not tight": *loose*. Line 3: after *some* a noun is needed, and the noun is *advice*. Line 4: the word means "if", so *whether*.
:::

:::qc
q: Find the error: "The sun was setting, and the plain flew over the hills."
options: ["plain should be plane", "setting should be sitting", "hills should be hill"]
answer: 0
right: Right. A plain is flat land. The thing that flew is a plane.
wrong: Ask what flew over the hills. It cannot be flat land. It is a plane (an aeroplane).
:::

## Heteronyms: the sound changes the meaning {lvl=3 id=s-hetero label="Beyond the board"}

A heteronym is spelt in one way but spoken in two, and the pronunciation tells you which meaning is meant. You can only tell them apart in a sentence.

| Word | Sound one | Sound two |
|---|---|---|
| **lead** | *leed*: to guide | *led*: the grey metal |
| **tear** | *tair*: to rip | *teer*: a drop from the eye |
| **wind** | *wind*: moving air | *wynd*: to turn, as a clock |
| **bow** | *bow* (as in *cow*): to bend forward | *boh*: a weapon, or a ribbon |
| **row** | *roh*: a line, or to use oars | *rau*: a quarrel |
| **close** | *klohz*: to shut | *klohs*: near |
| **live** | *liv*: to be alive | *lyve*: happening now, as in a live match |
| **minute** | *MIN-it*: sixty seconds | *my-NYOOT*: very small |

(The respellings above are rough guides, not dictionary symbols.)

### The stress shift

A large family of two-syllable words change **stress** to change their job: the noun is stressed on the first syllable and the verb on the second.

| Word | Noun (stress on first) | Verb (stress on second) |
|---|---|---|
| record | *a REC-ord* | *to re-CORD* |
| present | *a PRES-ent* (a gift) | *to pre-SENT* |
| object | *an OB-ject* | *to ob-JECT* |
| permit | *a PER-mit* | *to per-MIT* |
| increase | *an IN-crease* | *to in-CREASE* |
| desert | *the DES-ert* (dry land) | *to de-SERT* (abandon) |

Spelling is the same, so the stress is the only clue. A speaker who puts the stress wrongly (*I will re-CORD the record*) may be misunderstood.

### Accent changes the list

Whether two words are homophones depends on who is speaking. In many British accents the *r* in *court* is silent, so *caught* and *court* sound alike. In accents that pronounce that r (many Indian speakers do), they stay different. So a homophone list is a statement about one accent, and exams that use homophone pairs stick to the ones that hold for the standard pronunciation. ## Word play: puns, malapropisms and contronyms {lvl=3 id=s-play label="Beyond the board"}

Writers and comedians use these twins on purpose.

### Pun

A pun uses a double meaning to make a joke. *I used to be a banker, but I lost interest.* Here *interest* means both "curiosity" and "money paid on a loan". Headlines, advertisements and slogans use puns all the time. In a literature question on style, the right label is "pun" (or "play on words").

### Malapropism

A malapropism is a **wrong word that sounds like the right one**, used by mistake and with confidence: *He is a very eminent danger* (for *imminent*). The word comes from Mrs Malaprop, a character in Sheridan's play *The Rivals* (1775), who is remembered for her mix-ups. [Unverified: the date and the story behind the name are from memory.] The humour comes from the gap between the grand tone and the wrong word.

### Contronyms: a word at war with itself

A few words have two **opposite** meanings. The sentence decides.

| Word | One sense | Opposite sense |
|---|---|---|
| **sanction** | to approve | to punish |
| **cleave** | to split | to cling together |
| **dust** | to remove dust | to sprinkle powder, as in dusting a cake |
| **bolt** | to fasten | to run away |
| **fast** | moving quickly | fixed firmly, as in "stuck fast" |
| **oversight** | supervision | failure to notice |

These words are the strongest proof that meaning lives in the sentence, not in the word.

:::qc
q: "The council sanctioned the new park." What does sanctioned mean here?
options: ["approved", "punished", "closed", "bought"]
answer: 0
right: Right. A council can approve a new park, and punishing a park makes no sense, so sanctioned means approved here.
wrong: Sanction can mean approve or punish. A council with a new park is more likely to approve it than punish it, so the meaning is approved.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. Homophones sound alike but are spelt differently (night, knight); homonyms share spelling and sound (bank, bat); heteronyms share spelling but not sound (lead, tear); confusables only come close (affect, effect). {lvl=1}
2. For homophones, the ear cannot help: decide the meaning first, then pick the spelling. {lvl=1}
3. Use the substitution test: they're = they are, you're = you are, it's = it is. {lvl=1}
4. A homonym's meaning comes from the sentence around it. Two unrelated words that share a spelling are homonyms; one word with related senses is polysemy. {lvl=1}
5. Affect is usually a verb (to influence); effect is usually a noun (a result). {lvl=2}
6. Principal means head or main; principle means rule or belief. Accept means receive; except means apart from. {lvl=2}
7. British spelling: noun with c (advice, practice, licence), verb with s (advise, practise, license). {lvl=2}
8. Fewer goes with things you can count, less with quantity; between with two, among with more; farther for distance, further for more. {lvl=2}
9. The board tests these inside editing, gap-filling and words-in-context tasks, and the wrong word is a real word, so read for meaning. {lvl=2}
10. Heteronyms change meaning with sound or stress; puns, malapropisms and contronyms show that meaning lives in the sentence. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Choosing their, there or they're by ear: use the long-form test (they are) and the ownership test. {lvl=1}
- Writing "its" with an apostrophe to mean belonging to it: it's means it is or it has. {lvl=1}
- Trusting a spell checker to catch a wrong but real word: reread for meaning. {lvl=1}
- Mixing up to, too and two: two is the number, too means also or excessive, to is the rest. {lvl=1}
- Using affect as a noun or effect as a verb in the commonest sense: ask what part of speech fits. {lvl=2}
- Writing "advice" for the verb or "advise" for the noun: noun with c, verb with s. {lvl=2}
- Writing "principle" for the head of a school: the principal is the person. {lvl=2}
- Using "less" with countable things ("less students"): use fewer. {lvl=2}
- Mixing up lie and lay, rise and raise: check whether there is an object. {lvl=2}
- Taking a word's first meaning without checking the sentence: many words (bore, bear, light) have several. {lvl=2}
- Pronouncing a noun with verb stress (record, present, object): the stress marks the job. {lvl=3}
:::
