## Why one word beats a phrase {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Compare two ways of saying the same thing.

- *The judge looked at the letters, which could not be read at all.*
- *The judge looked at the **illegible** letters.*

The second sentence is shorter, cleaner and sounds more confident. That is the point of **one-word substitution**: a single word that carries the meaning of a whole phrase or clause.

It is useful in three places.

1. **Writing.** Essays, letters and reports read better when a long description becomes one exact word.
2. **Reading.** Newspapers and textbooks use these words everywhere, and a reader who knows them moves faster.
3. **Exams.** School tests and competitive exams often give you a phrase and ask for the word, or the other way round. [Unverified: the Class 10 board paper does not set a separate one-word question; the skill is tested through reading, vocabulary in context and school-level tests.]

There is one condition. The word must mean exactly the same as the phrase, and your reader must know it. A word that is too rare, or only half right, makes writing worse.

## How to crack a phrase {lvl=1 id=s-crack label="Foundation"}

You do not need to memorise every word in a list. You can **decode** a phrase in three short steps.

1. **Ask what kind of thing the phrase describes.** A person (who), a place (where), a quality (an adjective), or an action (a verb)? This tells you the shape of the answer.
2. **Find the key idea inside the phrase.** "One who *hates* mankind": hate. "A place where *birds* are kept": birds. "Cannot be *erased*": erase.
3. **Match the key idea to a root or ending you know.** Hate = mis-; birds = avi- (Latin for bird); cannot be erased = in- (not) + a root meaning wipe out, which gives indelible.

Every word is built from parts, and the parts repeat. The table below is a small toolkit of the most useful ones.

| Part | Meaning | Words built from it |
|---|---|---|
| phil-, -phile | love, lover of | philanthropist, bibliophile, anglophile |
| mis-, miso- | hate | misanthrope, misogynist |
| -phobia | fear | claustrophobia, acrophobia, xenophobia |
| -mania | craze, madness | kleptomania, pyromania |
| -cide | killing | homicide, suicide, regicide |
| -cracy | rule, government | democracy, autocracy |
| omni- | all | omniscient, omnipotent, omnivorous |
| poly- / mono- / bi- | many / one / two | polyglot, monogamy, bilingual |
| anthrop- | human being | philanthropist, misanthrope |
| auto- | self | autobiography, autocrat |
| in- (im-, il-, ir-) | not | invisible, impossible, illegible, irreparable |
| -able, -ible | can be done | edible, audible |

Most of these parts are Greek or Latin. This lesson gives the commonly taught meanings, not the original spellings. [Unverified: some meanings are simplified.]

:::note title="Do not guess from the sound" tone=warn
If the phrase says "one who collects stamps" and you remember only the first two letters of a word, check the meaning in your head before you choose. Philatelist and philanthropist both start with phil-, but one loves stamps and the other loves mankind. Always match the meaning, not the beginning.
:::

:::qc
q: Which part of "misanthrope" carries the idea of hate?
options: ["mis-", "-anthrope", "-ope", "an-"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Mis- means hate, and anthrop- means human being, so a misanthrope hates people.
wrong: Look at the table. Mis- means hate; anthrop- means human being. Together they give "hater of mankind".
:::

:::qc
q: "A place where bees are kept" is an ___.
options: ["apiary", "aviary", "aquarium", "arsenal"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Apiary is for bees. Aviary is for birds, aquarium for fish and arsenal for weapons.

## Words for what cannot be done {lvl=2 id=s-cannot label="Class 10"}

A very common pattern in board-style exercises is the phrase **"that cannot be ..."**. The answer is an adjective, built as **in- + root + -able/-ible**. The prefix changes spelling to match the next letter: *il-* before l, *im-* before b, m and p, *ir-* before r, and *in-* elsewhere.

| Phrase | Word |
|---|---|
| that cannot be seen | invisible |
| that cannot be heard | inaudible |
| that cannot be read | illegible |
| that cannot be erased | indelible |
| that cannot be avoided | inevitable |
| that cannot be corrected (of a person or habit) | incorrigible |
| that cannot be repaired or made good | irreparable |
| that cannot be used up | inexhaustible |
| that cannot be dissolved | insoluble |
| that cannot be done without | indispensable |
| that cannot be conquered | invincible |
| that cannot be captured (of a fortress) | impregnable |
| that cannot be believed | incredible |
| that cannot make mistakes | infallible |
| that cannot be counted | innumerable |
| that cannot be satisfied | insatiable |

The positive pairs are shorter and just as useful.

| Phrase | Word |
|---|---|
| that can be eaten | edible |
| that can be drunk | potable |
| that can be heard | audible |
| that can be done | feasible |
| that can be seen through | transparent |

:::note title="Inflammable is the trap" tone=warn
*Inflammable* means **easily set on fire**, the same as *flammable*. The in- here comes from the Latin word for "in", not from "not". That is why safety labels today prefer *flammable* and *non-flammable*, so no one is confused. If the phrase is "that cannot be set on fire", the word is **non-flammable** or **fireproof**, not inflammable.
:::

:::qc
q: Which word means "that cannot be avoided"?
options: ["inevitable", "indelible", "incorrigible", "invincible"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Inevitable is what you cannot avoid, such as the end of a long exam. Indelible cannot be erased, incorrigible cannot be corrected and invincible cannot be conquered.
wrong: All four begin with in-, so look at the root. Inevitable comes from "evitare", meaning to avoid, so it is what cannot be avoided.
:::

## Killing, ruling and fearing {lvl=2 id=s-cide label="Class 10"}

Three suffix families are heavily tested because one rule gives you a whole list.

**-cide: the killing of ...**

| Phrase | Word |
|---|---|
| killing of a human being | homicide |
| killing of oneself | suicide |
| killing of one's father | patricide |
| killing of one's mother | matricide |
| killing of one's brother | fratricide |
| killing of a king | regicide |
| killing of an infant | infanticide |
| killing of a whole race or people | genocide |
| killing of insects (also the substance) | insecticide |

**-cracy: rule or government by ...**

| Phrase | Word |
|---|---|
| rule by one person with unlimited power | autocracy |
| rule by the people | democracy |
| rule by a small group | oligarchy |
| rule by the upper class or nobles | aristocracy |
| rule by the wealthy | plutocracy |
| rule by officials | bureaucracy |
| rule by religious leaders | theocracy |
| government by a single king or queen | monarchy |
| absence of government | anarchy |

**-phobia and -mania: fears and manias**

| Phrase | Word |
|---|---|
| fear of closed spaces | claustrophobia |
| fear of heights | acrophobia |
| fear of water | hydrophobia |
| fear of open or public places | agoraphobia |
| fear of strangers or foreigners | xenophobia |
| uncontrollable urge to steal | kleptomania |
| uncontrollable urge to set things on fire | pyromania |

Notice the logic. *-cide* is always killing, so the first half tells you who or what dies: *patri-* (father), *matri-* (mother), *fratri-* (brother), *regi-* (king). The same logic applies to *-cracy*: the first half says who rules.

:::qc
q: "Rule by the people" is called ___.
options: ["democracy", "autocracy", "plutocracy", "anarchy"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Demo- comes from the Greek for people, so democracy is rule by the people. Autocracy is rule by one, plutocracy by the wealthy, and anarchy is the absence of rule.
wrong: The root decides it. Demo- means people, so rule by the people is democracy. Auto- means self or one, which gives autocracy, rule by one person.
:::

q: "A poem of mourning for someone dead" is an ___.

| a two hundredth anniversary | bicentenary |
| a period of one thousand years | millennium |
| happening every year | annual |
| happening every two years | biennial |
| happening twice a year | biannual |

:::qc
q: "That goes on without a pause" is ___.
options: ["incessant", "ephemeral", "obsolete", "perennial"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Incessant means without ceasing, such as incessant rain. Ephemeral is short-lived, obsolete is out of use and perennial lasts all year or forever.
wrong: The key idea is "without a pause". Incessant means never ceasing; the others are about short life, being out of date or lasting through the year.
:::

## How the question is set {lvl=2 id=s-format label="Class 10"}

The question appears in a few standard shapes. [Unverified: this is a description of common school and test formats, not an official CBSE specification.]

1. **Phrase to word.** "Choose the word that means 'one who loves books'." (Multiple choice or write-in.)
2. **Underlined phrase in a sentence.** "The audience listened to the speech *that was made without preparation*. Replace the phrase by one word." The answer is *extempore*, and you must fit it into the sentence.
3. **Match the columns.** Phrases in one column, words in the other.
4. **Fill in the blank from a box** of words.

A routine that works:

- Read the whole phrase, not only its first words.
- Name the type: person, place, adjective, verb.
- Spot the root or pattern (cannot be, killing of, rule by, place where).
- Check the grammar. Does the word need an article, a plural or a past form once it is in the sentence?
- Read the sentence again with the new word in place of the whole phrase.

:::worked title="Replacing an underlined phrase"
Replace the underlined phrase with one word: *The old fort, **which could not be captured**, stood on the hill for centuries.*

The phrase "cannot be captured" is a fortress, so the pattern is in- plus root plus -able/-ible, and fortresses are described as *impregnable*. The word is an adjective, so it replaces the phrase exactly: *The old fort, **impregnable**, stood on the hill for centuries.* (A better version drops the commas: *The impregnable old fort stood on the hill for centuries.*)
:::

:::qc
q: Replace the phrase: "She spoke without any preparation."
options: ["extempore", "verbatim", "anonymously", "posthumously"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Extempore means done without preparation, as in an extempore speech.
wrong: Verbatim means word for word, anonymous means without a known name and posthumous means after death. Without preparation is extempore.
:::

## Where the roots come from {lvl=3 id=s-roots label="Beyond the board"}

The words in this lesson are mostly borrowed from **Greek** and **Latin**, languages that scholars used for science, law and religion for centuries. When a new idea needed a name, writers combined old roots. That is why a word you have never met can often be solved from its parts.

| Root | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| xeno- | stranger, foreign | xenophobia |
| pyro- | fire | pyromania, pyrotechnics |
| klepto- | to steal | kleptomania |
| anglo- | English, of England | Anglophile |
| aud- | to hear | audible, audience, audio |
| leg- | to read | legible, illegible |
| morti-, mort- | death | mortuary, mortal |
| avi- | bird | aviary |
| api- | bee | apiary |
| omni- | all | omniscient, omnipotent |
| geno- | race, kind | genocide |
| homo- | human (in homicide) | homicide |

[Unverified: some of these meanings are simplified from the dictionary forms.]

Try the method on words not in the list. *Xenophobia*: xeno- (stranger) + -phobia (fear) gives "fear of strangers or foreigners". *Pyromaniac*: pyro- (fire) + mania gives "one with an uncontrollable urge to start fires". *Anglophile*: Anglo- + phile gives "a lover of England or English things". You can work out whole families this way, even when you have never seen the word.

:::qc
q: What is a "bibliophobe" most likely to dislike?
options: ["Books", "Birds", "Boats", "Bees"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Biblio- relates to books and -phobe means one who fears or dislikes, so the word suggests someone who dislikes or fears books. The word is rare, but the method works.
wrong: Biblio- is the book root, as in bibliophile and bibliography. A bibliophobe is, by the parts, a person who fears or dislikes books.
:::

## Near-miss words {lvl=3 id=s-near label="Beyond the board"}

The hardest items are not strange words but **neighbours**: pairs and triples whose meanings overlap. Learn the difference once.

| Group | Differences |
|---|---|
| epidemic, pandemic, endemic | An epidemic spreads quickly through a community or region; a pandemic spreads across countries or the world; an endemic disease is regularly found in one region or group |
| atheist, agnostic, sceptic | The atheist does not believe in God; the agnostic says God's existence cannot be known; the sceptic doubts claims until they are proved, and is not only about religion |
| cynic, pessimist, sceptic | The cynic distrusts people's motives; the pessimist expects the worst outcome; the sceptic wants proof |
| eulogy, elegy, epitaph, obituary | Praise; a poem of mourning; words on a tomb; a news notice of death |
| autobiography, biography, memoir | Life story by oneself; life story by another; a personal account of part of a life, usually by the one who lived it |
| amateur, novice, veteran | One who does it for pleasure; a beginner; one with long experience |
| biennial, biannual | Every two years; twice a year (some writers use biannual loosely for either, so check the context) |
| egoist, egotist | Often treated as the same in school lists; fine distinctions (egoist: puts self first; egotist: talks about self) are drawn by some dictionaries [Unverified] |
| monologue, soliloquy | A long speech by one speaker in front of others; a speech where a character speaks his thoughts aloud, alone |

In a test, the answer can be a word with exactly the same sound as a wrong option. Slow down, look back at the key words of the phrase, and rule out the neighbours one by one.

:::qc
q: A disease found regularly in one region or group of people is ___.
options: ["endemic", "epidemic", "pandemic", "chronic"]
answer: 0
right: Right. Endemic is the regular presence of a disease in a place. Epidemic is a sudden wide spread, and pandemic is a spread across the world.
wrong: The key is "regularly found in one place". That is endemic. The other two describe spreading widely, and chronic describes a long-lasting illness.
:::

## When one word is not better {lvl=3 id=s-style label="Beyond the board"}

A one-word substitute is a tool, not a goal. Experienced writers decide each time.

- **Audience first.** *Teetotaller* is fine in an email to friends. *Sesquipedalian* is not, because few readers know it. If your reader must open a dictionary, the long phrase was kinder.
- **Tone.** Some words carry feeling that the phrase does not. *Miser* sounds insulting, while "someone who spends very little" sounds neutral. Choose by what you mean.
- **Accuracy over show.** A wrong word is worse than a clear phrase. If you are unsure between *epidemic* and *pandemic*, describe the situation in plain words.
- **Euphemism.** Sometimes a gentler word is used on purpose: *passed away* for *died*. That is substitution too, but for kindness rather than brevity.
- **Rhythm.** In speech and creative writing, a short phrase may sound better than a heavy word. Read your sentence aloud.

Notice, too, that one-word substitutes keep company with other words: we say an *incessant* downpour and an *irreparable* loss, not an "irreparable rain". Which words naturally travel together is the next idea to study.

:::qc
q: You are writing a notice for Class 6 students. Which line is best?
options: ["Please do not bring anything that can be set on fire.", "Please do not bring any inflammable items.", "Please refrain from bringing combustible materials."]
answer: 0
right: Right. For young readers the plain phrase is clearest. A safe one-word choice would be "flammable", but the first line leaves no doubt.
wrong: The words in the other two lines are correct, but for Class 6 readers they are harder than the phrase. Choose the version your reader will understand at once.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A one-word substitute is one exact word that replaces a whole phrase with the same meaning. {lvl=1}
2. Decode in three steps: find the type (person, place, quality, action), find the key idea, match it to a root or ending. {lvl=1}
3. Many people words end in -ist, -phile or -crat: philatelist, bibliophile, aristocrat. {lvl=1}
4. Place words often end in -ary, -arium or -ory: aviary, apiary, aquarium, dormitory. {lvl=1}
5. "Cannot be" gives in-/im-/il-/ir- + root + -able/-ible: invisible, illegible, irreparable. {lvl=2}
6. -cide is killing (patricide, suicide, regicide) and -cracy is rule (democracy, autocracy, oligarchy). {lvl=2}
7. In a sentence, the substitute must fit the grammar: type, number and tense. {lvl=2}
8. Know the near-miss groups: epidemic, pandemic, endemic; eulogy, elegy, epitaph, obituary; atheist, agnostic, sceptic. {lvl=3}
9. Use a one-word substitute only when your reader knows it and it means exactly what you mean. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Choosing a word from the first letters or the sound alone: philatelist and philanthropist both start with phil-. {lvl=1}
- Mixing up aviary (birds), apiary (bees) and aquarium (fish). {lvl=1}
- Mixing up bachelor, widow and widower, or orphan with a child who has lost only one parent. {lvl=1}
- Reading "inflammable" as "cannot burn": it means easily set on fire. {lvl=2}
- Confusing eulogy (praise), elegy (a poem of mourning), epitaph (on a tomb) and obituary (a news notice). {lvl=2}
- Forgetting the grammar after substitution: a missing article (an aviary), wrong tense or number. {lvl=2}
- Using a rare word that the reader does not know. {lvl=3}
- Confusing near-miss words: epidemic and pandemic, biennial and biannual, atheist and agnostic. {lvl=3}
- Using a heavy word where a plain phrase would be kinder and clearer. {lvl=3}
:::
