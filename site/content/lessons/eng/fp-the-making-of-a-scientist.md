## What happens {lvl=1 id=s-plot label="Foundation"}

The chapter opens with a surprise. At twenty-two, Richard Ebright, once a scout of the year, excites the scientific world with a new idea about how cells work. He and his college room-mate explain it in an article for an important journal, the first time that journal has printed the work of college students. The author compares this to a fifteen-year-old playing in the big baseball leagues and scoring on his very first turn at bat.

Then we go back to the beginning. Ebright is an only child in Pennsylvania with no team to play with, so he collects: butterflies from kindergarten on, and also rocks, fossils and coins, and he watches the stars. His mother, widowed when he was in third grade, spends evenings with him, gives him equipment and finds him learning to do. By the second grade he has collected all twenty-five butterfly species of his town. Then his mother gives him a children's book, *The Travels of Monarch X*, about monarch butterflies that migrate to Central America. The book invites readers to help study migration, and soon he is tagging monarchs for Dr Frederick Urquhart of Toronto. To get enough butterflies he raises thousands in his basement. He later loses interest in the tagging because it is tedious and brings little feedback, with only two of his tagged butterflies ever recaptured.

In the seventh grade he enters a county science fair and loses, because his entry is a display of slides, not an experiment. He writes to Dr Urquhart, receives a pile of experiment ideas, and the next years are full of projects. In the eighth grade he looks for the cause of a virus that kills monarch caterpillars, tests whether a beetle carries it, finds nothing, and wins because he shows that he tried. The next year he tests whether birds avoid monarchs, supporting the idea that the viceroy butterfly copies the monarch's look for safety.

In his second year of high school he asks what the twelve golden spots on a monarch pupa are for. Everyone thought they were decoration; Dr Urquhart did not. Ebright builds a device and shows the spots make a hormone the butterfly needs. Over later years he grows wing cells in a laboratory, works in army and agriculture laboratories during summers, and identifies the hormone's chemical structure. While studying X-ray pictures at Harvard he gets the idea for a theory of how a cell reads the instructions in its DNA. He and his room-mate James Wong work all night drawing and building models, and write the paper. He graduates second in a class of 1,510.

Notice that the chapter is not about a lonely genius. Each helper widened what Ebright could do at a different stage. His mother set the foundation, Dr Urquhart supplied questions, and Mr Weiherer shaped his character.

## The making of a scientist {lvl=2 id=s-making label="Class 10"}

**Curiosity.** From the first, Ebright had a "driving curiosity". The question about the gold spots, a plain "what is this for?", led to a hormone and then to a theory about cells.

**Persistence and effort.** He kept a basement full of monarchs for years, did research through high school and college, and put in three or four hours of debate research at night as well.

**Real experiments, not displays.** The lost fair taught him that science tests ideas. The eighth-grade project shows that an honest attempt with no result still counts for something, since science reports what happens.

**Support.** A curious child needs materials, time and a path to experts. The story gives credit to the mother and the mentor.

**The will to win for the right reasons.** Mr Weiherer says Ebright did not seek prizes for their own sake; he wanted to do the best job he could. This is the third ingredient in the author's closing recipe.

**Breadth.** He is also a debater, canoeist, outdoors-person and photographer. The author shows that deep interest in science need not narrow a life.

## Reading a profile {lvl=3 id=s-craft label="Beyond the board"}

**Structure.** The piece begins at a peak moment (the journal article) and then goes back to the start. This is a hook: the reader learns where the story ends up and wants to know how. After that it follows time in order, from kindergarten to Harvard, so each event looks like the cause of the next. The last paragraphs step back and draw the lesson.

**Evidence from voices.** The author seldom says "he was determined" on his own. He lets Ebright, his mother and his teacher speak. A direct quotation is stronger evidence than a statement by the writer, and it adds warmth. Notice the modest, humorous voice of the scientist himself, as when he laughs that only two tagged butterflies were ever recaptured.

**Tone.** Admiring but plain. "And did he ever!" is playful, an informal turn that pulls the reader in. The author uses a sports comparison (the home run) to make an abstract achievement understandable to a general reader. The explanation in the footnote shows that the writer expects readers who may not know baseball, so the comparison must be glossed.

**Selection.** A profile cannot include everything. The author chooses events that support a single thesis, that scientists are made through curiosity, support and real work. Ask what has been left out: failures other than the two fairs, the cost of effort, and the question of whether every child has such a mother or mentor. That last point is a fair criticism: the chapter shows how much depends on opportunity.

**Understatement.** When he sees the photographs, he does not shout "Eureka!" The author notes this on purpose: real discovery is quiet, a belief that grows, followed by a night of drawing and building models.

:::qc
q: Why does the author begin with Ebright's achievement at twenty-two rather than with his childhood?

answer: 1
right: Yes. Opening with the surprising result creates a question, then the rest of the chapter answers it in order.
wrong: The childhood is in fact the main body of the chapter. The opening is placed first for a reason connected with the reader's interest.
:::

## Answering the usual questions {lvl=2 id=s-answers label="Class 10"}

A pattern for a short answer (40 to 50 words): **claim, evidence, comment.**

| Question type | Claim | Evidence | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| How did a book become a turning point? | *The Travels of Monarch X* turned a collector into a researcher | It told of monarch migration and invited readers to tag butterflies for Dr Urquhart | It showed him that collecting could become science |
| How did his mother help? | She supplied the means and the encouragement | Equipment, trips, evenings of learning tasks, a letter to Dr Urquhart | Her support made the rest possible |
| What did he learn from losing? | Science needs real experiments | The winners had tested ideas; his slides were a display | He resolved to do a real experiment next year |
| What are the qualities of a scientist? | Mind, curiosity and a healthy will to win | Gold-spots question, years of work, Mr Weiherer's comments | Quality needs support and effort |

For a long answer (120 words or more), add a short opening line that answers the question directly, then two or three linked paragraphs, then a closing line that returns to the question.

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- Do not retell the whole biography when a question asks for qualities. Pick two or three qualities and give one event for each. {lvl=1}
- Do not say Ebright became a scientist only because he was clever. The text gives equal weight to curiosity, effort and help from others. {lvl=2}
- Do not say he won the eighth-grade fair because his experiment succeeded; he found no real results and won because he showed he had tried. {lvl=2}
- Keep the order straight: butterflies, the book, tagging, the lost fair, the eighth- and ninth-grade projects, the gold spots, the Harvard theory. {lvl=2}
- Do not claim that the theory is proved. The chapter says that if it proves correct, it could be a big step; he was still testing it. {lvl=2}
- Do not copy long lines from the chapter into your answer. A short phrase with your own comment scores better. {lvl=3}
:::
