## Why we need nouns {lvl=1 id=s-why label="Foundation"}

Imagine talking without any names for things. You could point, but you could not talk about a thing that is far away, long gone, or only an idea. A **noun** is the word that names a person, place, thing or idea, so that it can be spoken about even when it is not in front of you.

Because nouns name the things a sentence is about, they usually act as the **subject** (who or what does something) or the **object** (what is done to), and the rest of the sentence is built around them.

- *Meera* (subject) *opened the window* (object).

Two problems follow, and almost all noun grammar is the answer to one of them:

1. **How many?** One window or two? This gives us singular and plural, and the question of what can be counted at all.
2. **Which one?** A window, or the window you already know about? This gives us the articles *a*, *an* and *the*.

Keep these two questions in mind. They explain nearly every rule in this lesson.

## The five kinds of noun {lvl=1 id=s-kinds label="Foundation"}

Nouns are sorted by what kind of thing they name.

| Kind | What it names | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Common** | any one of a class of things | boy, river, mango, school |
| **Proper** | one particular person, place or thing (begins with a capital letter) | Kabir, Chennai, the Ganga, Diwali |
| **Collective** | a group taken as a whole | team, class, flock, jury, family |
| **Material** | a substance something is made of | gold, wood, milk, cotton, water |
| **Abstract** | a quality, state, feeling or idea you cannot touch | honesty, speed, childhood, anger, freedom |

Why have so many kinds? Because they behave differently. Proper nouns usually take no article. Material and abstract nouns are usually uncountable. Collective nouns can take a singular or a plural verb. The names are a help for remembering the behaviour.

### Telling them apart

- Can you touch or see it? If not, it is probably **abstract** (*bravery*, *friendship*).
- Is it a group of things with one name for the whole? **Collective** (*a fleet of ships*).
- Is it stuff you can have more or less of, rather than one or two? **Material** (*sugar*, *iron*).
- A word can change kind with its use. *Iron* is a material noun in "Iron is a metal" but a common noun in "She bought an iron" (the appliance). *Time* is abstract, but *a good time* and *three times* count it.

:::qc
q: In "Her kindness won the hearts of the whole class", which noun is a collective noun?
options: ["kindness", "hearts", "class", "Her"]
answer: 2
right: Right. Class names a group of students taken as a whole. Kindness is abstract, hearts is a plural common noun, and her is a pronoun.
wrong: A collective noun names a group as one unit. Look for the word that stands for many people at once: class.
:::

## Countable and uncountable nouns {lvl=1 id=s-count label="Foundation"}

This split decides which small words can go in front of a noun, so it matters more than it first seems.

**Countable** nouns can be counted one by one: *one pen, two pens*. They have a singular and a plural form and can take *a/an*.

**Uncountable** (mass) nouns name something thought of as a lump or a stuff, not as separate units: *water, rice, advice, furniture, information, luggage, weather, news*. They have no plural form, do not take *a/an*, and take a singular verb.

| | Countable | Uncountable |
|---|---|---|
| With *a/an* | a chair | (not allowed) *a furniture |
| Plural | chairs | (none) *furnitures |
| *many / few / number of* | many chairs, a few chairs | |
| *much / little / amount of* | | much furniture, a little furniture |
| *some / a lot of / any* | some chairs | some furniture |

To count an uncountable noun, bring in a unit: **a piece of** advice, **an item of** furniture, **a glass of** water, **a loaf of** bread, **a bit of** news.

Common traps are nouns that are countable in other languages (and in some everyday speech) but uncountable in standard English: *advice, information, furniture, luggage, equipment, knowledge, homework, scenery, progress, work* (as in a job done). Write *The furniture is new*, never *furnitures*.

## Number: making the plural {lvl=1 id=s-number label="Foundation"}

The plural exists to answer "how many?". Most nouns add **-s**, and the rules below cover the rest.

| Ending of the noun | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| most nouns | add -s | boy, boys; book, books |
| -s, -x, -z, -ch, -sh | add -es (so it can be pronounced) | bus, buses; box, boxes; church, churches; dish, dishes |
| consonant + y | change y to i, add -es | city, cities; baby, babies |
| vowel + y | add -s | day, days; key, keys |
| -f / -fe (many) | change to -ves | knife, knives; leaf, leaves; wife, wives; wolf, wolves |
| -f / -ff (some) | just add -s | roof, roofs; chief, chiefs; cliff, cliffs |
| consonant + o (some) | add -es | hero, heroes; potato, potatoes; tomato, tomatoes |
| -o (other) | add -s | photo, photos; piano, pianos; radio, radios |

### Irregular plurals

These come from older forms of English or from Latin and Greek and simply have to be learnt.

| Singular | Plural |
|---|---|
| man, woman | men, women |
| child | children |
| tooth, foot, goose | teeth, feet, geese |
| mouse | mice |
| sheep, deer, species | sheep, deer, species (unchanged) |
| criterion, phenomenon | criteria, phenomena |
| analysis, crisis | analyses, crises |

*Fish* is usually unchanged (*three fish*); *fishes* is used when different kinds of fish are meant. *Dozen, hundred* and *thousand* stay singular after a number (*two dozen eggs*) but take -s when they mean "very many" (*dozens of people*).

### Compound nouns

In a compound noun the main word takes the plural: *brother-in-law, brothers-in-law; passer-by, passers-by; commander-in-chief, commanders-in-chief*. When the parts are equal, or the last word is the main one, the end takes it: *girlfriend, girlfriends*; *spoonful, spoonfuls*.

## Gender and the possessive {lvl=1 id=s-gender label="Foundation"}

### Gender

English nouns have **natural** gender (by sex), not grammatical gender as Hindi and French do. A table has no gender in English, whereas it does in Hindi.

- **Masculine**: boy, king, lion. **Feminine**: girl, queen, lioness.
- **Common** (either sex): student, child, teacher, doctor, friend.
- **Neuter** (no sex): book, river, stone.

Pairs like *actor/actress, host/hostess, prince/princess* exist, but many such words are now used for everyone (*actor*, *host*, *poet*), and the board answer key usually accepts the pair form, so learn it for the paper.

### The possessive (case)

The possessive shows who owns or is linked to what. Its pattern follows the same "how many" question.

| Owner | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| singular | add 's | the boy's bag, Ravi's book |
| plural ending in -s | add only an apostrophe | the boys' hostel, the teachers' room |
| irregular plural | add 's | children's park, women's college |
| two owners sharing | 's on the last only | Raj and Rani's house (one house) |
| two owners, separate | 's on each | Raj's and Rani's houses (two houses) |

The 's form is used mainly for people and animals. For things, use *of*: *the leg of the table*, not *the table's leg* (though *a day's work* and *today's news* are accepted). Another way is *a friend of mine*, *a book of Neha's*.

:::note title="Its or it's?" tone=warn
*It's* is short for *it is* or *it has*. The possessive *its* has no apostrophe: *The dog wagged its tail.* Similarly *theirs, hers, yours* never take an apostrophe.
:::

:::qc
q: Which phrase correctly means a park for children?
options: ["the childrens' park", "the children's park", "the childrens park", "the childs' park"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Children is an irregular plural that does not end in s, so it takes 's.
wrong: Children is already plural without an s, so add 's, exactly as for a singular noun.
:::

## Collective nouns and agreement {lvl=2 id=s-collective label="Class 10"}

### Collective nouns

A collective noun is singular in form but names a group. Some are tied to what is grouped, and board papers like to ask them: *a **fleet** of ships, a **flock** of sheep, a **bunch** of keys, a **pride** of lions, a **swarm** of bees, a **crew** of sailors, a **jury** of citizens, a **panel** of experts*.

### Does the verb take the singular or the plural?

This is the main source of errors. The rule rests on whether the group acts **as one body** or as **separate individuals**.

- As one unit, use a **singular** verb: *The team **is** playing well. The jury **has** reached its verdict. My family **is** in Pune.*
- As individuals doing different things, a **plural** verb is possible in standard British usage: *The team **are** arguing among themselves.* (Board answer keys prefer the singular in most items; treat the plural as a special case.)

### Singular-looking and plural-looking nouns

| Group | Examples | Verb |
|---|---|---|
| look plural, are singular | news, mathematics, physics, economics, measles, billiards | singular: *The news is good. Physics is my favourite subject.* |
| always plural | scissors, trousers, spectacles, jeans, clothes, police, cattle | plural: *My trousers are torn. The police have arrived.* |
| plural in form, one thing | a pair of scissors | singular with the pair: *A pair of scissors is on the table.* |
| uncountable | furniture, advice, information | singular: *The information is correct.* |

Also, a quantity or amount treated as a single sum or distance is singular: *Fifty rupees is too much for a pen. Ten kilometres is a long walk.*

### Subject-verb agreement with nouns joined by and, or

- **and** joins two subjects into a plural: *Ravi and Neha are friends.* But when the two names are one person or one idea, the verb is singular: *Bread and butter is my usual breakfast.*
- **or / nor** takes its verb from the **nearer** subject: *Neither the teacher nor the students were late.*
- *Each, every, either, neither* are singular: *Each of the boys has a bag. Every student was present.*

:::qc
q: "Mathematics ___ taught in the third period."
options: ["are", "is", "were", "have been"]
answer: 1
right: Right. Mathematics is a singular subject name even though it ends in -s.
wrong: Mathematics, like physics and economics, ends in s but is singular. Use is.
:::

## Articles: why English has a, an and the {lvl=1 id=s-articles-why label="Foundation"}

Go back to the second question, **which one?**. When you say "Close **the** door", the listener knows which door. When you say "I need **a** pen", any one pen will do, and the listener does not yet know which.

That is the whole logic of the articles:

- **the** (definite article): the listener can tell *which one*.
- **a / an** (indefinite article): *one*, any one, not yet identified.
- **no article** (the zero article): a plural, an uncountable noun, or a general idea.

The word the article stands for is old: *a/an* is a weakened form of *one*, and *the* a weakened form of *that*. So "a book" is 'one book, any' and "the book" is 'that book, the one we both know'.

### A or an: it depends on the sound

We use **an** before a **vowel sound** and **a** before a **consonant sound**, because *an* is easier to say before a vowel (compare *a apple*, hard to say, with *an apple*). The test is the sound, not the spelling.

| Starts with | Sound | Article | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| vowel letter, vowel sound | /a e i o u/-like | an | an apple, an egg, an umbrella, an onion |
| silent h | vowel sound | an | an hour, an honest man, an heir |
| vowel letter, sounds like "y" or "w" | consonant sound | a | a university, a European, a unit, a one-rupee coin, a useful book |
| consonant letter said as a letter name | vowel sound | an | an MBA, an SMS, an X-ray, an FIR |
| other consonant | consonant sound | a | a boy, a house, a ship |

## When to use the {lvl=1 id=s-the label="Foundation"}

Use **the**:

1. **For something already mentioned.** *I bought a shirt and a cap. The shirt is blue.*
2. **For something unique, so there is only one.** *the sun, the moon, the earth, the sky, the equator*
3. **When the noun is made specific by the words after it.** *The book on the table is mine.*
4. **With the superlative and with ordinals.** *the tallest boy, the best answer, the first day, the last page*
5. **With a few kinds of proper noun.**
   - rivers, seas, oceans: *the Ganga, the Arabian Sea, the Pacific*
   - mountain ranges and island groups: *the Himalayas, the Andamans*
   - countries whose names include a plural or a political word: *the United States, the Netherlands*
   - well-known buildings and holy books: *the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort, the Gita, the Ramayana*
   - newspapers: *The Hindu*
6. **For a class as a whole** (see the next section) and with **musical instruments**: *She plays the guitar.*
7. **With an adjective, to mean a group of people**: *the poor, the rich, the blind, the young.*

:::qc
q: "My uncle lives near ___ Arabian Sea."
options: ["a", "an", "the", "no article"]
answer: 2
right: Right. Names of seas, oceans and rivers take the.
wrong: Seas, oceans, rivers and mountain ranges take the: the Arabian Sea.
:::

## When to use a or an {lvl=1 id=s-a label="Foundation"}

Use **a / an**:

1. **With a singular countable noun, when it is new or one of many.** *A boy is waiting outside.* (the first mention)
2. **To say "one" in a loose way**, as in a price or rate: *forty kilometres **an** hour, twice **a** day, ten rupees **a** dozen*.
3. **To say what someone or something is**: *My mother is **a** doctor. This is **an** interesting book.*
4. **After the words such, what, quite, many, half:** *such **a** pity, what **a** surprise, quite **a** few, many **a** student, half **an** hour.* In *such a pity* and *what a surprise* the article comes after the word, not before: not *a such pity*.

A and an are used only with **singular countable** nouns. Never use them with plurals or uncountable nouns (*a water, an information* are wrong).

## No article {lvl=2 id=s-zero label="Class 10"}

Leaving the article out (the **zero article**) is a rule too. Use none:

1. **With plural and uncountable nouns used in a general sense.** *Dogs are loyal. Milk is good for you. Honesty is the best policy.*
2. **With names of meals, languages, subjects and games.** *We had lunch at one. She speaks Tamil. He studies physics. They play cricket.*
3. **With most proper nouns:** persons, cities, most countries, continents, single mountains: *Asha, Mumbai, India, Asia, Mount Everest.* Before a title and a name too: *Prince Charles, Dr Rao.*
4. **With institutions used for their purpose:** *go to school* (to study), *go to bed* (to sleep), *go to hospital* (as a patient), *at home, in class, by bus, by train, on foot.* Compare: *The school is near the station* (the building).
5. **After a possessive or demonstrative:** *my book, this city, Ravi's bag.* An article and a possessive do not go together: not *the my book*.
6. **With times of day and seasons in set phrases:** *at night, at noon, at dawn* (but *in the morning, in the evening*; *in summer* or *in the summer*).

:::note title="Same noun, different job" tone=tip
The article follows the **use**, not the word. *She went to school* (to study; no article) but *She went to the school to meet the principal* (the building, a visit). *Dinner is ready* but *The dinner was excellent* (a particular dinner, made specific).
:::

## Articles in the board's questions {lvl=2 id=s-board label="Class 10"}

A gap-filling passage often hides articles among other grammar. Use this routine at each gap:

1. Is the noun **singular countable**? If not (plural or uncountable), the answer is usually no article, or *the* if it is specific.
2. **First mention or already known?** First mention: *a/an*. Already known, unique, or made specific: *the*.
3. If you chose *a/an*, check the **sound** of the next word, which may be an adjective, not the noun (*an old man, a useful tool, an honest answer*).
4. Check for a **superlative or ordinal** (*the*), and a **possessive** (no article).

Common error-correction items:

- *She gave me many informations.* → *a lot of information* (uncountable).
- *He is an university student.* → *a university student* (sound).
- *I want to learn the French.* → *French* (a language).
- *The Mount Everest is the highest.* → *Mount Everest is the highest* (a single mountain, no the).
- *I like the dogs.* → *I like dogs* (in general).
- *She wore an one-piece dress.* → *a one-piece dress* (it starts with a *w* sound).

:::worked title="A seven-gap passage in a minute"
Fill the gaps: *Yesterday I visited (1) zoo near my house. (2) zoo has (3) elephant that is over fifty years old. We had (4) lunch at (5) canteen next to (6) main gate. It was (7) best day of my holidays.*

(1) **a**, a first mention of one zoo. (2) **The**, same zoo, now known. (3) **an**, a new singular countable noun, vowel sound. (4) **no article**, a meal. (5) **the**, because "next to the main gate" points to one particular canteen; with no such detail, *a* would be possible, so read the context. (6) **the**, "main gate" is a unique gate of the zoo. (7) **the**, superlative: *the best*.
:::

:::qc

## Beyond the board: finer shades {lvl=3 id=s-beyond label="Beyond the board"}

### Three ways to talk about a whole class

Each of these is correct, but they differ in tone.

- *The tiger is a wild animal.* (the + singular: formal, scientific, a type)
- *A tiger is a wild animal.* (a + singular: any one tiger, a definition)
- *Tigers are wild animals.* (plural: the most natural and the most common)

*Man is mortal* (a singular noun with no article for the whole species) is a classical form; modern writers choose *humans are mortal*.

### Proper nouns turned common

A proper name takes an article when it stands for something else. *She is **a** Shakespeare of our school* (a great writer); *the Tendulkars* (the family); *a Ford* (a car of that make); *the Delhi I remember* (the city of one's memory, made specific).

### Abstract nouns can become countable

When an abstract noun names a particular case or kind, it can take *a* and be plural. *Difficulty* in general is uncountable, but *a difficulty*, *many difficulties*; *beauty* is general, but *a beauty* is a beautiful person or thing; *kindness* versus *a kindness*. The article marks the move from the idea to an instance.

### The with comparatives, and the with by

*The more you read, the better you write* uses *the* before two comparatives to show that one change goes with the other. It is a different *the* from the one before nouns, historically an old form meaning "by that much".

### Small words that rearrange the article

- *Many a student has failed* (singular verb, an old, formal pattern); *a number of students are* (a plural verb) but *the number of students is* (a singular verb): the article decides what the head noun is.
- A number-noun adjunct stays singular: *a ten-year-old boy, a five-rupee note, a two-hour film* (not *ten-years-old boy*). With the same words used differently: *The boy is ten years old.*
- *The same, the only, the very, the next* take *the*.

### Possessive on a phrase

The 's can follow a whole phrase: *the Queen of England's crown*, *my sister-in-law's car*. For two owners who share, put it on the last: *Raj and Rani's house*. A double possessive, *a friend of my father's*, is correct and often clearer than *my father's friend*.

:::qc
q: Which is the natural way to say that a particular family lives next door?

answer: 1
right: Right. The + a plural surname refers to the whole family: the Sharmas.
wrong: To refer to a whole family, use the and add -s to the surname: the Sharmas, with a plural verb.
:::

## Key ideas to remember {lvl=1 id=s-keys}

:::keys
1. A noun names a person, place, thing or idea. Two questions drive its grammar: how many (number) and which one (articles). {lvl=1}
2. The five kinds are common, proper, collective, material and abstract; the same word can change kind with its use (iron, time). {lvl=1}
3. Countable nouns take a/an and have plurals; uncountable nouns (advice, furniture, information, luggage, news) take neither and use much, little, some or a unit such as a piece of. {lvl=1}
4. Plural rules: -s; -es after s, x, z, ch, sh; consonant + y becomes -ies; many -f/-fe become -ves; irregular forms (children, teeth, mice, criteria) are learnt. {lvl=1}
5. The possessive: boy's (one), boys' (many), children's (irregular plural); for things use of. Its (own) has no apostrophe. {lvl=1}
6. A collective noun takes a singular verb when the group acts as one; news, mathematics and physics are singular; scissors and trousers are plural. {lvl=2}
7. The means 'the listener knows which one': already mentioned, unique, made specific, a superlative or ordinal, or a river, sea or range. {lvl=1}
8. A/an means 'one, not yet identified'; use an before a vowel sound (an hour, an MBA) and a before a consonant sound (a university, a European). {lvl=1}
9. No article for plural or uncountable nouns in general, meals, languages, subjects, games, most proper nouns, and institutions used for their purpose (go to school). {lvl=2}
10. Beyond the board: three ways to speak of a class, proper nouns that become common, abstract nouns that become countable (a difficulty), the + plural surname. {lvl=3}
:::

## Toolkit and traps {lvl=1 id=s-traps}

:::traps
- "Many advices", "furnitures", "informations", "luggages": these are uncountable and have no plural. {lvl=1}
- "A university" versus "an university": decide by sound, not by the first letter. {lvl=1}
- Wrong plural spelling: "boxs" should be "boxes" and "babys" should be "babies". {lvl=1}
- Wrong apostrophe: "childrens' park" should be "children's park"; "boy's hostel" for many boys should be "boys' hostel"; and "it's tail" should be "its tail". {lvl=1}
- Using "the" before a general plural or uncountable noun: "The honesty is the best policy" should be "Honesty is the best policy". {lvl=2}
- Using an article before a language, subject, meal or a single mountain: "the French", "the Mount Everest" should be "French", "Mount Everest". {lvl=2}
- Treating news, mathematics or physics as plural: "The news are good" should be "The news is good". {lvl=2}
- Leaving out the before a river, sea, ocean or range: write "the Ganga", "the Arabian Sea", "the Himalayas". {lvl=2}
- "Such a" order reversed ("a such girl"): the article follows such and what. {lvl=2}
- Treating a collective noun as a plural by habit: "The jury have reached their verdict" is a British option; the board key usually expects "The jury has reached its verdict". {lvl=2}
- Plural 'a ten-years-old boy': in a compound adjective the number noun stays singular, "a ten-year-old boy". {lvl=3}
